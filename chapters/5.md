ROWAN

There were seven chairs at the council table of Ratherlon, and on the morning the raven came in from Eastgirt only five of them held a man.

Rowan counted them as he always counted them, from the king's end down. The Maester sat nearest the window, because his eyes were poor and the light was kind to vellum. Lord Orston sat beside him in the plain brown wool that made a man look like part of the paneling. Ser Corren, Lord Commander of the Kingsguard, filled his chair as a boulder fills a gateway. Ser Boremund, the Master-at-Arms, had pushed his own a hand's breadth back from the table to make room for his knees. Lord Elmrick, the Master of Laws, sat at the foot with his stout hands folded over his belly and the broken veins on his nose gone a little darker than usual, which meant he had taken wine with his breakfast.

Two chairs stood empty at the king's right hand. The nearer was the Hand's. The farther was the Master of Coin's. A steward laid a clean cushion on each of them every morning, as though a man might arrive in the night and wish to be comfortable.

Rowan's own seat was a clerk's stool at a small table below the foot, with the king's seal in its leather bag, the minute roll, and a stack of fresh vellum. He had sat on that stool for a year. No one had ever told him to, and no one had ever told him to rise.

The king came in last, wind-reddened from the roosts, with his green cloak thrown back and a scrap of dried fruit still caught in the stubble of his beard. Every man at the table stood. Rodrik waved them down before they were fairly up and took his chair at the head, and the Maester, with the grave slowness of the very old, laid a curl of onion-skin parchment in front of him.

"It came an hour past dawn," said Osmund. "Brown wax, Eastgirt's loft. The hand is the princess's."

"It is very small," said Rodrik.

"It is very close-written, Your Grace. She has used a crow-quill and a great deal of patience."

Rodrik turned the scrap in his big fingers and frowned at it. The lines ran so tight that they looked like the stitching on a glove. "I cannot read this without a glass, and I have never in my life been able to read anything Elyra wrote when she was in a hurry." He pushed it across the polished oak toward the stool at the foot. "Read it out, Rowan. You read better than I do when I am angry, and I expect to be."

Rowan rose, took the parchment, and carried it to the window where the light was good. He read it through once silently, as he read everything before he gave it voice, and then he cleared his throat and began.

"*Brother. I write from the outer watch, the night of the day I came. Lark and I reached the holdfast at noon and the men in the yard nearly shot at her. Duke Simon met me with a face like a closed gate and asked where the king was. I told him the king was keeping the realm so that there should be a realm to defend, and he did not like it, but he heard it.*"

"He would not have liked it from me either," Rodrik said.

"*Alton Kelward, the duke's brother, found the dead man and the cloth. The cloth is dyed purple, the color of Rosegate, and it has been scented with rose soap to make the lie plainer. A Crownfold perfumer's soap, not a Vine Shore one. I will give you my reasons when I see you. This afternoon Alton and I rode into the gullies north of the watch and were ambushed by twelve men in bone armor with their faces smeared in ash. Lark broke them. Five we took alive.*"

Ser Boremund grunted approval. Rowan read on, in the even voice he used for the sums of the realm, and felt the room settle around the words.

"*They confessed to Alton before witnesses and again to me. They were paid in silver. The man who paid them was met on the north road. He spoke like a Crownfold man and wore high boots. He gave them the purple cloth and told them to pin it to a dead watchman, and said that when Eastgirt marched south the borders would stand empty for a month. He did not give a name. I do not think they knew it.*"

The Maester's quill, which had been scratching, stopped.

"*Brother, this is no raid. Someone in the Crownfold has bought a dead man and a lie, and the price was silver. Simon holds his levies by a thread. Harrun will be with us in ten days and will find a duke who wishes to burn a rose garden. I will keep the thread from snapping as long as I can. Send word what you want done with the prisoners. Your sister, Elyra.*"

He lowered the parchment. Nobody spoke for the space of a long breath. Beyond the narrow windows a Nyxen called from the roosts, one long note falling away to nothing, and Rowan saw the king's eyes go toward the sound and come back.

"High boots," said Prince Edwyn, from the window seat, where he had been lounging so quietly that Rowan had forgotten him. He held no seat on the council and had attended every meeting since the coronation. When Rodrik had asked him once what he supposed he was doing, Edwyn had said that someone must provide the audience. "I congratulate our sister. She has narrowed the field to every man in the Crownfold with a cobbler and a grievance. Half the stewards between here and the Fold wear boots to the thigh, and the other half would, if they could afford them. I own three pairs myself."

"You own three pairs and have paid for none of them," Rowan said.

"I am wounded, and I will recover. Brother," Edwyn said, turning his cup toward the king, "I say only that the girl who wrote that has a gift for finding things, and a gift for finding them in a way that does not help."

"It helps," said Lord Orston. He had not moved in his chair, and he spoke so softly that Elmrick leaned forward to hear. "It tells us the man is rich, because silver in that quantity is not carried by a hedge-knight. It tells us he is Crownfold-born. It tells us he is careful, because he left no name, and a careful man is a man with something to lose. I have already set a boy to count the cobblers who have sold riding boots of that cut since midsummer. It will take a month."

"A month," Rodrik said. "And between now and then Simon Kelward is a barn full of straw with a torch lying beside it."

"The torch was dropped by a man who knows how dry the straw is," said Orston. "That is the second thing the letter tells us."

Lord Elmrick cleared his throat. "Your Grace, if I might offer a reading. The Vine Shore merchants have a number of rivals in the Crownfold, and a number of old quarrels with the river traders. A silvered man in high boots, a scrap of Rosegate purple. It might well be a Foldhall merchant's trick to ruin Duke Roland's trade for a season."

"It might," said Rodrik. "It might also be a Merelands lord with a grudge, or an Ashen king who has learned to buy Crownfold accents. I do not wish to name a man this morning, my lord, I wish to find one." He drew a breath. "Ser Boremund. What do you need to put five hundred swords on the kingsroad?"

Boremund considered the question with the unhurried air of a man who had been asked it many times. "Time, Your Grace, and coin. The men I can have by the new moon. The wagons are harder. A column that walks a thousand miles eats the country it walks through, and Eastgirt has none to spare. Everything we need must go with us."

"Rowan."

The king did not look round. He spoke the name as he might have spoken to a hound that was already at his heel, and Rowan, who had spent a year training himself not to feel that, felt it all the same. He had done the sum on the stair coming up. He had done it, in fact, three days ago, on the morning that Orston's rumors from the Eastverge first reached the solar, and had left the vellum in his sleeve against the day it should be wanted.

"Five hundred men for sixty days is eleven hundred marks, Your Grace, with bread, salt beef, shoes, and the farriers' charges, and allowing for two horses in ten lost on the road." He laid the figures out as flatly as he could. "For four hundred and fifty it is nine hundred and ninety. Under a thousand."

"Why does the thousand matter?" said Rodrik.

Rowan glanced down the table. Lord Elmrick had already begun to smile.

"Because it does, Your Grace," said the Master of Laws gently, "by the statute of Aldren VII, which was passed after a Selborn lord drew down the treasury in a single winter to build himself a stair. No sum above a thousand marks may leave the royal coffers without the king's seal and the countersign of the Master of Coin, set down together. It is a good law. It has saved us from several fools."

"There is no Master of Coin."

"No, Your Grace."

"Prince Rowan keeps the seal. He has kept it since my father's death."

"He keeps the seal, Your Grace, and does the work. But the law names an office, and the office is empty. If the king signs alone for eleven hundred marks, the lords of the Crownfold may say, with every right, that he has set the statute aside, and that a king who sets aside one statute may set aside another." Elmrick spread his plump hands. "I do not say it myself. I am only reminding the council what will be said."

Rodrik looked at the two empty chairs, one after the other. His jaw moved under the short dark beard.

"Then I will fill the seat," he said.

Rowan stood very still at the window with the parchment in his fingers. He had a rule, which he had made at nine years old and kept ever since, never to hope for a thing in a room where anyone might see his face. He kept it now. He found, to his mild surprise, that it took the whole of his attention.

The door opened.

"Your Grace," said the steward in the doorway, with the stiff careful vowels of a man delivering bad news in good order. "The lords of the petition are come. You gave them the second hour of the morning."

"So I did," said the king, after a moment. He sat back in his chair. "Show them in."

* * *

They came in a body, six lords in fine cloth with the smell of the guest-hall still on them, and Rowan watched them cross the floor and arrange themselves along the table's side as a cook arranges what she has to serve. Lord Fenlow came first in green velvet, with a smile that went no farther than his mouth. Baron Callenby came behind him, a heavy man with a thin gold chain across his chest and the damp eyes of someone who had not slept. Lord Hollisyn followed, plump and bright, with rings on all his fingers. Lord Merecroft, small and anxious, kept close to Callenby's elbow. Two more were of the Vine Shore, in the deep violet that Rosegate's bannermen wore, and neither would meet the king's eye.

Rodrik heard them standing. He did it with perfect civility, and Rowan, who had watched him do it a hundred times, thought that no one else in the room would have guessed the effort it cost him.

The petition was Fenlow's, though he let Hollisyn read it. It was well made. It spoke with sorrow of the dead on the eastern border and with admiration of the king's wisdom. It observed that the crown's silver was going east in great quantities, and that the lords of the Crownfold, who had paid it, took leave to doubt that their purses were being watched by a man who could not be called to account. It concluded, with every courtesy, that the seat of Master of Coin had stood empty too long, that the treasury was the realm's and belonged to no single prince, and that the lords humbly begged the king to fill it, and to fill it from among the men who bore the cost.

"We do not speak against Prince Rowan," said Fenlow warmly, when Hollisyn had done. "There is no man in the realm whose labor we respect more. But a prince is not a Master, Your Grace, and a labor is not an office. It would ease many minds to see the chair filled with a lord who answers to the council."

"And you have a lord in mind," said Rodrik.

"Lord Hollisyn has been a faithful steward of his own lands for thirty years."

"Lord Merecroft," said Callenby, quite suddenly, with his chain clinking, "has the best head for a column of figures in the western Crownfold. I have said so these ten years."

Merecroft looked as though he wished to sink into the floor. Hollisyn, to Rowan's interest, did not look at Callenby at all. A silence fell across the table, in which the two nominees stood a little apart from each other, each regarding the other's patron with a gentle, puzzled courtesy.

"They are both worthy men," said Rodrik.

"They are," said Fenlow.

"They cannot both have the seat."

"No, Your Grace. That is the difficulty." Fenlow turned his palm up with an air of generous regret. "The council has weighed their merits for some months now, and each time the council has been divided. I fear it will be divided again. It is a hard thing, a divided council. I would not wish it on any king."

"I will take the advice of the council," Rodrik said.

He did. He asked each man at the table in turn. The Maester said that a Master of Coin should be a man who could read a tally and that he believed both lords could, at a pinch. Orston said that he would defer to the king's judgment. Ser Corren said that he was a soldier and kept to his trade. Ser Boremund said that he had never in his life met a man he trusted with a purse and that the choice between two lords did not move him. Lord Elmrick, who had been looking longingly at the pitcher on the sideboard, said that the law was silent on the question of whom, and that a Master of Laws could advise only on the how.

It was exactly as it had been the eight times before, and Rowan could have recited it in his sleep. He sat at his small table below the foot and wrote it down in the minute roll in a hand so neat it might have been engraved: *The council divided. No decision.*

"My lords," said Rodrik at last. He had risen, and he spoke without raising his voice. "I have heard you, and I thank you. The matter of the seat is a grave one, and I do not mean to leave it to chance. I mean to name a Master of Coin, and I will do it when I am certain that my choice will serve the realm and a faction will gain nothing by it. That day is later than this morning. You have my word that it will be soon." He turned his head slightly. "Meanwhile Ser Boremund will muster four hundred and fifty swords by the new moon, and will march when I give the word. They will be paid out of what the king's seal may spend alone."

Fenlow bowed. Callenby bowed. Hollisyn bowed, and his rings flashed. Rowan watched the six of them file out in their rich cloth, and saw that Fenlow was smiling, and that Lord Merecroft was weeping a little, quite silently, into his sleeve.

When the door had closed, Rodrik sat down heavily. He looked at the two empty cushions, and then, for no more than the space of a breath, at the clerk's stool at the foot of the table.

Rowan looked at the roll. He felt the look land on him, as he could always feel it, as a man feels a hand on his shoulder in a crowd, and he did not raise his head. The king's reasons were not hard to guess. To name a Brennan prince as Master of Coin in the middle of a quarrel over the seat, with the Vine Shore lords whispering that grain should stay at home, would have been to throw a torch into the thatch. Rowan would have given the same counsel himself, if he had been asked.

He had not been asked. That was the part that sat in him like a swallowed stone.

"Fifty swords short," said Rodrik, to nobody, "because of a law about stairs."

"They can be found, Your Grace," said Ser Boremund. "I know a few men of the Merelands who ride for bread."

"Find them." The king rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "Rowan, write to my sister. Tell her that Harrun and his twenty are hers, and that four hundred and fifty more will follow when the road allows. Tell her the prisoners are to be kept alive and kept apart. Tell her I want the north road watched for a Crownfold man in high boots, and that I want her to take care of herself."

"All of that, Your Grace?"

"In that order." The king managed a tired smile. "Put the last part in plain words. She will skip it otherwise."

* * *

The river stairs lay at the bottom of the lower town, where the streets gave up their slope and fell in a hundred and forty broad steps to the Fold. Rowan went down them in the thin afternoon light with Jaspern's boy Tobb at his heels, a sharp-faced child of eleven who carried the satchel with the air of a man bearing the crown jewels.

He liked the stairs. He seldom admitted it, but he liked them, because everything there was weighed. At every landing a moneychanger sat under a striped awning with a set of brass scales and a coin-box chained to his stool, and the whole long flight rang with the small dry chink of silver being counted. Boatmen quarrelled with factors. Barrels went by on rollers. The smell of the river came up cold and green from below, and over it lay the warmer, sharper smell of tar, tallow, and wet rope. In forty paces a man could hear the price of every good in the realm.

The house of Barrow and Wend stood on the fifth landing, a narrow building of black stone with an iron-bound door and a pair of brass scales hung over the lintel. Its master met him at the threshold. He was a long, stooped man called Barrow, with a ring of keys at his belt as heavy as a mace and a manner so smooth that it might have been poured.

"Prince Rowan." He bowed to a depth just short of a prince's due, which was an old moneychanger's art. "You honor the stairs. Will you take wine? We have a very fair Rosegate red."

"Thank you, no, Master Barrow. I have come on a small matter of the summer's lending." Rowan drew the folded vellum from his sleeve and laid it on the counter. "I wish to see the enrolments for the quarter, if you have them. Loans above a hundred marks, lent in silver, to any man of the Crownfold. I need only the names."

"Ah." Barrow's smile did not change. "I wish I could oblige you, Prince. I would do it gladly. But the enrolments are not yet in the roll."

"They are written down before the crown's clerk. It is the law."

"They are written on loose sheets, my lord, at the counter, and the clerk of the tallies sees them, as he should. But a loose sheet belongs to the house until it is fair-copied into the quarter's roll, and the fair copy goes to Foldhall on quarter-day. My clerks are behind this year. The summer was very busy." The master moneychanger inclined his head with gentle regret. "I fear that the roll you seek will come to the crown's clerk at midwinter, with the rest. By Aldren VII's charter, the crown may demand the loose sheets sooner, but only by the king's warrant, with the Master of Coin's countersign set to it. Or in a time of declared war, by the king's hand alone."

"There is no Master of Coin."

"A grief to us all, Prince."

It was beautifully done. Rowan looked at the long face and the keys and the faint, polite, unhurried smile, and thought that if the man had been lying, he was the best liar on the stairs, and that if he was telling the exact truth he was nearly as good. He found that he could not tell which, and that it irritated him far more than a plain refusal would have done.

"The realm is not at war, Master Barrow," he said.

"Not yet, we all pray." Barrow bowed again. "I will tell my clerks to make haste. They are good lads. I would not want you to think the house had anything to hide."

"I would not think it," said Rowan, and gathered up his vellum.

At the door a side-passage opened onto the landing, and a man stepped out of it with his hat pulled low and a long cloak over his arm. He did not look at Rowan. He crossed the stones in four strides and went down toward the quays, and Rowan, who had been about to descend himself, found that he had stopped.

It was the boots. They were new, and they were very good. They came to the thigh in oiled black leather, with a silver-chased buckle at each knee, and the soles were so clean that the man might have walked down from the keep without touching the street. No clerk wore such boots. No factor wore them either, unless his lord was generous, or he himself was rich.

"Why do you look at his feet, Prince?" said Tobb, in a whisper.

"Because a man may hide his face and his purse, Tobb, but he must go to a cobbler," Rowan said. "And a cobbler keeps tallies."

The man reached the foot of the steps. A groom held a bay horse there, and the stranger swung into the saddle with the careless ease of long practice and was gone along the quay before Rowan could see his face.

He stood for a long moment on the landing.

It was nothing. He told himself so with some firmness. Half the men in the Crownfold wore high boots, as Edwyn had said. A moneychanger's side door was a door through which a hundred men passed in a day. And an unnamed man in good leather leaving a house of lending was no more to be wondered at than a baker's boy leaving a bakery.

But it was an odd thing, he thought, to have been told that a rich man in high boots was buying a dead man's loyalty in silver, and in the same afternoon to have been refused the sheets that would show who was borrowing silver. The coincidence was probably honest. Most coincidences were. He had spent his life learning to price them, and on the whole he had found that they cost less than people supposed.

He turned his steps up the long flight, and Tobb followed, and the chink of the moneychangers' scales went on behind them, bright and steady and indifferent, like a very small rain.

He could go back to the council with it. That was the plain course. He could stand before Rodrik tomorrow and say, *A house on the stairs will not show its sheets, and a man in good boots left it as I arrived,* and the king would hear him out and ask what proof there was. There was none. Rowan would be left with a story, and the lords of the petition would be left with a prince who had gone down to the river to pick a quarrel with honest lenders on the strength of a boot.

Better to wait. Four months was not so long. Midwinter would bring the roll, and the roll would give him names, written in a clerk's hand under the crown's own seal, and a name was a thing a king could act on. He could be patient. It was the one fault no one had ever charged him with.

He told himself so, climbing the stairs. He told himself so a second time at the sixtieth step, a little less firmly, and a third at the hundredth.

* * *

The candle in the counting-room was half burned by the time he sat down to fair-copy the minutes, and the rest of Foldhall had gone to its supper and its bed. He had not eaten. He seldom did on the days when the council sat.

He wrote the day's business in the order it had been taken: the raven from Eastgirt, the lords' petition, the muster of four hundred and fifty. He wrote the king's promise to name a Master of Coin and set it down as exactly as it had been said, in the king's own words. Then he came to the line he had written eight times before, and wrote it a ninth. *The council divided. No decision.*

He sat looking at it with the pen lifted.

Something snagged. He could not say what. It was the faint, irritating catch of a column that adds correctly and still feels wrong, a sensation he had learned over many years to respect. He put down the pen, went to the shelf behind him, and drew out the rolls of the earlier meetings. There were eight of them, tied in ribbon and dated in his own hand across the span of the year.

He laid them side by side on the counting-cloth and read the entries for the empty seat, one beneath another.

Lord Fenlow had named Lord Hollisyn on the first occasion. Baron Callenby had named Lord Merecroft. The council had divided, and the king had deferred. He turned to the second roll, and found Hollisyn and Merecroft again. On the third, the same. On the fourth, the fifth, the sixth, the seventh. He read them all, and then read them over again from the beginning, in case his tired eyes had played him false.

Hollisyn and Merecroft. Always the same two, always put forward by the same two sponsors, always set against each other, and always, in the end, with the council split and the king unwilling to overrule it.

In a year of meetings, neither Fenlow nor Callenby had once put forward another man.

Rowan sat back. He had known Lord Hollisyn since boyhood. He was a pleasant, pompous man who could not have kept a stable's accounts, let alone a kingdom's. Merecroft was kind and honest and so frightened of figures that he left the room whenever a sum went above a hundred. No council in its right mind would have chosen either one. Each had been put up against the other, month after month, by two lords who seemed to have no wish at all to win.

A seat that could not be filled kept a prince at his stool without a title. It kept the treasury's gate swinging a little loose. It kept a king from signing alone, and a warrant from being drawn, and a house on the river stairs from being obliged to show its loose sheets until midwinter.

He sat for a long while with his hands flat on the cloth.

He had thought, all that year, that the council was foolish. It was a comfortable thought, and he had held it with some affection. It occurred to him now, in the smell of tallow and old parchment, that it might have been made to look so.

"Counting again?"

Edwyn stood in the doorway with a lute slung across his back and the lantern from the stair swinging in one hand. He wore the sable-lined cloak that he put on when he intended to be admired, and his dark eyes went over the rolls on the table with idle interest, and then, with a little less idleness, to Rowan's face.

"I am recounting," said Rowan.

"There is a difference?"

"A great one. You count to find out what is there. You recount to find out what someone has put there."

Edwyn laughed. He came in, set the lantern on the table, and leaned over the nearest roll with his chin on his fist. "I have never in my life been so glad to be an idle man. Is it the Vine Shore grain again? Orston tells me that half of Rosegate's barns are said to have flooded, and the other half are said to be full."

"It is nothing," Rowan said, and drew the rolls toward him in a single sweep, and began to tie the ribbons. "A column that does not agree. I will find the error."

"You always do. It is the most tiresome thing about you." Edwyn straightened, and gave him a look that was fond and a little puzzled, and tucked the lantern under his arm. "Come up and eat. Elyn has had the cooks keep a goose for you, and she says that if you do not appear she will send Ser Colm to fetch you in a sack."

"In a moment."

"You said that yesterday."

"And I came up yesterday."

"Late," said Edwyn, and went out whistling the first bars of a song Rowan did not know. It was a bright, silly air, in the key that Edwyn used when he was pleased with the world, and it faded down the stair until the silence closed over it.

Rowan did not go up at once. He sat with the nine rolls tied in front of him, and the ninth, still wet, laid a little apart. He had been given a great many tasks in the year since his father died, and he had done each of them as it came, without being asked, because it needed doing. It had not occurred to him until tonight to ask who had arranged that it should.

He blew out the candle. In the dark he could still see the two names, side by side in his own neat hand, like the two pans of a scale that would never come level.
