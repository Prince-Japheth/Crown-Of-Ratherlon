MERECROFT

Lord Hamo Merecroft had written the letter four times, and burned it three.

The fourth copy lay on the coverlet beside him in the grey before dawn, folded once, unsealed, with its top edge curling in the damp. He sat on the edge of the bed in the guest-hall of Foldhall with one boot on and the other in his hand, and he read the first line to the cold fireplace, aloud, in the voice he used when he was alone.

"Your Highness, I have a thing to tell you."

It was a good line. It was plain, and it was true. He had stood in front of the glass in his chamber at Merecroft a month ago and said it to his own face until his face stopped looking sick. Then the thing that came after it stuck in his throat as a fishbone does, and he put the boot on the floor and the letter on the coverlet and sat looking at both.

Downstairs a pot clattered. Someone laughed in the yard. A swallow had got into the eaves above the window and was making a small furious racket. It was the third week of the harvest moon and the sort of morning when a man of five and forty with a good manor and a good sister ought to wake up glad. Hamo had not woken up glad since the spring.

He pulled on the boot. His hands were shaking, as they had shaken since the hunt, and it took three tries to fasten the buckle.

The hunt. He did not want to think about the hunt. He had laughed at the baron and the pears, and he had liked the laugh, because it was the first honest one he had had in weeks. And in the middle of it he had looked up across the grass and seen a pale man in a plain dark doublet standing at an oak with a spare horse, running a thumb along a girth-strap. The man had not looked back. That had been the worst of it. He never looked back. He tested the buckle as though nobody in the world had a thing to hide.

A knock fell on the door, two quick raps and a third.

"My lord?" It was Dunstan, his steward, who had served him twenty years and had never once knocked three times.

"Come in."

Dunstan came in with his cap in his hand. He was a stout, grey, careful man with a face like a loaf, and the loaf had gone the color of tallow.

"It's Wim, my lord."

The boot was half on. Hamo did not finish putting it on.

"They found him at first light. At the water stairs, by the third landing." Dunstan twisted the cap. "The watch say he went in drunk in the night, and the river took him. He's lying under sailcloth. They sent a boy to ask whose man he was, and the boy came to me."

"Wim does not drink."

"No, my lord."

"He has not touched ale in six years. He told me so himself, the day I hired him."

"No, my lord," said Dunstan, very quietly. "I told the sergeant that. He said they all say it."

Hamo stood up. The room tilted a little and settled. On the coverlet the letter lay with its curled edge, and beside it, as he turned for his cloak, he saw the small leather case where Wim had carried every letter that Hamo had ever sent up the river: to Rosegate, to the Varen steward, to the factor at the Fold ford. Wim had been given the last one on Tuesday night, at the postern gate of the castle, in the dark, with a mark pressed into his palm and a whispered order to put it into no hands but the Duke's.

Four days. Hamo counted them on his fingers, and for once he counted correctly.

* * *

The river stairs ran a hundred and forty steps down from the lower town to the Fold, broad and worn and wet at the foot. A crowd had gathered on the third landing, the quiet crowd that a drowned man draws: bargemen with their caps off, washerwomen with their arms folded under their aprons, a pair of boys who had been told to go home and had not. A sergeant of the city watch stood at the edge of it with his thumbs in his belt.

The body lay at the foot of the landing under a square of sailcloth, with its boots showing.

Hamo knew the boots. They were the brown ones, resoled in the spring, with a patch on the left toe that Wim's wife had sewn with red thread because she had run out of brown. He stood looking at the red thread and could not make himself look at anything else.

"Your man, my lord?" The sergeant had come up beside him. He was a square, weather-beaten man named Pike with a manner that had been polite to lords for twenty years and meant to go on being so for twenty more. "My condolences. A bad end. They go in all the time, this season. The stairs are slick and the ale is cheap."

"He did not drink."

"So I'm told, my lord."

"His purse. Was it taken?"

"There was nothing in it, my lord, to take." Pike shrugged. "Four coppers and a fish-hook."

"I gave him a silver mark on Tuesday."

The sergeant's face stayed exactly as it was. "Then he spent it, my lord, and I'd say he spent it in the taverns by the water, for there's nowhere else a bargeman spends a silver mark. I will make inquiry if you wish it."

It was a kind offer and an empty one, and Hamo knew it. He had known it before he asked. He heard himself say, "I should like that," and heard his voice thin out at the end, and a hand closed round his elbow.

"My lord Merecroft."

He turned his head. The man standing beside him wore a plain dark coat of good cloth and a clerk's flat cap, and he carried a tally-stick under one arm, and he had a thin, serious, young-old face that Hamo had seen at a hundred feasts in the corner of the room, writing something down. He was the second son of the old king.

"Prince Rowan," said Hamo.

"I am sorry for your loss." Rowan Brennan did not bow, in a crowd, in a coat like that. He simply inclined his head, the exact depth, and let it be seen. Beside him stood a boy of ten or eleven with an inky face and a satchel as large as his chest, who stared at the sailcloth and then at the ground. "I was coming to count the fish-boats for the counting-house. I did not expect to find a man of yours."

"He was a bargeman," Hamo said. "He ran my letters upriver."

"A trusted servant, then."

"Yes." The word came out cracked. "For nine years."

"Tobb," said the prince, without turning his head. "Find the widow, if she is here. Ask her what she needs, and tell her that the counting-house will see to the burial. Do not argue with her about the cost."

The boy fled. Hamo watched him go and wished he had gone with him.

"You are very kind, Your Highness."

"It is Master Jaspern who is kind. He keeps a fund for this. I merely sign." The prince's dark eyes rested on Hamo's face, and there was a stillness in them that Hamo could not read, the stillness of a man adding a column. "You have the look of a man who has not slept, my lord. May I say it? You have had it all week."

"A bad harvest. A good many worries."

"The Vine Shore harvest is not bad. The flood spoiled a part of Rosegate's, and Duke Roland has said as much. Is it something at Merecroft?"

It was a plain question. It was the plainest in the world, put by a man with a gentle voice and an honest face who had just ordered a widow's burial paid for out of a fund he did not own. And Hamo thought, with a clearness that went through him like cold water: *Say it. Say it now. Say, Your Highness, I have a thing to tell you.*

He opened his mouth.

"Lord Merecroft!"

The voice came from the foot of the stair, warm and easy and carrying. A man was coming up the last of the steps with his hat in his hand: a pale man in a dark doublet, thin, composed, with a small leather purse held out before him in both palms like an offering. He wore tall boots of oiled black leather with a silver-chased buckle at each knee, and there was not a spot of river-damp on them.

"My lord, forgive me. I heard it at the gate. Garrow Pell, factor to Lord Fenlow." He bowed to Hamo, and then to the prince, to each exactly the right depth, and straightened with the purse still held out. "My lord asked me to bring this to the widow at once. Ten marks in silver. He says a man who dies on the river in the king's service, as a bargeman does, should not leave a wife to beg."

"He was not in the king's service," Hamo heard himself say.

"No?" The pale grey eyes were mild. "I understood he ran letters. I thought perhaps it was the king's business."

The stairs were very quiet. A gull cried far out on the water. At Hamo's side, Prince Rowan said nothing at all, and Hamo felt the silence on his left like a hand.

"He ran mine," said Hamo.

"Of course," said the factor. "I beg your pardon, my lord. A bad thing, the river. A man should not carry letters in the dark." He bent his head. "I hope he had no heavier burden than ale."

Then he turned, and went to find the widow with his ten marks, and the crowd on the landing parted around him as water parts around a post.

"A generous gift," said Prince Rowan.

"Yes."

"Lord Fenlow is generous." The prince looked after the pale man. His face was perfectly smooth. "Was it a long acquaintance, my lord? Between your bargeman and Master Pell?"

"I do not think they ever met."

"No," said Rowan. "I should not have thought so either."

He said nothing more. He stood a moment longer, with the tally-stick under his arm, and then inclined his head again, precisely, and walked down the stair toward the boy and the widow. Hamo watched the narrow dark back go away from him and thought, with a sick lurch: *he gave me the door, and I did not go through it.*

* * *

Isolde found him in the afternoon, sitting on the window-seat of his chamber with his cloak still on and the letter in his lap.

She did not knock. She never had. She came in with a rustle of brown wool and shut the door with her heel, a tall woman of two and thirty with her hair coiled low on her neck and a look on her face that Hamo remembered from when he was nine and she was twelve and he had lost her best cloak-pin in the millpond. She took the stool across from him. She looked at the cloak, and the letter, and at him.

"Wim," she said.

"Yes."

"I am sorry. I liked Wim. He taught me to skip a stone." She folded her hands. "Now. Tell me what you did."

"I did nothing."

"Hamo."

"I sent him with a letter."

"To whom?"

He told her. It took longer than it ought to have, because he kept starting sentences and abandoning them, and she waited with a patience that was worse than a scolding. He told her that on the evening of Tuesday he had written four lines to Duke Roland of Rosegate, in his own hand, and sealed them with the plain seal and not the great one. That he had put them into Wim's hand at the postern gate. That the lines had said: *My lord, a remonstrance is being drawn, in your name and without your knowledge, to withhold the Vine Shore grain from the crown. I have been asked to sign it. I have not signed. I wished you to know.*

When he finished, the window was full of the late sun and a pigeon was walking along the sill.

"You wrote it as plainly as that."

"I thought it should be plain."

"And Wim carried it."

"And Wim was found at the foot of the stairs, with nothing in his purse. And no letter in the case."

Isolde sat very still. After a moment she said, in the quiet voice she used for things that mattered, "Who knew you had sent it?"

"No one."

"Hamo."

"No one. I told no one. I told Dunstan to pack a bag for a ride and I handed it over at the gate myself." His voice was climbing and he could not stop it. "I looked about. There was no one in the yard. The postern is out of sight of every window in the keep."

"Then someone was watching the postern."

"Or Wim was drunk."

"He never drank."

"No," said Hamo. He put his face in his hands. "No. He never did."

She let him sit like that. After a time she rose and came to the window-seat and sat down beside him, and put an arm around his shoulders, an awkward, bony arm, and laid her cheek against his hair as she had when he was small.

"What did you say yes to?" she said.

He lifted his head.

"At Fenlow's table. In the spring. You came home from that dinner and you did not eat for two days. Dunstan told me. You were sick in the garden." Her grey eyes were steady. "You said yes to something, and it was not the remonstrance, because you wrote to Rosegate that you had not signed. So it was something before that."

"I cannot tell you."

"Because you are afraid."

"Because I do not know what it was." The words came out in a rush, high and cracked. "That is the truth, Isolde. That is the terrible part. He asked me a question, and it was such a small question, and I said yes because he was smiling and the wine was good and I wanted him to like me. He asked if I would lend my name to a letter to be written, if the need arose, concerning the Vine Shore's rights. If the need arose. I said yes. I said, of course. And I have been trying ever since to remember what else was in the room."

"What else was in the room?"

"His factor." Hamo stared at her. "He sat at the foot of the table, and he wrote nothing down, and he never said one word. And when I rose to go he caught my sleeve and said, 'My lord, you are very good. We shall make it as easy for you as we can.'"

Isolde said nothing for a long moment. Her arm had grown very tight around his shoulders.

"Go to the queen," she said.

"I cannot."

"Go to Prince Rowan, then. He was kind to you today. I saw him from the gate."

"I tried."

"You did not try." She took his chin in her fingers, as she had done when he lied about the cloak-pin, and turned his face to hers. "I will not tell you what to do, Hamo. I am your sister, not your lord. But I am your sister, and I have seen a man's face when he has been bought, and yours is not that face. Yours is the face of a man who has been kept." Her voice shook once, and steadied. "Promise me you will say it to someone before the harvest feast."

"I promise."

"You are lying to me."

"I am trying not to."

"That is the best you have ever done," said Isolde, and kissed his forehead hard, and got up and left the room without another word.

* * *

It was dark when he went to the counting-room.

He had not meant to go. He had meant to go to the sept-yard, where the bargemen would be burying Wim, and he had stood a long time at its gate in the cold with his hat in his hand and the sound of a hymn in the air, and had not gone in. A widow was in there, and children. He could not face them. So he had turned and walked back through the lower town, up the long hill in the dark, past the shuttered stalls and the dying cook-fires, and somehow his feet had taken him up the narrow stair behind the solar that only clerks used.

A single lamp burned in a niche at the stair's end. Beyond it stood a low oak door with iron bands, and under it, a line of yellow light.

He stood in front of it. He lifted his hand. He could hear someone moving inside: the scratch of a quill, the thump of a heavy book being closed, the dry cough of an old man. It was perfectly ordinary. It was the sound of a kingdom being kept, a roll at a time, by people who did not know his name.

*Say it,* he told himself. *Say, I have a thing to tell you.*

The door opened.

It was an old man, bent like a bow, with spotted hands and a pair of spectacles pushed up on his forehead, and he looked at Hamo as a heron looks at a minnow.

"Yes, my lord?"

"I..." Hamo's tongue was thick. "I wished to know the hours of the counting-room."

"From the fourth hour of the morning until the lamps burn out, my lord. Every day but the feast of the first frost." Master Jaspern's eyes were pale and shrewd. "Was there a matter you wished to enroll?"

*Yes. Yes, there was a matter. Four hundred matters. Write them all down.*

"No," said Hamo. "No. Only the hours."

"As you wish."

The old man waited. Behind him, in the lamplight, Hamo could see the long racks of tallies climbing into the dark, and a table, and an empty stool pushed back as though someone had just risen from it. He could see his own shadow on the wall, small and thin and frightened, with its hat in its hands.

"Good night, Master Jaspern."

"Good night, my lord."

The door shut. The line of light under it grew narrow and steady. Hamo stood a moment longer, and then he went down the stair with his hand on the cold stone, one step at a time, counting. He counted aloud, in the dark, in a whisper, to keep from thinking, and when he came to the hundredth step he stopped, because there were no more figures in him.

At the foot of the stair, in the shadow of the postern, a pale man in a dark doublet was waiting with a lantern.

"My lord," said Garrow Pell. "A cold night for walking. I hope the widow was comforted."

Hamo looked at him. He looked at the lantern, and the oiled boots, and the calm grey eyes that had never once, in his hearing, said anything that could be written down.

"She was," he said.

"I am glad. My lord Fenlow will be glad." Pell lifted the lantern, so that its light fell across Hamo's face and made him blink. "He asks that you dine with him again before the harvest feast. A small table. Only the friends who understand one another."

"I am not certain that I..."

"We shall make it as easy for you as we can, my lord." The factor smiled, very gently. "Shall we say the day after tomorrow?"

There was a gull on the roof of the postern. It shifted its feet on the slates and was still.

"Yes," said Hamo Merecroft. "Of course."

He went out through the postern into the dark. The lantern did not follow him. But he walked all the way to the guest-hall with the back of his neck bare and his shoulders hunched, listening for a footstep, and when he reached his chamber he locked the door and put a chair beneath the latch. He sat down on the edge of the bed with the letter in his lap. He turned it over once. The creases were soft with handling, and the ink had begun to blur where his thumb had pressed.

Then he held the corner to the candle, and watched it catch, and held it until it burned his fingers.
