RODRIK

The high summer heat had turned the tilt-yard of Foldhall into a crucible of dust and dented steel. 

It was a heat that smelled of old sweat, oiled leather, and the coppery tang of drawn blood. Rodrik sat in the center of the royal box, his elbows resting heavily on the oak railing, a cup of watered wine sweating in his grip. The box had been built by his grandfather for the comfort of the royal family, draped in the heavy black and bronze banners of the crown, but today it felt like an oven. The canopy offered shade, but it trapped the hot air rising from the packed earth below, making the space close and suffocating.

Below him, two squires were beating each other senseless with blunted tourney swords. 

It was not a grand melee. It was merely an afternoon diversion organized by the Master-at-Arms, Ser Boremund, to keep the younger sons of the Crownfold from finding worse trouble in the city taverns while the summer dragged on. But the court had gathered all the same. The wooden stands ringing the yard were packed tight with lords, ladies, merchants, and knights. The air was thick with the scent of roasted almonds, cheap perfume, and the sharp tang of horse manure blowing in from the stables. 

In the absence of a war, men of ambition would crowd around a wooden sword just to be seen cheering by the newly crowned king. They wore their finest silks and velvets, sweating profusely but refusing to abandon their finery for the sake of comfort. It was a display of wealth, of power, and of subtle positioning in a brand-new reign. 

"The Callenby boy drops his shield too early," Rodrik observed, his voice low enough that only the man standing directly behind his chair could hear. 

Ser Corren, the Lord Commander of the King's Guard, did not move his head. He stood like a monument in his heavy charcoal-grey cloak, the bronze wyvern clasp resting against his collarbone, his gauntleted hands resting on the pommel of his longsword. Corren was a veteran of the First Ashen War, a man who had left half his left ear and all of his mercy in the eastern gullies. He had seen real combat, not this wooden theater, and he held it in quiet contempt. 

"He relies on his reach, Your Grace," Ser Corren replied, his voice a gravelly rumble that barely carried over the roar of the crowd. "It works well enough against boys who are afraid of the wood. A seasoned man will step inside the guard, take the glancing blow off a pauldron, and open his belly before the boy can recover his balance."

Rodrik took a slow sip of his wine. The water had gone warm, tasting flat and stale. "Then let us hope the Eastverge does not send boys," Rodrik murmured, watching the Callenby squire take a brutal strike to the shoulder that sent him stumbling backward into the dust.

It had been thirty days since Captain Harrun marched east. Thirty days of absolute silence from the road. The distance to the Eastverge was a thousand punishing miles, a reality that the lords of the Crownfold seemed eager to forget. Elyra was still pacing the walls of the keep, spending half her days in the roosts with Lark and the other half staring at the eastern horizon, waiting for the sixty days to pass so she could take the sky and give the infantry their head start. 

The logistics of the march had consumed the court. Wagons of grain, barrels of salted beef, and chests of arrowheads had been requisitioned from the surrounding farmlands, draining the royal stores at an alarming rate. Rowan had spent weeks calculating the exact tonnage required to feed twenty men on a thousand-mile march, factoring in the inevitable spoilage and the toll of the high passes. Every ounce of silver spent was meticulously recorded in his ledgers. 

Rodrik felt every one of those thirty days as a physical weight, a stone added to his own shoulders. It was the first test of his reign, and the entire continent was watching to see how the new king would answer it. 

A sudden roar from the crowd pulled his attention back to the yard. The Fenlow squire, a thick-necked youth with rust-colored hair and a brutal, hacking swing, had managed to crack his wooden blade across the Callenby boy's helm. The sound of wood on steel cracked like a whip. The Callenby boy went down hard, his wooden sword spinning away into the dirt, raising a cloud of red dust that hung stagnant in the windless air. 

Ser Boremund stepped forward, raising a heavy, mailed hand to call an end to the bout. His voice carried like a cracked bell over the applause. He hauled the Callenby boy to his feet, checking his pupils for a concussion, before waving the chirurgeons forward. 

"A strong showing," Lord Fenlow said, leaning over the railing of the adjacent box. 

Fenlow was a wealthy man who smelled heavily of rosewater and owned half the timber in the western hills. He wore a doublet of rich green velvet that seemed entirely unsuited for the sweltering heat, yet he did not sweat. He had the kind of face that always seemed on the verge of a smile, though the warmth never quite reached his pale blue eyes. His hands were soft, entirely unacquainted with the rough texture of a sword hilt or a Nyxen's hide.

"My nephew has the makings of a true knight," Fenlow continued, his voice loud enough to ensure the lords sitting nearby could hear every word. "He understands that hesitation is death. It is the kind of strength Foldhall will need if the Ashlands truly press the borders, Your Grace. House Fenlow stands ready to offer three hundred such men, should the crown require them."

Rodrik did not sigh. A king sighing was a king showing his throat. He took another slow sip of his watered wine, letting the silence stretch out until Fenlow shifted uncomfortably against the railing. 

"The realm has strength, Lord Fenlow," Rodrik said finally, his tone perfectly level. "It is written in the laws of the men who built this castle. It is maintained by the steel of the men standing in this yard. And it is defended by the blood of the men marching on the kingsroad as we speak."

"Of course, Your Grace," Fenlow agreed quickly, dipping his head in a shallow, practiced bow. "But twenty swords is a small number for a thousand miles of border. A king who sends too few men invites questions from his lords. And questions breed instability. The merchants are already speaking of a downturn in trade from the Vine Shore, purely based on the uncertainty of the eastern roads."

It was the same argument dressed in a different tunic. Every day, a different lord found a way to test the boundaries of the new reign. They brought it up at supper, during the morning petitions, and now over the broken teeth of a squire in the dirt. They were pushing him, trying to see if King Rodrik was as hard as King Aldren VIII had been. They wanted to know how much coin and influence they could extract from the young king before he snapped. 

Rodrik gripped his cup tighter, the silver digging into his calloused palm. "The Ashlands will test the borders regardless of how many spears House Fenlow offers, my lord. Reavers respect steel, not numbers on a ledger. If you are so concerned with the eastern roads, perhaps you should send your strong nephew to hold the watch with Captain Harrun, instead of paying Ser Boremund to let him beat boys with sticks in a yard."

Fenlow's smile tightened, the edges of it going brittle. He glanced quickly at the surrounding lords to see who had heard the rebuke. "You speak truly, Your Grace. My house lives only to serve the crown. We only pray for a swift victory."

Rodrik turned his attention away, dismissing the man without a word. He looked down the length of the royal box. Three seats down, his brother Rowan sat with a heavy ledger open on his lap, entirely ignoring the blood in the dirt. Rowan's ash-charcoal hair was pulled back severely, and his face bore the tight pinch of a man trying to balance an impossible sum. He was dressed plainly, in unadorned black wool, a stark contrast to the peacocks surrounding him. 

Beside Rowan, Edwyn leaned back in his chair, his long legs stretched out in front of him. He held a cup of summer wine loose in his grip, watching his older brother with dark, endlessly amused eyes. Edwyn wore a tunic of deep plum silk, unlaced at the collar against the heat, looking entirely at ease in a setting that was slowly driving Rodrik mad. He had spent the morning sparring with the master-at-arms, but showed no signs of fatigue.

"You are missing a fine display of butchery, Rowan," Edwyn called out over the cheering crowd, as two new squires were pushed into the yard. "The Quenby boy just lost two teeth to a shield bash. You should write that down. A kingdom runs on teeth, does it not? We can use them to pave the kingsroad when the stone runs out."

Rowan did not look up from his numbers. The scratch of his quill was relentless, loud enough to be heard over the dull roar of the crowd. "A kingdom runs on grain, Edwyn. The Quenby boy's teeth will not feed the capital when winter comes. If you care to look at the ledgers from the Vine Shore, you will see that Duke Roland's harvest is three weeks late, and his taxes are suspiciously light for a season with so much rain. He is hoarding the yield, waiting to see which way the wind blows."

"I have no interest in the Vine Shore's grain," Edwyn countered with a lazy grin. He was not angry. He was never angry. He taunted Rowan purely for the sport of it, treating the steward's rigid, unyielding patience as a puzzle to be picked apart. "I am much more interested in why Lord Elmrick's second daughter keeps looking at the royal box instead of the tourney. Do you think she is admiring your ledger, brother? Perhaps she has a deep passion for taxation."

Rowan finally paused, the quill hovering over the parchment. He did not look at the Elmrick girl, who was indeed staring unabashedly at the royal box from the lower stands, fanning herself with a painted silk fan depicting a flight of green birds. 

"She is admiring the proximity to the throne," Rowan said dryly. "And since I have a wife and two children, and the king has a queen, she is likely calculating how long it will take you to drink yourself into a stupor so she might press an advantage. You are the only unattached royal in this box, Edwyn. She is hunting."

Edwyn laughed, a bright, carrying sound that turned several heads in the lower stands, including the Elmrick girl, who blushed deeply and hid behind her fan. "A harsh assessment, but mathematically sound. You see? You do have a talent for observation when you lift your eyes from the ink. You should apply it more often. You might notice that Lord Fenlow is currently whispering to Baron Callenby about exactly how many spears House Fenlow commands."

Rodrik stood up, the legs of his heavy wooden chair scraping loudly against the floorboards. The noise of the yard, the heat, the endless posturing—it was all suddenly too much. The crown felt like a vice tightening around his temples, a physical pressure that made it difficult to breathe. The air in the box felt stagnant, thick with the smell of cheap perfume and unspoken treachery.

Ser Corren stepped back immediately, clearing the path, his hand resting lightly on his sword hilt. Two more knights of the King's Guard, stationed at the base of the stairs, snapped to attention, their chainmail clinking softly in the heat.

"I am going to the solar," Rodrik announced to his brothers, his voice carrying the hard edge of a command. "If the lords ask, tell them I am reading reports from the Icewide."

"They will ask if the reports brought you a war," Edwyn noted lightly, swirling the dark red wine in his cup. "They are remarkably single-minded today. Fenlow has brought up his timber contracts three times before noon."

"Then tell them to mind their timber and their grain, or I will feed them to Hart," Rodrik said. "He is hungry, and I am losing my patience."

He left the royal box without waiting for a reply, descending the wooden stairs with long, angry strides. Ser Corren matched his pace exactly, a silent, imposing shadow. The wooden planks of the stairs groaned under the combined weight of their boots and armor. 

Rather than turning left toward the royal apartments in the upper keep, Rodrik turned right, pushing through the heavy iron-bound doors that led to the eastern wing, and the long, covered bridge that connected the main keep to the roosts. 

"Your Grace?" Ser Corren asked, his voice rough but respectful. "The solar is..."

"The solar is full of ledgers and whispers," Rodrik snapped, his boots striking hard against the stone of the bridge. "I need air that doesn't smell like Lord Fenlow's rosewater."

The air in the roosts smelled of raw meat, woodsmoke, and the sharp, ozone tang of massive predators. As they crossed the bridge, the temperature climbed noticeably. The roosts were a massive, cavernous structure built directly into the side of the mountain, a series of enormous, open-faced caves enclosed by thick iron bars and reinforced stone walls. 

The sound of the tilt-yard was completely eclipsed by the low, continuous rumbling and occasional sharp hisses echoing through the cavern. There were dozens of Nyxens housed here, ranging from the small, lithe courier beasts to the massive, terrifying war-mounts of the royal family. 

A Handler, dressed in thick, scorch-marked leather and carrying a long iron hook, rushed forward and bowed low as Rodrik approached. 

"Your Grace," the Handler stammered, wiping soot from his forehead. "We did not expect you. Prince Gareth is flying one of the younger beasts in the lower pens, but..."

"I am here for Hart," Rodrik said, not slowing his pace. 

"Of course, Your Grace. He is restless today. The heat..."

Rodrik ignored him, striding past the smaller enclosures toward the massive, isolated pen at the far end of the cavern. Hart was not a beast that shared space willingly. He was one of the largest living Nyxens since Oak's departure, a terrifying mass of iron-grey fur and muscle. 

When Rodrik reached the iron bars, Hart was pacing. The beast moved with a terrifying grace, his four heavy, clawed limbs eating up the ground in long, fluid strides. His massive, bat-like head swung back and forth, his nostrils flaring as he took in the scent of his rider. He let out a low, rumbling growl that vibrated in Rodrik's chest, a sound like grinding stones. 

"Open it," Rodrik ordered the Handler. 

Ser Corren stepped forward, his hand resting on his sword hilt, a reflexive motion. Even a veteran of the Ashen War respected the sheer destructive power of a fully grown Nyxen. 

The Handler pulled the heavy iron lever, and the massive gate ground open with a screech of metal on stone. 

Rodrik stepped inside the pen alone. The heat radiating off Hart was intense, like standing in front of an open forge. The beast stopped pacing and turned toward him, his massive wings half-unfurling, the thick, downy joints flexing. Hart's eyes were a cold, intelligent amber, locking onto Rodrik with absolute focus. 

Rodrik did not flinch. He walked forward, raising a bare hand, and placed it firmly on the coarse, iron-grey fur just above the beast's blunt snout. 

Hart let out a sharp exhalation of breath, blowing a cloud of hot dust over Rodrik's boots, and pushed his massive head against Rodrik's palm. The aggression bled out of the beast instantly, replaced by the profound, unspoken bond between rider and mount. 

"They are testing us, Hart," Rodrik murmured in Aurouvian, his voice low and steady. "They sit in the stands and watch boys beat each other with sticks, and they plot how to squeeze coin from a new king before I have even settled on the throne."

Hart rumbled, a deep, resonant sound of agreement, closing his amber eyes as Rodrik stroked his heavy brow. 

"They forget," Rodrik whispered, leaning his forehead against the Nyxen's hard skull. "They forget what we are. They forget what happened the last time they tested Foldhall."

He stood there for a long time, drawing strength from the immense power of the beast. Here, there were no whispers. There was no politics. There was only blood, and loyalty, and the sky. When he finally stepped back, he felt the suffocating pressure in his chest ease. The crown was heavy, but he had the strength to bear it. 

He left the pen, nodding to the Handler to close the gate, and turned back toward the main keep. 

But he did not go directly to the solar. Instead, he climbed the narrow, winding stone stairs that led to the eastern battlements. Ser Corren followed him without a word, his chainmail clinking faintly with every step. When they reached the top of the wall, the wind caught them, hot and dry, smelling of distant pine and dust. 

From this vantage point, Foldhall was a sprawling beast of stone and slate, clinging to the side of the mountain. Below them, the kingsroad wound its way out of the city gates, cutting a thin, pale scar across the dark green of the farmlands, stretching relentlessly east toward the horizon. 

Rodrik rested his hands on the sun-baked stone of the crenellations and stared out at that road. 

Thirty days. Harrun was thirty days out. By now, the men would be exhausted, their boots worn thin, their shoulders bruised by the weight of their packs and armor. They would be marching through the arid foothills, the heat baking the moisture from their bodies. And they still had thirty more days to go before they reached the deep gullies of the Eastgirt. 

Rodrik remembered the march. He remembered the First Ashen War. He had been a boy, barely old enough to wield a real sword, riding a younger, smaller Hart beside his father. 

King Aldren VIII had been a terrifying man. He had not ruled with diplomacy or soft words. He had ruled with Oak. Oak had been a Nyxen of legendary size, a beast so massive that his wingspan had cast a shadow large enough to cover a small village. When the Ashlands had risen in rebellion ten years ago, Aldren had not sent envoys. He had mounted Oak and burned the rebel camps to ash. The war had been brutal, swift, and completely one-sided. 

Rodrik remembered watching the sky turn orange with fire, smelling the sickening scent of roasting flesh and burning bone armor. He remembered the absolute, paralyzing fear in the eyes of the reavers as Oak descended upon them like a god of destruction. 

His father had taught him a harsh lesson on that campaign. *The realm does not respect love, Rodrik,* Aldren had said, his face smeared with soot and blood. *Love is a luxury for merchants and minor lords. The realm respects terror. It respects the shadow of a Nyxen. As long as Oak is in the sky, they will not dare to challenge Foldhall.*

But Aldren was dead. And Oak... Oak had gone mad with grief the night the king passed. The ancient beast had shattered his chains, broken through the iron bars of the roost, and flown north into the high, unmapped crags, refusing all riders. The greatest weapon in Ratherlon's arsenal was gone. 

And the realm knew it. 

That was why Lord Fenlow felt bold enough to mock the crown's military response in the tilt-yard. That was why the Ashlands were gathering their tribes in the deep gullies. That was why the Vine Shore was withholding its grain taxes. They all smelled blood in the water. They looked at Rodrik and saw a king without the ultimate deterrent, a young monarch vulnerable to the slow, grinding machinery of court politics and border wars. 

*They push because they want to see if I will break,* Rodrik thought bitterly, gripping the stone until his knuckles turned white. *They want to know if I am my father.*

He closed his eyes, letting the hot wind rush over his face. He missed his father. He missed the brutal, simple certainty of Aldren's reign. But he could not be Aldren. He had to be Rodrik. And Rodrik could not solve every problem with fire. 

He had to hold the realm together with diplomacy, with steel, and with the loyalty of men like Ser Corren and Captain Harrun. He had to be a king who built, rather than a king who destroyed. 

"The wind is turning, Your Grace," Ser Corren said softly from behind him, breaking the silence. 

Rodrik opened his eyes. The sky above the eastern mountains was beginning to bruise, dark clouds gathering on the horizon. A summer storm was brewing. 

"Let it turn," Rodrik said, his voice hard as iron. "We will weather it."

He finally turned away from the battlements, leaving the kingsroad and the memory of his father behind him, and began the long descent back into the labyrinth of Foldhall to face his council. 

The corridors of the upper keep were cooler, built from thick grey stone that held the night's chill long into the afternoon. But they were no less crowded. Foldhall was a city within a castle, a sprawling labyrinth of ambition and duty. Tapestries depicting the glorious victories of the First Ashen War hung from the walls, their vibrant threads dulled by decades of smoke from the torches. Statues of long-dead kings stared down from their alcoves, their stone faces offering no answers to the problems of the living.

As Rodrik walked, the court parted for him like water before the prow of a ship. Serving girls carrying massive trays of roasted meat and sweet bread dipped into quick, terrified curtsies, pressing themselves against the tapestries to give the king a wide berth. Two minor knights from the Merelands, arguing loudly over a wager regarding the outcome of the melee, fell completely silent and bowed deeply as the charcoal-grey cloak of the King's Guard swept past them. A maester, burdened with a towering stack of bound ledgers detailing the kingdom's dwindling silver reserves, nearly dropped his burden in his haste to clear the corridor. 

The world was packed tight with people, yet Rodrik felt entirely isolated within it. He was surrounded by men who swore him fealty, but he knew exactly what they whispered when he left the room. They looked at his new crown and saw an opportunity. They did not see the man who had ridden a Nyxen into battle; they saw a throne they could influence.

When he finally reached his royal solar, the heavy oak doors were already standing open. The room was large and airy, with tall, narrow windows that looked out over the sprawling city of Foldhall and the distant, hazy line of the eastern mountains. A fire was unlit in the massive hearth, the room smelling of old paper, beeswax, and lemon polish. The walls were lined with towering bookshelves, holding the recorded history of a kingdom that felt increasingly fragile.

Standing by the long oak table, his hands folded neatly in front of him, was Lord Harwood. 

Harwood was the Master of Whisperers. It was a terrible title, one that Rodrik actively despised. It announced to every visiting lord, every merchant, and every ambitious second son exactly who they should not speak to. It completely defeated the purpose of a spy. A true spymaster should be a man no one noticed, a steward or a cupbearer, someone who faded into the background of a busy hall. But his father, Aldren VIII, had appointed Harwood to the position, and Rodrik had not yet found a reason to remove him. 

Harwood was a small, unassuming man who wore plain brown spun wool, entirely devoid of any house colors or heraldry. He kept his voice so low that men instinctively leaned forward to hear him, putting themselves off balance. He possessed a face that was completely forgettable, a quality that made him exceptionally dangerous in a court filled with peacocks.

"Your Grace," Harwood murmured, bowing deeply as Rodrik entered. 

"Close the doors, Corren," Rodrik ordered, stepping further into the room. 

The Lord Commander pulled the heavy oak doors shut with a solid thud, sealing the solar off from the noise of the keep. Ser Corren took up his post on the inside of the door, standing at rigid attention, his eyes fixed firmly on the far wall. He was a man who heard everything and repeated nothing, a true shield of the crown. 

"What do you have, Harwood?" Rodrik asked, walking straight to the sideboard. He poured himself another cup of wine, not bothering to water it this time. He did not offer one to the spymaster. The dark vintage from the southern vineyards was strong, meant to be sipped, but Rodrik drank a quarter of it in a single swallow, letting the heat of the alcohol burn away the frustration of the tilt-yard. 

"Rumors from the Eastverge, Your Grace," Harwood said softly, stepping closer to the table. His boots made absolutely no sound on the thick rugs. "The merchants traveling the high passes say the Ashlands are moving. It is not just the usual summer raiding parties looking for grain and steel. They say the tribes are gathering."

Rodrik paused, the silver cup halfway to his lips. "Gathering? Under one banner?"

"They have no banners, Your Grace. You know this. But they have a purpose. The bone-wearers are massing in the deep gullies, stockpiling dried meat, forged iron arrowheads, and leather bindings. They are preparing for a long campaign. They are waiting for the snows to close the roads from the Crownfold. Once the passes are blocked, they will sweep down into the Eastgirt."

Rodrik frowned, staring into his dark wine. "Harrun is thirty days out. It will be another thirty before he reaches Duke Simon. If the tribes are gathering in force, twenty swords will not hold the watch. They will be slaughtered before the snows even begin to fall. We sent a token force to secure the peace, not an army to fight a war."

"Duke Simon knows this," Harwood added smoothly, his tone betraying no emotion. "He has already called his levies. The blacksmiths in his holdfasts are working day and night to produce spearheads and chainmail. But he is a proud, stubborn man, and he resents the crown's slow response. He will not ask for Foldhall's army until he is already bleeding from a dozen wounds. He intends to meet the Ashlands in the field with only Eastgirt men."

"He will lose the field," Rodrik stated flatly, setting his cup down with a sharp clatter on the wooden table. "The Eastgirt men are tough, born to the hard country, but they cannot fight a unified Ashlands without the Crownfold infantry to anchor their lines. Simon is an excellent commander, but he cannot perform miracles with vastly outnumbered levies."

"There is another matter, Your Grace," Harwood said, his voice dropping even lower. It was a calculated move, forcing Rodrik to step closer, to enter the spymaster's space, to become conspiratorial. "One closer to home."

"Speak it. I am weary of riddles today."

"The lords in the yard," Harwood said, his eyes flicking briefly toward the narrow window that overlooked the tilt-yard. "They are not just whispering about the Eastverge. They are whispering about taxes. Lord Fenlow has been speaking privately with Baron Callenby, Lord Elmrick, and several of the Vine Shore lords who arrived last week. They have met three times in the last two days."

Rodrik drank his wine, the liquid burning a slow path down his throat. "Let them whisper. Men with too much coin and too little war always find something to complain about. It is the nature of the court. They plot because they are greedy."

"This is more than a complaint over harbor dues, Your Grace," Harwood pressed gently, his tone never rising above a murmur. "They are suggesting that if Foldhall cannot secure the eastern roads, the Vine Shore should withhold its grain shipments entirely, retaining them for their own defense. They are already drafting the legal arguments in their private solars. They plan to present it to the council before the harvest festival."

Rodrik stared at the spymaster, his anger flaring bright and sudden, hot as a forge fire. "They want to test me. They want to see if a new king will fold to a strike on the treasury before I have even been on the throne for a year."

"They want to know if you are your father, Your Grace. When King Aldren died, and the beast Oak abandoned the skies, the realm felt the foundation crack. Oak was a symbol of absolute terror, a guarantee of peace through fear. Now he is gone, and the lords are looking to see how far they can push the new crown."

"The crown will push back," Rodrik snapped, slamming his silver cup down on the table with enough force to dent the metal. The sound echoed sharply in the quiet room, causing Harwood to blink slowly. "It is my sword, and my Nyxen. If Fenlow wants to discuss withholding timber or grain, tell him to come to the roosts and discuss it with Hart. I am sure the beast would love to hear his thoughts on taxation before he burns him to ash."

"That would silence Lord Fenlow, undoubtedly," Harwood agreed, a thin, humorless smile touching his lips. "But it would not silence the realm. The whispers will continue. They will spread to the minor houses, the merchants, the smallfolk. They will grow louder."

Rodrik stared at the man for a long, dangerous moment. He had half a mind to order Ser Corren to throw the spymaster out the window, to let him fall into the dust of the yard below, a warning to all the whispering lords. But Harwood was only telling him the truth. A king who killed the messenger soon found himself entirely deaf to the plots forming in the shadows. He needed the man's eyes and ears, even if he despised the words they brought him.

"Leave the reports on the table," Rodrik ordered, waving a hand in dismissal, turning his back on the spymaster. "Keep your ears in Fenlow's camp. If he speaks of the Vine Shore taxes again, I want to know exactly who agrees with him, and I want a count of the swords they command."

"As you command, Your Grace." Harwood bowed deeply and slipped out of the room, moving as silently as a ghost passing through a stone wall. 

When the doors closed again, Rodrik stood alone in the center of the solar. He felt the suffocating weight of the crown pressing down on him, heavier than the physical gold he wore on his brow during feast days. The Eastverge was on the brink of a brutal war, his lords were actively testing him in the tilt-yard, and the legacy of his father hung over every decision he made. He was a king surrounded by ambitious men, and he had to prove he was strong enough to hold them in line.

He walked to the window and looked out over the sprawling city. The roofs of Foldhall baked under the afternoon sun, a sea of slate and thatch that stretched out toward the farmlands. Beyond the city walls, the kingsroad wound its way east. He gripped the stone ledge of the window, his knuckles turning white. 

The heavy oak door opened behind him. 

He turned quickly, a sharp reprimand already forming on his lips. He had given strict orders to Corren not to be disturbed by anyone, not even his brothers. But the words died in his throat before he could voice them. 

It was Elyn. 

She wore a gown of deep blue, the color of the Vine Shore, the heavy fabric trimmed with silver thread that caught the light from the window. Her dark hair was pinned back against the heat, exposing the elegant line of her neck. Two sworn shields of the King's Guard stood in the corridor behind her, their green cloaks swapped for the formal charcoal-grey of the royal detail. They were armed and alert, scanning the hallway for any sign of trouble.

She stepped into the solar and pushed the heavy oak doors shut herself, entirely ignoring Ser Corren, leaving the guards outside in the hall. 

"You left the tourney," Elyn said quietly, her grey eyes locking onto his across the long room. 

"I left a den of vultures," Rodrik replied, running a hand through his dark, hammered-steel hair. He let the anger bleed into his voice, knowing he did not need to hide it from her. She was the only person in the world who did not require a kingly facade. "Fenlow is demanding more swords to my face, and Harwood tells me the Vine Shore is plotting to withhold grain behind my back. They want to test the new king. They want to see if I am soft."

Elyn walked across the room, her steps completely silent on the thick Myrish carpets. She stopped in front of him, close enough that he could smell the faint scent of lavender and the sharp tang of the sealing wax she had been using all morning to answer petitions. 

"They see a vulnerability in transition," Rodrik continued, his voice dropping, the deep frustration evident in every syllable. "If I ride to the Eastverge to settle Simon Kelward's feud myself, they will say I am abandoning the capital and neglecting the Vine Shore. They will use my absence to hoard their taxes and build their own strength."

Elyn did not answer immediately. She reached up and placed her hands flat against his chest, right over his heart, feeling the rapid, angry pulse beneath his tunic. Her eyes were steady, calm, and completely devoid of the careful, diplomatic mask she wore for the court. 

"Rodrik," she said softly. 

"I should have gone with Harrun," he muttered, covering her hands with his own calloused fingers. The touch grounded him, pulling him back from the edge of his anger. "I should have mounted Hart and burned the Ashlands reavers out of those gullies myself. It would have silenced Fenlow instantly. It would have reminded them exactly what the crown can do when it is provoked."

"Rodrik," she said again, louder this time, her voice firm and demanding his full attention. 

He stopped talking. He looked down at her, really looked at her for the first time since she entered the room. She looked tired, the kind of deep, bone-weary fatigue that came from managing a hundred minor crises, settling petitions, and balancing the delicate egos of the court before noon. But beneath the exhaustion, there was a quiet, luminous triumph. It radiated from her, bright and entirely undeniable. 

"You are not going to the Eastverge," Elyn said, her voice dropping to a fierce, intense whisper. "You are going to stay right here in Foldhall, and you are going to let Elyra handle Simon Kelward and his border war."

Rodrik frowned, confusion fighting the residual anger in his chest. "Elyn, the lords..."

"The lords will have to wait," she interrupted gently, a brilliant smile finally breaking through her composed expression. 

She moved one of his hands, guiding it down, pressing his rough, calloused palm flat against the fabric of her gown, right over her stomach. 

The silence in the solar stretched out, heavy and absolute. The distant noise of the yard, the ambition of Lord Fenlow, the vast, punishing distance of the eastern road, the dark whisperings of the spymaster—all of it vanished in a single heartbeat, leaving only the steady warmth of her skin beneath his hand. 

Rodrik stared at her. His breath caught sharply in his throat. The king who could hold a war council without flinching, the man who had faced the First Ashen War without a single tremor of fear, suddenly found himself entirely without words. The entire world narrowed down to the space between them. 

"A child," Rodrik whispered, the word feeling impossibly fragile on his tongue. 

"An heir," Elyn said, her eyes shining. "A son, or a daughter who will ride a Nyxen and terrify them all. We are starting our family, Rodrik. Right now."

Rodrik dropped to his knees. The movement was sudden, stripped of all royal dignity. He wrapped his arms around her waist, burying his face in the heavy fabric of her gown, holding her as if she were the only solid thing in a collapsing world. 

He did not care about the King's Guard standing outside the door. He did not care about the impending war in the Eastgirt. He did not care about Fenlow, or Callenby, or the Ashlands. The crushing, suffocating weight of the new crown lifted from his shoulders, replaced by a fierce, protective joy that burned hotter and brighter than any furnace. This was not a political victory. This was life.

"If it is a boy," Rodrik whispered, pulling back slightly to look up at her face, "we will name him after your father. Or mine."

Elyn shook her head, a soft smile touching her lips. "No. If it is a boy, we will give him a new name. Not an Aldren, not a Rodrik. He will not carry the ghosts of the past. He will be the first of his name."

Rodrik stood, tracing the line of her jaw with his thumb. "And if it is a girl?"

"Then we will name her Seren," Elyn said fiercely, the exhaustion entirely gone from her grey eyes. "After the first daughter of the dynasty who refused to let the lords dictate her fate. We will name her Seren, and we will put her on a Nyxen before she can walk."

Rodrik laughed, a genuine, booming sound that echoed off the stone walls of the solar, causing Ser Corren to shift his weight outside the door. "Seren. The lords will whisper about it for weeks."

"Good," Elyn replied, leaning her head against his chest. "Let them whisper. Let them fear it. As long as they respect it."

"They will," Rodrik promised, holding her tight against him. "They will respect the blood of Foldhall, or they will learn what it means to face it."

He stayed there for a long time, listening to the steady, reassuring beat of her heart beneath the heavy fabric. The cool draft from the high windows washed over them, but Rodrik only felt the warmth of his wife's embrace.

Elyn rested her hands on his shoulders, her touch light but grounding. "We will have to tell the council," she murmured softly, her fingers tracing the tense line of his neck. "Rowan will want to calculate the cost of a royal nursery, and Edwyn will likely write a terrible song about it before supper. The court will need to know."

Rodrik let out a rough, breathy laugh, the sound muffled against her gown. "Let Edwyn sing. Let Rowan count the coppers. Let Fenlow choke on his own ambition in the yard. They can have their ledgers and their wooden swords. We have the future."

"I will summon the maester to confirm it," Rodrik said, his voice finding its strength again, the deep rumble of a king who had reclaimed his kingdom. "And then I will stand in the great hall tonight and announce it to the entire court."

"Do not gloat, Rodrik," Elyn chided gently, though her smile widened, revealing the quiet joy she had been holding back. "A king who gloats is a king who invites envy. We will announce it plainly, as a fact of our lives. A strong foundation for our reign."

"A strong foundation," he agreed, his eyes locking onto hers. "And if any lord in that hall dares to complain about grain taxes tonight, I will take them to the roosts myself and let Hart explain it to them."

Elyn laughed, a bright, clear sound that completely transformed the heavy atmosphere of the solar. "Leave Hart out of this for now. He has enough to be angry about with the heat. We do not need him setting fire to the great hall."

Rodrik pulled her into a tight embrace, resting his chin on the crown of her head. He closed his eyes, inhaling the scent of her hair, the lavender, and the wax. The realm was still fractured. The Eastverge was still bleeding. The Ashlands were still massing in the deep gullies, preparing to test the strength of Foldhall's steel. 

But for the first time since his coronation, Rodrik did not feel the suffocating pressure of being a new king. The kingdom of Ratherlon would endure. And whatever challenges the winter brought, they would face them not as a newly crowned couple struggling for respect, but as a dynasty taking root. 

Later that evening, the Great Hall of Foldhall was a riot of color and noise. The long wooden tables were groaning under the weight of the feast—roasted boar, stuffed pheasant, honeyed root vegetables, and river trout baked in clay. The torches burned brightly in their iron sconces, casting dancing shadows against the high stone walls. 

At the high table, Rodrik sat in the heavy oak chair that served as his throne. Elyn sat beside him, wearing the silver crown of the queen consort, her face serene and composed. 

The lords of the Crownfold were eating and drinking with their usual boisterous energy. Lord Fenlow was holding court at the center table, regaling a group of minor barons with the tale of his nephew's victory in the tilt-yard. Baron Callenby was arguing with Lord Elmrick over the price of timber. It was business as usual. 

Until Edwyn stood up. 

Rodrik's youngest brother did not ask for silence. He merely picked up his lute, a beautiful instrument crafted from polished dark wood, and strummed a single, resonant chord that cut through the din of the hall like a knife. 

The conversations died away, replaced by the expectant silence of a court that knew a performance was beginning. Edwyn was a master of the crowd. He possessed a clear, carrying tenor voice that could silence a room faster than a royal command. 

He began to sing. It was a new song, one Rodrik had never heard before. It was a fast, lilting melody, full of clever wordplay and sharp rhymes. 

It was a joyful, soaring song about a new king and queen, and how the stonemasons of Foldhall would have to start carving a new cradle, one forged strong enough to hold a future rider of the sky. 

The hall erupted in cheers. Several of the younger knights slammed their tankards against the tables in loud, booming appreciation. The tension of the new reign, the anxiety over the borders—all of it dissolved into a moment of pure, shared celebration.

Rodrik watched Lord Fenlow's face. The wealthy timber lord raised his wine cup high in a toast, a genuine, booming laugh escaping his lips. 

Rowan, sitting on Rodrik's left, closed his ledger with a sharp snap. "Edwyn is loud," the steward muttered, though the corners of his mouth were twitching up into a rare smile. "And the cost of a royal christening will drain the silver reserves."

"Let him sing," Rodrik said, leaning back in his chair and picking up his own cup of wine, raising it toward the hall. "The silver will flow, Rowan. Tonight, we celebrate."

Elyn placed a hand over his. "It is a beautiful song," she whispered, her eyes shining in the torchlight. 

Rodrik looked around the Great Hall. He saw the joy, the relief, and the sudden, fierce loyalty in the eyes of his lords. He saw the master of whisperers, Harwood, standing in the shadows near the kitchens, nodding slowly to himself, conceding that the crown had just won a massive political victory simply by living. 

The winter would still come. The Ashlands would still march. The Eastverge would still bleed. But they would face it together. The kingdom of Ratherlon was alive, and its heart was beating strong.
