WYNN

The first barrow of mash went into the roost at the fourth hour, and Hart did not look at it.

Wynn set the trough down on the flagstones of the open bay and stepped back, wiping his hands on his apron. The mash steamed in the cold: pears and bruised apples chopped fine, with a fistful of oats and a ladle of honey water to bind it, the way old Oren had taught him to make it in the winter of his first year. The great tawny beast lay on the stone with his chin on his forelimbs and his eyes on the gate. The tip of his tail lay across his own hind foot, perfectly still.

"Come on, my lord," said Wynn. "It's good this morning. I put in the Callenby pears."

Hart breathed out through his nose. A leaf that had blown onto the stone skittered a hand's breadth away.

Up the ladder in the loft, a board creaked, and old Oren came down it backward, one rung at a time, with his bad knee held out to the side like a man carrying a pail. He stopped on the bottom rung and looked at the trough, and at Hart, and at Wynn.

"Not touched?"

"Not a bite."

"He had a pear off me at the third hour."

"A pear is not mash."

"No," said Oren, and came down the last rung. He was a small, bowlegged man of sixty with white stubble and a face that had spent forty years in the weather, and the stains on his leather apron were older than Wynn's mother. He went and put both hands flat on the Nyxen's ribs, first on one side and then on the other, and stood with his eyes shut. Wynn had seen him do it a thousand times. Oren counted ribs the way other men counted coin, by feel, and since the winter his eyes had begun to cloud he did it more.

"He's not sick," said Oren.

"He's sulking."

"He's waiting." Oren took his hands away and wiped them down his apron. "The king said stay. The king said he'd bring pears. So Hart's staying, and he's waiting for the pears, and he'll not eat what a handler puts in front of him while the king's word is still hanging. It's a point of honor with him."

"He is a Nyxen. They have no honor."

"Tell him that."

Wynn looked at Hart. Hart looked at the gate. After a moment, to his own surprise, Wynn said, "I'm sorry, my lord," and felt his face go hot, and Oren turned away to hide his grin.

They had forty beasts in the Foldhall roost on a day like this one, if a man counted wild adults, and eleven if he counted only the ones that slept in bays. Of those eleven, two belonged to the family. The rest were the old unbonded kind, the great grey and tan mothers and uncles who came and went as they pleased across the Crownfold and the high crags and roosted in the cliff-bays when the weather turned. They had no riders. They had handlers, which was a poorer thing, and Wynn had been one for six years, since he was sixteen and his mother put him in the cart that took him up the hill.

He fetched the long brush. The bay's open face looked out over the roost-yard and the castle's slate roofs and the pale line of the kennel road, and the whole length of the cliff behind him was pocked with the dark mouths of other bays, each with a trough, each with a bed of straw. Down in the yard, in the first of the light, boys were carrying water. A woman named Brisa was dragging the nursery gate open with her hip.

From the farthest bay on the east side, a slate-brown shape unfolded itself and yawned.

"Briar's up," Wynn said.

"She'll want her breakfast."

"She'll eat it, too."

"Prince Edwyn's got his Nyxen well in hand," said Oren. "I've said it for years. A man who can talk a lord out of his purse can talk a beast onto a trough."

Hart lifted his head and stared at Briar with an expression of such pure and profound reproach that Wynn nearly dropped the brush. Then, slowly, the great tawny head lowered itself onto the forelimbs again, and one ear turned toward the gate, and stayed there.

* * *

Wynn kept the pup.

He had not been asked. It had happened in the spring, with Brisa's belly too big to stoop and Oren's knee gone bad, and one wet morning someone had pushed a wicker basket into his arms and said, *mind that.* The thing inside the basket had been the size of a loaf and the color of wet sand, with a face that was all eyes and ears and a mouth that opened and shut with soundless energy. Lark had dropped it three days before and gone back to the Princess without a backward look. Her pups did not interest her, Brisa said. Her pups interested her about as much as a man's old hat.

It was four months since, and the pup had grown past the basket, and the stool, and the wheelbarrow he had tried to put it in, and was now the size of a large hound, all legs and ribs and flopping wings that had never yet lifted it off the ground. It lived in the nursery pen behind the roost-yard, a walled paddock of trampled grass and a sagging lean-to. It met Wynn at the gate every morning in a wild scrabbling gallop that sent clods flying.

"Down," said Wynn. "Down. I said down."

The pup did not go down. It threw itself at his knees, and he went over into the straw with a sack of pears on top of him, and the pup stood on his chest and began to wash his face with a tongue the length of his forearm.

"You," said Wynn, with his eyes shut, "are a disgrace to your parents."

Its father had made a sound like that once, a long gusty rumble in the chest. Wynn laughed in spite of himself. He sat up, and the pup shoved its blunt head under his arm and leaned there, a warm solid weight that smelled of milk and fruit and a little of dog. He scratched the base of its ears, where the skin was thin, and it groaned.

Behind him, the nursery gate creaked.

"Don't do that," said Oren.

"I'm only scratching him."

"I know what you're doing." The old man leaned on the gate, his arms folded on the top bar. He did not come in. "I did it myself, the first year. You get a pup of your own, you think, I'll bring this one up right. You'll have it eating from your hand and sleeping across your feet, and the pup'll love you, and you'll love it, and that's as it should be."

"Then why..."

"Because it'll not be yours. You know that, Wynn. You've known it since you were sixteen. It's not for a handler to ride. It'll take a Brennan on its back or none at all. And when that day comes, it'll go to them like water going downhill, and it'll not so much as look round." Oren's eyes were cloudy and kind. "The first one I raised was a great red mother called Sorrel. She was the best Nyxen I ever knew, and she went to a boy of eleven in a velvet coat who had never raised a hand to her in his life, and she didn't turn her head. I stood in the yard for an hour afterward. I'm not saying don't love them. You'll love them whether I say so or no. I'm saying love them with your hands open."

Wynn looked at the pup. The pup looked at him.

"It's not decided who he goes to," said Wynn. "The king said there's no rider yet. Prince Rowan tried it as a boy and it went round him. The Princess Rhaenaya's too far south. He might be anybody's."

"He might."

"He might be a long time unbonded."

"He might," said Oren, and the corner of his mouth lifted. "Or he might, by some mischance, be yours."

"You said a handler can't."

"I said it is not for a handler to ride." The old man pushed off the gate. "I've never said what a Nyxen can't do. They're animals, boy, and animals are fond of whoever feeds them. Now get those pears off the ground before the little devil eats the lot."

* * *

At midmorning a boy came up the roost stair with a tally-stick and a satchel, and Wynn, who was greasing a bay-gate hinge, heard the voice before he saw the face.

"I have to count the crates," it said. "I'm sorry. I'm really very sorry. It isn't that anyone thinks you're stealing."

"Who sent you?"

"The counting-house. Master Jaspern. He said Prince Rowan wants the fruit cost by the week, not the month, and I'm to write it all in the big book, and I'm to be polite."

He was perhaps eleven. He had a thin, serious, inky face and brown hair cut straight across by a bowl, and he held the tally-stick before him like a lance. Wynn looked down at him from the ladder.

"What's your name?"

"Tobb, sir."

"I'm not a sir. I'm Wynn." He came down. "Fruit's in the cellar under the east bays. You'll want the lamp."

They counted. It took the better part of an hour, in the cold of the cellar, with the lamp on a hook and the boy ticking off notches while Wynn lifted the lids of the crates. Pears from the Merelands, packed in straw. Apples from the Vine Shore, in sacks. Dried plums, dried figs, honey in stoneware crocks. Tobb wrote it all down in a hand that was extraordinarily small and neat, and at the end of it he stood looking at the page with his lip caught in his teeth.

"That's a great many pears," he said.

"There's forty beasts."

"Eleven, sir. Your own book says eleven in the bays."

"The wild ones come in for the mash when it's cold." Wynn leaned on a crate. "Hart alone eats a bushel a day. A bushel and a half when the king's been flying."

"A bushel is eight pecks." Tobb's lips moved. "It's two marks and a half a week, for Hart. I do not think Prince Rowan will be pleased."

"Why not?"

"He doesn't like it when things cost more than they should." The boy's face was stricken. "But he always says, a thing that costs too much is a thing that tells you where to look. So I think he'll go and look."

"At what?"

"I don't know, sir. Pears, I suppose." Tobb shut the book. "I'm very sorry to have troubled you."

Wynn watched him climb the cellar stair into the light, a small figure with a satchel bigger than his torso, and thought that of all the people who worked in the castle, a counting-house boy and a roost handler had most in common. They were both employed to keep an eye on things bigger than themselves.

* * *

It was past noon when Hart lifted his head.

Wynn was on the ledge, mending a strap. He had not been looking. He caught it from the side of his eye, the way a man catches a fish rising: a tightening in the great body, a flicker along the neck. The tawny beast, who had lain like a stone since sunrise, was suddenly on his haunches with his ears straight up and his nostrils wide and the whole long length of him trembling.

"Oren," said Wynn.

"I see him."

"What is he..."

"Hush."

Wynn heard nothing. There was nothing to hear. The wind was out of the east and brought the faint smell of woodsmoke from the town, and a dog was barking at the kennels, and somewhere a very long way off a bell was ringing the hour. He looked at Hart. Hart's head had turned to the east and tilted a little, as a man tilts his ear toward a voice at the end of a corridor.

Then Hart roared.

The sulk and the long complaint of the morning were gone out of it. What came instead was enormous and ragged and unashamed, a child's whoop of pure joy stretched across the sky. The sound hit the cliff behind them and came back doubled. Briar jerked upright in her bay. In the yard below, every boy dropped his bucket.

Hart went off the ledge like a stone thrown upward. There was a crack of wings and a rush of air that flattened Wynn's hair against his skull, and when he looked up, Hart was already a hundred feet above the roost and climbing, tawny gold against the pale sky, and then he was a dot, and then he was nothing at all.

The strap had fallen from Wynn's hands. He stood on the ledge, staring at the empty sky.

"The pipe," said Oren, quietly.

"I heard nothing."

"Nobody hears it. It's pitched above the ear. Only his own Nyxen can hear a rider's pipe, from here to the sea." The old man was grinning, and his cloudy eyes were bright. "That's the call. I've seen it a hundred times and I've never seen it fail. They'll be in the middle of eating, or sleeping, or fighting, and the note comes, and that's the end of everything else. Look at the trough, lad."

Wynn looked. The mash sat in the bay, cold and untouched, with a skin of honey on the top.

"He did not even..."

"He didn't want it. He wanted the king." Oren shook his head slowly. "That's why they stay when they're told. A hound that comes to your whistle comes for the bone. A Nyxen that lies ten hours in his bay with his heart tearing, because the one he loves said wait, I've no word for that." Oren rubbed at his stubble. "Ask the Maester. He'll have a long one."

They did not see Hart again until the sun was low. Wynn spent the afternoon filling troughs and carrying water, and mending the strap, and taking the pup out for its walk along the wall, where it dragged at the lead and tried to eat a snail. Down in the yard the boys had gone back to their buckets, and the cliffs filled with long red light, and a flight of rooks went over, black against the gold.

The great beast came out of the west with the sunset behind him.

Wynn heard him before he saw him: a long, slow, contented rumble, rolling over the roost like thunder going home. Then the shape, huge and slow and low, swinging down out of the sky in a wide lazy spiral with his wings spread wide and his legs trailing. He landed in his own bay with a bump that shook the loft. He stood in the straw for a moment, swaying very slightly, and a smell of crushed pears rolled off him in a wave.

"By the roost," said Wynn. "He smells like a cider press."

"He's had a baron's basket." Oren's voice shook with laughter. "He'll have had a basket and half a hamper, if I know the king. And a lord of the Crownfold holding the pears out on his palm, shaking like a leaf."

"Will he eat his supper?"

"Of course he'll eat his supper. He's a Nyxen."

And Hart did. He put his great head into the trough and finished the cold mash in six enormous mouthfuls, with the honey skin and all, and then he turned and looked at Wynn with eyes that were soft and heavy and entirely contented, and lowered himself onto the straw with a groan like an old door.

Wynn went to him. He laid a hand on the huge warm shoulder, and Hart sighed.

"He's all right," Wynn said, and was embarrassed to find that his voice cracked.

"He was never anything else," said Oren.

* * *

The loft was Wynn's by turns, one night in three. It was a low, dim, hay-smelling room built across the rafters above the bays, with a cot, a lantern, a bucket, and a hatch in the floor through which a man could see straight down onto the back of any Nyxen in the east range. A handler slept up there so as to hear if one of the beasts was ill. Mostly it was a place for staring at the ceiling.

Wynn lay on his cot with the lantern turned low, and listened to the roost breathe. It was a sound he had stopped noticing years ago and noticed now: forty great chests, rising and falling in the dark, a slow, huge, shifting hush like the tide on a beach. Somewhere to the west Briar snored. In the nursery pen, behind the wall, the pup gave a thin yelp in its sleep.

He dozed. He woke because Hart's breath had changed.

It was a small thing. The beast beneath him had been asleep for hours, heavy and slack, with a faint whistle in his throat. Now the whistle had stopped. Wynn lay still. He could feel, through the boards, a faint steady thump, like a tail on straw.

Below him, someone coughed.

Wynn rolled onto his stomach and put his eye to the crack at the edge of the hatch. The lantern had burned low. Through the gap he could see the open face of the bay and a rectangle of stars, and in the starlight a man sitting on the stone with his back against the great tawny shoulder.

He wore no crown. He had a dark cloak over his shirt, and one arm was bound in linen and held against his chest, and with the other hand he held a pear. He bit into it. Hart's tail thumped again on the straw.

Wynn stopped breathing.

"I brought the pears," the king said, very softly. "I said I would. I'm sorry they are only the one."

Hart turned his huge head and set his nose gently against the man's shoulder.

"I know. I know. You'd have knocked him into the stream." The king chewed, and swallowed, and laughed, a small tired laugh with no breath behind it. "I was a poor king today. I carved a slice of hart and gave it to a man I think is lying to me, and I smiled while I did it. And then I raised my hand to my brother in the kennel yard, and he turned his horse to the cart."

The beast made a low sound in his chest.

"Yes. Rowan." The king was silent for a moment. "I'll do it. I'll do it tomorrow, or the day after. I only wish I knew what he'd say. I think he'd say, *Thank you, Your Grace,* and bow, and go away and be perfectly polite for the rest of his life. Which is worse than if he shouted."

Wynn closed his eyes.

He should not be here. He knew it. A handler heard things in the roost that he was not meant to hear: the Princess crying over a pup that died in her arms, a king's drunk confessions on the night his father died. It went with the work, like the smell of the straw. There was a rule about it, older than the castle, and it had no name because nobody had ever needed one. What was said to a Nyxen in the dark was said to a Nyxen. The roost did not hear it.

He opened his eyes. The king was lying back now with his head against the huge shoulder, the half-eaten pear still in his hand, looking up at the stars.

"I wonder sometimes what you hear," said Rodrik. "In the night. When you call to the north."

Hart said nothing. After a moment the king's hand came up and rested on the broad tawny brow.

"I know. It's all right. I'm here."

They stayed like that for a very long time. Wynn lay on the boards with his cheek against the dust and watched the starlight move across the king's face, and his arm went numb, and he did not shift. When at last the king got to his feet, with a hand on the Nyxen's neck and a stifled grunt of pain, he stood for a moment looking down at the bay.

"Thank you," he said, to no one in particular. "That was kind. Good night."

He went out into the dark. His footsteps crossed the yard, and a gate clanked, and then there was nothing.

Wynn lay still a long moment. Then he turned back on his cot and pulled the blanket to his chin, and listened. Below him the huge chest rose and fell. After a while, so softly that he was never certain afterward he had not dreamed it, Hart lifted his head and turned it toward the north, toward the black ridge of the Fold crags against the stars, and let out a single low, questioning note.

Nothing came back.

Hart waited. Then he sighed, and laid his head down on his forelimbs, and the whistle in his throat began again.

* * *

"You were up late," said Oren, in the grey of the morning, as Wynn came down the ladder with the bucket.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Hear anything?"

Wynn looked at him. The old man was sitting on an upturned crate, with his bad knee out in front of him and a cup of small beer in his hands. His cloudy eyes were fixed on the middle distance.

"No," said Wynn.

"Good lad." Oren drank. "I never do either."

The sun came up over the Crownfold. It lit the long line of the cliff, one bay at a time, and the troughs were filled, and the boys came running with their buckets. In the nursery, the pup began to shriek for its breakfast. Wynn picked up the barrow, and set his shoulder to the handle, and went down the stair toward the sound.
