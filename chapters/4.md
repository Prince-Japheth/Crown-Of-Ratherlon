ELYN

The first hard frost whitened the yard of Foldhall in the sixth month of Elyn's carrying, and with it came two messages from the Eastverge, which was one more than she wanted and one fewer than she needed.

The first was a rider from the relay posts on the kingsroad, a thin boy grey with road dirt who had changed horses at every post for eleven days and looked as though he had slept on each of them. The second was a raven, which had needed only three days for the same distance. The raven reached her first, because Maester Osmund carried it up to the solar himself, and he did that only for ravens that wore the brown wax of Eastgirt.

She read it standing, because sitting had become a matter of planning. The slip was no longer than her finger, and the hand on it was Elyra's gone small and crabbed, written by someone who had no table.

*The Redcut is lost. Simon lives and will keep the leg. Gareth is here. Send Hart.*

Elyn read it twice, as she read everything she did not want to be true. Then she read it a third time to be certain she had read the last two words and had not merely wished them there.

Maester Osmund waited by the door with his hands folded into his sleeves. He was an old man, stooped from sixty years of bending over vellum, and he had the gift of standing in a room so quietly that people said things before him that they would not have said before a wall.

"Where is the king?" she asked.

"At the roosts, Your Grace. Oren has a boy running to him now."

"Then send another boy to Prince Rowan and Prince Edwyn, and ask Ser Corren to fetch Ser Boremund from the tilt-yard." She laid the slip on the table and put her palm flat over it, as if it might lift in a draft. "And bring the rider to the kitchens, Maester, and see that he is fed before anyone asks him a question. He has been eleven days in the saddle. He will not remember his own name if we ask for it now."

"It will be done, Your Grace."

When he had gone she lowered herself into the cushioned chair that Rodrik had insisted upon, and that she hated. The child turned beneath her ribs and kicked twice, hard, as it did whenever she was frightened. She thought it unkind of the child to be so observant.

They came in the order she expected. Rowan arrived first with his ledger under his arm, because Rowan was always near a ledger and therefore always near the solar. Ser Boremund came next, still in his padded jack from the yard, smelling of horse and sweat, and stood against the wall with the careful stillness of a large man in a small room. Lord Orston came without being sent for. He was the Master of Whisperers, a small brown man in plain wool who had the misfortune to be remembered by everyone who tried to forget him, and he took the corner by the bookshelves as if it had been waiting for him.

Edwyn came last, in a sable-trimmed cloak that he had plainly not been wearing five minutes before, and he arranged himself against the window frame with a cup in his hand. She had never seen him arrive anywhere by accident.

The king came in behind him with frost still melting on his shoulders and the smell of Hart on his sleeves. He did not greet anyone. He crossed to the table, and Elyn slid the slip across to him without a word.

Rodrik read it once. He did not read it twice, because he had known the last two words before he reached them.

"Hart," he said quietly. "She wrote Hart. She did not write the king."

"She would not have asked for the king," Elyn said. "She knows what you would answer, and she knows what the lords would say, and she is a sister who has learned to ask for the thing that can be given."

"There is a longer letter," Rowan said, nodding toward the boy's leather satchel that Maester Osmund had left upon the sideboard. "It is ten days older than the raven, but it will tell us how the Redcut came to be lost."

Elyn broke the seal herself and read it aloud, because her voice was steadier than her hands.

*Brother,*

*The Ashen came down the dry road eleven days ago in numbers I will not put in a letter that can be taken from a dead rider. I will say only that Harrun's twenty and Simon's levy together are a wall that a child could walk around, and I have watched them walk around it. We hold the gully mouths by day. By night the Ashen go where we are not, and we cannot be everywhere.*

*Lark and I can scatter a band, but a scattered band is a band that returns. Gareth came down out of the cold on the ninth day, with Thorn beside Lark as though they had flown the same sky all their lives. He has said perhaps thirty words since, and twenty of them were about Thorn's feet. He is worth a hundred men and he knows it and does not say so, which is how you can tell.*

*Lord Alton Kelward has ridden at my side since the first ambush and has not once told me to fly home. I do not know what to call that, so I call it useful.*

*The Ashen do not fear what they can count. They feared Oak because no man could count him. If the king's shadow were seen over these gullies, they would remember what it is to be afraid, and I think the snow would catch them on their own side of the border. Think on it, Rodrik. I know what you will think. Think on it anyway.*

*Your sister, Elyra*

Elyn lowered the page. The room had gone quiet in the way a hall goes quiet when many men have reached the same conclusion and none wishes to be first to say it.

"Two thousand, by my count," Lord Orston said from his corner, in the soft voice that made men lean toward him. "Perhaps more, since the bands that fight are fewer than the bands that watch. They fly no banner that the Ashen king will own to. He swears to our envoys that he has no knowledge of them, and he weeps a little when he swears it. I have seen the letters."

"Then they are his men, and he is a liar," Ser Boremund said from the wall. He said it without heat, the way he might have remarked that a horse was lame.

"They are his men on the days that serve him and strangers on the days that do not," Rodrik said. "It is an old trick. It has never stopped being an effective one." He set his hand upon the slip. "Hart flies when I fly. That is the answer she knows I will give, and I will not pretend to be surprised by it."

"Your Grace." Rowan shut his ledger and held it against his chest with both arms, which was how he stood when he meant to say something he had rehearsed. "I would ask you to hear the other answer before you give that one."

"Give it," Rodrik said.

"The law gives the crown to the first child of the king's body. Yours is not yet born, and a child born to a dead king's widow in a realm at war is a child every lord will wish to hold in his own hand. If you fall in an eastern gully with Hart beneath you, the crown does not pass to a son or a daughter. It passes to your eldest brother, and your eldest brother is a man who has never ridden a Nyxen and never will." He said it evenly, with no flinch at all. "A realm with a king who cannot ride is a realm that has lost its sky. Every house in the Crownfold knows that. The Ashen know it. They knew it the day Oak flew north, and what they are testing in the Redcut is not Simon Kelward. It is the question of what Foldhall will do without a shadow."

"Rowan has given the king three excellent reasons to stay home," Edwyn said from the window, swirling his cup, "and I notice that all three end with Rowan on the throne."

"They end with a dead king, Edwyn," Rowan said without turning his head. "I have no wish for a chair that is warm only because my brother's body was in it last."

"Nor would the chair wish for you," Edwyn said pleasantly. "It is a chair that enjoys being sat in by men who shout."

"Enough," Rodrik said, but there was no anger in it. He looked at Rowan for a long moment, and Elyn saw him weigh what his brother had said, because it was true and Rodrik never pretended that true things were false. "You are right about the law. You are right about the sky. I will answer both."

"Then send me," Edwyn said. The languid tone had gone out of his voice, and in its place was something quicker and more honest. "Briar is rested and I am idle. If it is Hart that Elyra wants, Hart will carry me. He knows my scent. He is a Brennan beast and I am a Brennan rider, and the Ashen will not stop to ask which of us is sitting in the saddle."

"He will carry you as a man carries a stranger's child," Rodrik said. "With great care, and only as far as the door." He shook his head slowly. "Hart is older than I am, Edwyn. He has a hundred battles behind him, and he has opinions. I have seen him refuse my father's own orders in the First Ashen campaign when he judged them foolish. If you put him over the Redcut and ask him to dive on a band he does not like the look of, he will circle once, sit down on the nearest ridge, and wait for you to think better of it. I would not wish that on you. I would not wish it on a Kelward levy watching from below."

Edwyn opened his mouth, closed it, and inclined his head, which for Edwyn was the same as a bow.

"Then it is settled," Rowan said, very quietly. "It must be you."

"It must be me." Rodrik straightened, and the weight of the decision settled across his shoulders in a way that Elyn could see from across the room. "But not by running out of the gate at noon like a boy who has lost his wooden sword. If I go, I go as a king goes, with the realm put in order behind me so that every lord who watches understands that Foldhall did not bolt."

He looked at Elyn then, and she understood that he was not asking her leave but asking her counsel, which was harder.

She had thought carefully about what she would say. She had thought about it while she read the slip the third time, before anyone else had entered the room.

"You will go, Rodrik," she said. "I watched you read that slip. You did not look at the word Redcut, nor at the word Simon. You looked at the word Hart, and you did not move your eyes from it. The only question is how you go and what you leave behind."

"And if I stay?"

"If you stay, and Eastgirt falls, Lord Fenlow will say that the king preferred his supper to his border, and there will be forty men in this castle who will believe him. If you go, and you come back, you will be the king who ended it." She drew a breath, feeling the weight of the child press upward. "If you go and you do not come back, then Rowan is right, and I will have to be the queen who held the realm until a brother could be taught to keep it. I would prefer not to have that task. But I would prefer it to being the wife of a man who stayed, and who looked at the east every evening for the rest of his life."

Nobody spoke. Rowan studied the floor. Edwyn had stopped turning his cup.

"Rowan keeps the seal," Elyn continued, more steadily now that the worst had been said aloud. "He has always kept it, and the lords know he has kept it, and they will bring him their ledgers while you are gone and find that they are answered. I will hold court. The petitioners will come to me for the smaller matters, and I will send the larger ones to Rowan, and no lord will be able to say that the king's seat stands empty."

"And Edwyn?" Rodrik asked, and there was the smallest lift at the corner of his mouth.

"Edwyn sits at the feasts."

Edwyn made a sound of exaggerated woe. "You leave me with the feasts. Brother, I have more to offer than a lute."

"You have a great deal more," Elyn said, "and that is why you will sit at the feasts. Lord Fenlow talks when he drinks. So does Callenby, and so does every Vine Shore lord who has come to Foldhall to count the swords of the crown. They will tell you what they will not tell Lord Orston, because they will be trying to impress you." She met his dark, amused eyes. "You will listen to all of it and tell Rowan and me what it means. You are better at that than either of us."

For a moment Edwyn looked at her with a flicker of real surprise. Then he laughed, short and genuine, and lifted his cup toward her.

"I did not know I was being praised," he said. "I thought I was being assigned."

"You are being both," Elyn said. "It is the only way you ever learn to do as you are told."

Rodrik crossed the room and lowered himself to one knee beside her chair, stiffly, because it was not a thing a king did before his council. He put his hand over her belly, where the child had gone still again.

"Corren stays with you," he said, in a voice meant for her alone. "Not with me. If I need a sword between me and the Ashen I will have Hart. But I will not leave you with a guard who is half a stranger."

"Then Corren stays," she said. "And you will come back when the war lets you. I will not ask you to swear a day."

"I will swear you a day," Rodrik said. "The day you send for me, I come. A raven to Eastgirt, and Hart will make the distance in three days if I ask it of him, and he will want to, because he has grown fond of your pears."

"Then I will send for you on the day I must," she said, and she did not allow herself to think about what that day would be.

That evening in the great hall, with the long tables laid and the torches burning low in their sconces, Rodrik rose from the high chair and raised his cup. The hall went quiet as he did it. The lords of the Crownfold had the look of men who had come to hear something they could repeat elsewhere.

"My lords," Rodrik said. "The Eastverge has sent for help, and I am going to give it."

There was a low sound of surprise from the lower tables. Lord Fenlow, seated at the center with his green velvet sweating at the collar, put down his knife.

"I fly at dawn with Hart. The Master-at-Arms will follow on the kingsroad with five hundred Crownfold swords." Rodrik turned his head, and his grey-streaked beard caught the torchlight. "Lord Fenlow. At the tourney you offered me three hundred men, and I said the realm had strength enough. I was wrong to say it so carelessly. I accept your offer with thanks."

Fenlow's face did something complicated. Elyn watched it from the high table with quiet pleasure. He had offered three hundred men to be admired for the offer, and he had been handed the bill.

"Your Grace is gracious," Fenlow said, rising and bowing. "House Fenlow is honored."

"Ser Boremund will lead them with the rest," Rodrik went on, in the same even voice. "And since your nephew has the makings of a true knight, as you told me, I think he should go as Ser Boremund's squire and learn what a real sword weighs. A lad who fights so well with a stick ought to see what it does against a man who does not give up the stick."

A ripple of laughter passed through the lower tables, and in the middle of it Fenlow's smile went very thin. The Fenlow squire with the rust-colored hair sat a few places down with his mouth half open. Elyn looked at Rowan. Rowan was studying his ledger, but the corner of his mouth had moved, which for Rowan was the same as a cheer.

Across the hall, Elmrick's second daughter was fanning herself and watching the king with bright, calculating eyes. A moment later she let her gaze drift to Rowan, and then to Edwyn, and Elyn saw her weigh the two of them against each other and put her fan down as though she had lost interest in both.

* * *

The roosts were white with rime when Elyn came down to them at dawn, wrapped in three cloaks and leaning on Ser Corren's arm with a grace she did not feel.

The breath of every Nyxen in the bays hung in the cold like smoke from a banked fire. Hart stood in his open cliff-bay with his wings half-spread, and the frost lay thick across the stone at his feet, melting in a black ring wherever his heat touched it. Oren had the saddle up and the straps laid out, and he checked each buckle twice with the stooped patience of a man who had checked them on another king's beast for twenty years. He was a Foldhall man with mash stains on his sleeves that were older than Rodrik's crown, and he did not look at Elyn when she arrived, because he knew that if he did she would see that his eyes were wet.

Rodrik stood with one hand flat against Hart's shoulder. He was in his riding leathers with the green cloak pinned over them and the heavy gold circlet left behind in the solar, so that his ash-charcoal hair moved in the wind like a banner that did not belong to any house. He looked up when he heard her step.

"You should be in bed," he said.

"I have been in bed for a month," Elyn said. "It is a very dull place to be."

"Hart will be gone by the time your tea is warm."

"Then I will drink it cold and be glad to have seen him go." She let go of Corren's arm and walked the last steps alone, and Rodrik came to meet her, and they stood close together with the Nyxen breathing above them.

He did not make a speech. He took her face in both his calloused hands and kissed her forehead, and then her mouth, and then he laid his forehead against hers and said something low in Aurouvian. She did not know the whole of it, for she had only a few words of the family's private tongue, but she knew the last word, which was her own name.

"I will send ravens every third day," he said at last, in plain speech. "Eastgirt has a loft and Simon keeps good birds."

"I will answer every one."

"Keep the ribbons in order," he said, and there was the ghost of the old laugh behind it. "And do not let Edwyn sing at the feast that announces the birth. I will not have a song about my son that rhymes with hearth."

"It rhymes with earth," Elyn said. "He will find something worse."

Oren stepped forward then and cleared his throat, and Rodrik turned to him with the kind of gratitude a man shows to a friend who has interrupted a goodbye at the right moment. Elyn watched her husband swing himself up into the saddle with the ease of long practice, buckling the lines over his thighs, and she watched Hart settle under the weight as though a stone had been set down in its proper place.

Hart raised his broad head and called once, a deep, rolling note that she felt through the soles of her boots. It was not the morning complaint she knew. It was longer, and it ended on a rising tone, and when it was done the Nyxen held his head tilted to the north, toward the high crags above the roosts, and waited.

Nothing answered. The crags stood white and empty against the paling sky.

"He does that at dusk as well," Oren said quietly, at her shoulder. "Has done since the old king died. Calls north, and listens, and waits after, as if the answer might be slow in coming. I stopped asking him what for a long while ago."

Hart lowered his head and looked at the man in the saddle. Rodrik said something quiet that the wind took away. Then the great wings opened, and the sound they made was like a sail filling in a gale, and the ring of melted frost on the stone flashed and scattered. Hart went up in three strokes, and the downdraft sent Elyn's cloak snapping back from her shoulders and Ser Corren's hand to her elbow.

She watched until he was a dark shape against the east, and then a dot, and then a thing she was not certain she still saw.

"Your Grace," Ser Corren said gruffly. "You will catch a chill."

"In a moment," Elyn said.

She stood a moment longer than she should have. Then she let him lead her back up the stone stairs to the wall-walk, where the wind was cold and clean and smelled of distant snow. Rowan was already there, standing alone by a crenel with his ledger shut under his arm. He had come up to watch, and he had not wished to be seen watching.

"Your Grace," he said, bowing precisely the depth that was due.

"Rowan."

"You will have petitions from the lower town this morning. I have sent the smaller matters to the lesser hall. The Quenby boundary dispute, the Callenby widow's rent, the matter of the hired huntsmen. I thought you would prefer them to the tariff rolls."

"That was thoughtful," Elyn said, and meant it.

"The king has asked me to keep the seal," Rowan said, his eyes on the road where the last of the dust had settled. "I have always kept it. It is not a change."

"No," Elyn agreed. "It is not."

She thought of all the times she had meant to ask Rodrik why Rowan held no title for the work he did. The seat of the Hand stood empty and Rowan filled its labors without its name, and each time she had raised it, Rodrik had answered that a king could not be his own Hand, and then he had changed the subject in the tone he used for matters he would rather not hold. She had let it go. She was not certain now that she should have.

Rowan inclined his head once more, and went down the stairs with his ledger, and did not look back.

* * *

The court of the queen sat in the lesser hall on the first morning of every week, and it had grown larger in the ten weeks since the king flew east.

She had expected it. Petitioners who would have taken their quarrels to Rodrik came now to Elyn, and petitioners who would have taken them to Rowan came to her first, to see whether she would hear them. Two dozen people stood in the aisle that morning, in wet boots and heavy cloaks, and the steam of their breath mingled with the smoke of the hall fire. She sat in the carved chair on the low dais with a cushion at her back and Ser Corren a pace to her left, and Rowan at a small table below her with the seal and a stack of vellum, which was as near as he could be without sitting beside her.

It was the eighth month. Her belly had grown so large that she had given up pretending to hide it under her gowns, and the lords' wives had stopped pretending not to look.

The first case was Quenby's boundary, which she settled in a quarter hour by sending the claimants to walk the line with a surveyor and a bucket of lime. The second was the Callenby widow, whose husband's brother had been collecting her rents and keeping the coin. Elyn held the man in the hall for an hour before she spoke to him, so that he would have time to consider what he had done.

The third was a knight.

He was a minor knight of the Merelands, a lean man in a cloak too thin for the season, named Ser Garrick Orme, and he had come to Foldhall to demand a trial by combat against another knight who had called him a coward in a tavern on the river stairs. The other knight, a stout Crownfold man named Ser Lyle, stood across the aisle with his arms folded and a split lip that had not yet healed.

"He said it before witnesses, Your Grace," Ser Garrick said. His voice was tight, but he kept it low. "He said I ran at the Redcut watch-post two summers ago. I did not run. I was sent for reinforcements, and I rode two days to bring them. The men who held that post know it. I will not have it said."

"I said he ran because that was the tale told at my table," Ser Lyle said. "I did not know it was false."

"You said it at the top of your voice."

"I was drinking."

"That is an explanation and not a defense," Elyn said. She looked from one to the other. The hall had gone quiet, and she could feel the whole room leaning toward her, because trials by combat were rare in the Crownfold and every man present wished to see whether the queen would grant one.

She would not. A dead knight would settle nothing and would make a widow, and the king was in the east fighting men who did not care what Crownfold knights thought of each other.

"Ser Garrick," she said. "If I grant this combat, one of you dies. If it is you, the world believes you a coward and a fool who could not hold a sword. If it is Ser Lyle, the world believes you a murderer who could not bear a tavern insult. Neither of those is the truth, and neither is what you came here for."

"Your Grace," Rowan said, from his table below. "The law permits the demand. The queen may refuse it, but she should say on what ground."

She had known he would speak. The law did permit it, and he would not let her refuse without stating the reason aloud, so that no one could say afterward that the queen had ruled from feeling. It was a small thing and a correct one, and she was not certain whether he had done it to help her or to show the hall that he knew the law better than she did.

"On the ground that a knight's honor is his word, Prince Rowan, and this matter has a witness who can give it," Elyn said. She looked down the aisle. "Who held the Redcut watch-post two summers ago?"

A voice spoke from the back of the hall. "I did, Your Grace." It was an old sergeant with a grey moustache, who had evidently come in with the petitioners for the Callenby widow and stayed to watch. "Ser Garrick rode for reinforcements by the captain's order. I carried the captain's seal. I can swear to it."

"Then you will swear it," Elyn said, "before the Maester, and he will write it down, and Ser Lyle will repeat the truth in the same tavern where he spread the lie, at the same hour, and he will buy every man in the house a cup. That is my judgment."

Ser Lyle's face went through several colors. He looked at the sergeant, then at Ser Garrick, and finally bowed stiffly. "It will cost me a week's pay, Your Grace."

"It will cost you less than a funeral," Elyn said.

There was a burst of laughter from the lower benches, and Ser Garrick bowed very low, like a man who had come ready to die and been handed back his life. Below her, Rowan wrote the judgment in his small, upright hand without lifting his eyes, and she could not tell from his face whether he approved.

It was the last case before noon. She was rising slowly, one hand on Ser Corren's arm and the other pressed against the small of her back, when the gate-warden's boy burst into the hall, flushed to the ears.

"Your Grace! A litter, from the south road. It carries the Norvalen wolf and a banner of twelve riders, and the lady says to tell you that she is cold and that she refuses to say so twice."

Elyn laughed, for the first time in a week. "Bring her in. And bring hot wine, and do not let anyone tell her it is too early in the day."

* * *

Rhaenaya Brennan, Lady of Frostholt by marriage and fourth-born of Aldren VIII's children, came into the lesser hall on the arm of a Norvalen knight, with a bundle of white fox fur held against her chest and snow still melting on her shoulders.

She was a broad-faced woman with Rodrik's brown eyes in a softer setting, and hair the warm chestnut of her mother's line, worn braided and pinned against the cold. Where the firelight touched it, a faint metallic burnish showed over the brown, the Cinder appearing as a quality rather than a color. She moved with the heavy, deliberate walk of someone who had spent years in rooms where the floors were always cold, and when she saw Elyn she stopped, looked her up and down, and put a hand to her mouth.

"By the Nyxens," she said. "Rodrik wrote that you were large. He did not write that you were the size of a siege tower."

"He is a man and did not know the word," Elyn said. She crossed the floor as quickly as she could and took Rhaenaya's hands, and the cold of them went through her gloves. "You came by the south road in the dead of the frost?"

"Osric said Foldhall was the warmest place in the realm for a lying-in, and that the Frostholt midwives were all old and superstitious." Rhaenaya's mouth twisted. "He also said that he would sooner I were at my brother's hearth than at his own while he watches the reavers on the ice. I read that as a polite way of saying he was busy." She lifted the bundle in her arms. "And I have brought someone to meet you."

The fur parted. Inside it lay a baby of six months, round-cheeked and red with cold, with a thatch of dark brown hair that shone faintly silver in the light from the hall windows. He was glaring at the ceiling with an expression of profound suspicion.

"This is Alaric," Rhaenaya said. "He takes after his father. He thinks everything is an enemy until it gives him something to eat."

"He is beautiful," Elyn said, and was surprised to find that she meant it with her whole body. The child in her own belly rolled over as though to see the competition.

"He is loud," Rhaenaya said. "But yes. Come, sit, before you fall over. I have ridden sixteen days and I am not strong enough to catch a queen."

They sat by the fire in Elyn's solar with a pitcher of hot spiced wine between them and Alaric lying on a fur in front of the hearth, and Rhaenaya told her about the south. She was a good storyteller, dry and quick, with none of Edwyn's showmanship and all of his eye for what was funny. She described Frostholt as a place where the walls sweated ice and the servants wore so many layers that they could not be told apart, where the cold was spoken of as a kindness because it kept the smell of one's neighbors down. She made Elyn laugh until the child kicked.

She did not say how lonely she had been. Elyn saw it anyway, in the way Rhaenaya kept talking after the stories ran out and in the way she watched the door as though someone familiar might come through it.

"Osric is a good man," Rhaenaya said later, when the wine had warmed them both. "He is kind to me. He is dutiful. He does not shout." She turned her cup in her hands. "But it is a match of houses, Elyn, and there is a great deal of house in it. I am glad Rodrik married for the other reason."

"He did," Elyn said quietly.

"I know." Rhaenaya smiled. "Everyone knows. It is the most irritating thing about the two of you."

It snowed that night, and again the next day, and the court became a pleasant, muffled place where people moved from fire to fire in thick cloaks and complained about the weather in voices too cheerful to be believed. Rodrik's ravens arrived every third day as he had promised. Elyn read each of them standing by the window in the solar, in the pale light, and she learned to read them the way she had once read petitions, for what was not said.

He wrote of the road to Eastgirt, of Simon's leg, of Hart's appetite for pears. He wrote that Elyra had sent him a most rude letter about his tardiness and that Gareth had said one thing to him in greeting, which was "You are heavier," and had then gone to see to Thorn. He did not write a single word about the war, and by the fourth raven Elyn understood that it was going badly enough that he did not want her to know.

On the ninth day after Rhaenaya's arrival, it stopped snowing, and Edwyn announced that the pup was old enough to be shown to the family.

"Lark's pup," he said at breakfast, spreading honey on his bread with the air of a man conferring a blessing. "The one she dropped in the spring. Sired by Hart, which means the handlers have been praying over it for months, and Oren says it has been asking for company. There is an old custom, Elyn. A pup of the royal blood is walked among the family before it is turned loose, and the eldest who has no Nyxen of his own is offered first."

"That would be Rowan," Rhaenaya said, and turned her head.

Rowan, at the end of the table with a ledger propped open against the honey pot, did not look up. "It would."

"Will you try?" Elyn asked him.

"No," Rowan said.

Edwyn made a noise of delighted reproach. "You have not even heard the terms."

"I tried at nine, and again at ten, and every pup in the roost walked around me as though I were a puddle," Rowan said. He turned a page. "I have a kingdom's arithmetic to keep, and I am too occupied with ledgers to start training a beast I do not have the scent for. The handlers know it. The Maester knows it. The pup knows it, and it has never met me." He looked up at last, his face quite calm. "You are welcome to offer it the courtesy, Edwyn. It will be wasted, but the gesture will do you credit."

"Brother, I am wounded," Edwyn said, pressing a hand to his heart. "You make it sound as though I wanted it for myself."

"You already have Briar. You want it for the story." Rowan returned to his page. "Rhaenaya should try. She has not seen it since it was born."

"I will," said Rhaenaya, and her voice was light, but Elyn saw her hand tighten on the stem of her cup.

They went out to the gardens in the thin noon sun, with the snow packed hard on the paths and Nyxens dozing in every patch of light. Elyn went slowly, with Ser Corren at her side, and Rhaenaya walked ahead with Alaric wrapped in fur against her chest. Two of Edwyn's knights followed at a respectful distance and pretended not to be watching.

The pup was asleep under a bare pear tree with its wings folded over its nose. It was about the size of a large hound, all legs and ears and long feet, its tawny-brown fur still softer than a grown beast's, and it had evidently fallen asleep in the middle of a game, for there was a half-chewed pear near its paw and a scatter of fruit pulp on the snow. When it heard them coming, it lifted one eyelid and considered them with the deep, serious interest of a creature that had decided on its opinions and had not yet been given the opportunity to state them.

Rhaenaya stopped ten paces off and lowered herself to one knee in the snow. She held out her free hand, palm down, in the way the handlers did.

"Come, little one," she said. "I am not going to hurt you. Come and see."

The pup rose. It was clumsy, all wing and shoulder, and it covered the distance in two bounding lurches and stopped in front of her with its nose quivering. It sniffed her outstretched hand, slowly and thoroughly. Then it sniffed her sleeve, and her hair, and the fur that covered the baby.

Then it turned around, walked three paces, and sat down facing the other direction.

"Well," Rhaenaya said, after a moment. She laughed, and it came out a little cracked. "I did tell Osric. I told him at the wedding. I said, I have never had the smell for it, it is not in me." She stood, brushing snow from her knees. "It is not even an insult, is it? It is a pup. It has no opinion of me at all."

"It has an opinion of the pear," Elyn said gently.

"It has a very strong opinion of the pear." Rhaenaya straightened her shoulders and lifted Alaric from the fur so that he faced outward, his round cheeks pink and his suspicious eyes fixed on the snow. "Here. Try him. I know it is foolish. I have a cousin on the Kelward side whose eldest son carries the scent, and everyone said it would never come to me, and look at this child. It might have come to him instead."

She knelt again with the baby in her arms and held him toward the pup.

The pup looked. It turned its broad head and regarded the small, glaring face for the space of four breaths. Then it yawned, enormously, showing a pink mouth and small white teeth, and lay down on the trampled snow with its chin on its paws.

Alaric made a disgusted noise and began to cry.

"He has his father's gift for judging a creature," Rhaenaya said, and pressed her face into the top of his head. She stayed there for some moments with her eyes closed. "Come, Alaric. We will find you something you like."

Elyn had not meant to do it. She had come out to the garden with no purpose except to walk, and the pup was nothing to her, only a clumsy creature with a pear. But the pup lifted its head as she passed, and it rose again and padded up to her, and pushed its cold, damp nose against the swell of her belly with a great snuffling breath.

She stood perfectly still. The child turned beneath the pressure, and she felt the small movement like a greeting.

"Your Grace," said Ser Corren, very low.

The pup drew back and sneezed fruit pulp across her slippers. Then it yawned a second time, turned, and bounded off after a sparrow, its wings flapping uselessly and its ears flat with purpose.

"That," Edwyn said, from the path behind them, "is the most discouraging thing I have ever seen a Nyxen do. It has offered the queen no opinion at all."

"It has offered her a sneeze," said Rhaenaya, who was laughing now, and Elyn found that she was laughing too.
