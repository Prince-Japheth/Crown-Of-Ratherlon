ELYRA

The Redcut had been a river once. Ages of spring flood had sawn it through the red sandstone to the depth of five tall men, and then the water had found some other road to the sea and left the cut behind: a dry gully with walls the color of old blood and a floor of cracked clay that rang under a bront's hoof like a struck pot. At its mouth the gully opened onto the scrubland, six wagons wide. Eighty paces behind that mouth it narrowed to two, and the narrow place was the reason the Kelwards had built a watch-stone on the north wall before there was a Kelward lord of Eastgirt to build it.

Elyra sat on the parapet of that watch-stone with her boots hanging over a drop of forty feet and a heel of black bread in her fist, and watched the east burn.

It was a small fire, as the Ashen fires went. A smudge of orange at the foot of the sky, perhaps four miles off, with a grey thread of smoke leaning away from it in the evening wind. A grain-cart, she thought, or a drover's hut. On the first day she had flown to every one of them. On the ninth she could have told the hour by the smoke, because they came as regularly as the bells at Foldhall, one at dusk, one at midnight, one an hour before dawn, each in a different place and each a little too far from the last to be reached before the raiders were gone.

"That is the third cart this week," said Alton Kelward, behind her. "My brother's steward has stopped counting them. He says it is bad for his digestion."

"The cart is not what they are burning," said Elyra. She did not turn. "They are burning my sleep."

He came and leaned his forearms on the parapet beside her. He had come up the stair without a sound, as he did everything, and he carried two horn cups of the thin sour ale that the holdfast brewed from barley and spite. He gave her one. In the red light his face was all bone and shadow, with the dark hair tied back from it in the leather thong he never seemed to replace, and he looked, as he always looked at dusk, like a man doing sums in his head.

"They have not come for the Redcut," he said. "Not yet. They come for us to look at the Redcut while they do something else."

"You have said so every night since the second."

"And every night I have been right, and every night we have all looked at the fire." He drank. "I do not say it to be clever, Princess. I say it because it is the only thing I know. They send ten men to burn a cart. We send forty to chase them. The forty find a burned cart and a trail that goes in three directions, and by the time the forty come home half sick with riding, the ten have been replaced by a hundred somewhere that we are not."

"Then we should stop chasing."

"Then they will burn Eastgirt's outlying farms until the smallfolk come to my brother's gate with their children and ask what the crown is for." He smiled slightly. It did not warm him. "There is a very good reason why the Ashen do not fight as we do. They have studied us."

Elyra tore the bread in half and put the larger piece in her pocket, for Lark, who would not eat the Eastgirt mash and would eat anything that had come from Foldhall's ovens. Below her in the yard the beast lay on the baked earth with her chin on her forelimbs and her ears turned toward the watch-stone, tawny-brown in the red light, as patient as a hound waiting at a kitchen door. A cluster of Kelward grooms stood at a respectful distance with their bront-goads in their hands, and every one of them was pretending not to look. They had been pretending for nine days. The bronts in the paddock beyond had stopped screaming at the smell of her on the third.

"I wish," said Elyra, "that you would let me kill something."

"I wish that too. I am only trying to decide what." Alton finished his ale. "Be careful what you wish. My brother wishes the same, and it is why he is in the yard at this hour, shouting at a farrier."

It was true. She could hear him: a hoarse bellow from the forge, somewhere beneath the stair, rising and falling in the dry air like a bront in rut. Simon Kelward was not a man who knew how to wait. He knew how to hold, which was a different thing, and the nine days had taught her that the difference was most of what separated a good lord from a dead one.

She took her heel of bread out of her pocket, looked at it, and was about to eat it herself when Lark's head came up.

It came up all at once, as a dog's does at a footstep, and the ears that had been turned toward the watch-stone swung together to the south. A low sound went through the yard, a rolling, questioning thrum that Elyra felt in the stone beneath her. Every groom in the yard stepped back.

"What is it?" Alton had straightened. "Is it a raid?"

"No." She was on her feet on the parapet without knowing how she had come to be there, balanced like a girl on a garden wall, and she shaded her eyes against the last of the sun. "It is not a raid. She is not afraid. She is..."

A speck came out of the south, very low, moving fast along the line of the dry road. It was too large for a bird and too small for a thing that had been seen across a plain, and it flew with the slow, heavy, certain beat of a Nyxen that had been a long time in the air and had decided to be rid of it. She watched it come. She saw the grey of the hide, and the thick winter coat that was far too heavy for the Eastverge in late summer, and the long rider's shape bent forward in the saddle, and she let out a breath that was half a laugh.

"That," said Elyra, "is my brother."

* * *

Thorn came down in the yard of the Redcut with the awkward, flat-footed lurch of a beast that had spent his strength three days before and was coming down on will alone. His pads left long, dragging scars in the baked earth. He stood with his wings half-spread and his head hanging, a big, grey, rough-coated animal with ice still caked in the fur of his belly, and Lark went to him across the yard and put her nose beneath his jaw. They stood like that for a moment, the two long heads together. Then Lark shoved him, hard, in the shoulder, in a way that plainly meant *you took your time,* and Thorn grunted and shoved back.

Gareth was out of the saddle before the grooms could come. He was a lean young man in the fur-trimmed leathers of the Southfrost, with the sea-wind still in his cheeks and a short, rough beard that did not suit him, and his hair, a deep warm brown, caught the last of the light as he turned and flashed silver for an instant, like a fish rising. It was the only thing about him that said *Brennan.* The rest was a long, quiet, weathered face and a pair of eyes that went first to Thorn's feet, and only then to his sister.

"Elyra," he said.

"Gareth." She had come down the stair three steps at a time and crossed the yard at a run, and she caught him in a hug that lifted his boots from the clay. He bore it. He was not a man who returned embraces, but he did not step out of them either, and after a moment his hand came up and rested on her back with a stiff, unpracticed pat. "You smell of fish."

"Seal," said Gareth. "The Norvalen eat it. I have not been near a bath since Frostholt." He set her down. "Is there tallow?"

"Is there what?"

"Tallow. Mutton fat, goose grease, anything. His pads have split. The Icewide roads are salt and ice, and the salt gets into the cracks, and I have been rubbing them with seal oil these four days, but it is not enough." He looked back at Thorn, who was standing on three feet and holding the fourth off the ground with an air of great injury. "He has not complained once. I would like to be able to say the same of myself."

It was, Elyra thought, the longest speech she had heard him make in a year. She looked at his face, and at the cracks in his lips, and at the beast with his head hung low, and her laugh went suddenly shaky.

"You have come a very long way."

"You wrote that it was needed."

"I wrote a raven that said *come.* I did not think you would fly three days on the strength of one word."

"It was a good word," said Gareth. He took off a glove and rubbed the heel of his hand against his eye. "I was a fortnight on the ice with Osric's patrols, and nothing had happened in all that time but weather. I had begun to wonder what I was for."

A Kelward groom had appeared at his elbow with a pot of rendered fat and an expression of mingled awe and terror. Gareth took it, said something low to the man that made him bob his head, and knelt at Thorn's forefoot to work the grease into the split pads with his bare thumbs. The big grey beast sighed, long and shuddering, and leaned his weight onto the shoulder of his rider.

Alton Kelward stood a little apart, watching with his hands behind him. When Gareth finally looked up, the Kelward bowed.

"Prince Gareth. I am Alton, the duke's brother. You are very welcome at the Redcut."

"I know who you are," said Gareth. "Elyra writes about you."

Elyra, who had been bending to inspect Thorn's other foot, straightened so fast that her neck cracked.

"I wrote *twice,*" she said. "And only to say that you rode well."

"Four times," said Gareth, and returned to his grease. "And that you were useful."

There was a short silence. Alton's face did not change. But Elyra, who had spent nine days beside him on a wall, watched a muscle move in his jaw, and saw him look for a moment out at the burning dusk with the particular concentration of a man who has been told a thing and does not intend to be seen to have heard it.

"I am glad to be of use," said Alton gravely.

Simon Kelward arrived in the yard at that moment, red-faced and limping, with his iron-streaked beard still bristling from the forge. He was a broad, scarred, short-tempered man of five and thirty, with the same dark hair as his brother going rough at the temples, and he pulled up so short at the sight of the second Nyxen that his bodyguard walked into him.

"Two," he said. "Elyra, I did not dare to ask for two."

"This is Prince Gareth," said Elyra. "He is my brother, and your cousin, and he has been flying since dawn."

"Cousin Gareth." Simon came forward and gripped his forearm in a hand like a smith's tongs. For a moment the tension went out of his shoulders altogether, so that he looked like a much younger man. "I will not insult you with a speech. I have nine hundred bronts and three hundred men-at-arms in this holdfast and a front that is being unpicked a thread at a time. I asked for the king's shadow. I received my cousin, who has been a rock, and I thank her for it. And now I receive you. It is more than I had a right to hope."

"It is not enough," said Gareth, without looking up. "You will want more, and the king will send it. But it is a beginning." He bent over Thorn's foot. "Is there a place in this holdfast where a beast can sleep without being stared at?"

"There is a long paddock behind the farrier's," said Simon. "It has not held a bront in years. I will have it cleared."

"I would take it as a kindness."

"Prince Gareth." The Duke of Eastgirt frowned at the grey beast's feet. "Is that what I think it is? Salt rot?"

"It is cold and salt, and he will mend," said Gareth. "He does not mend faster for being discussed."

Elyra laughed. She could not help it. She put an arm through her brother's and held on, and after a moment she felt him lean, just slightly, into her shoulder, with the same helpless, grudging weight that Thorn had put on him, and understood that it had been a very long fortnight on the ice.

* * *

The Ashen were not stupid. That was what Elyra learned in the ten days that followed.

She learned it first from the ropes. They found them on the fourth morning after Gareth came, in a hollow a mile from the Redcut where a band of raiders had camped and left in a hurry: three neat coils of hemp as thick as her wrist, each ending in a fist-sized stone sewn into a leather cradle, and each coil laid down beside the next as a rope-maker lays his stock. Alton picked one up and turned it over in his scarred hands.

"Spliced," he said. "By someone who knew what he was doing. And the stone is not a river stone. It has been dressed." He pressed the leather with his thumb. "Wet-sewn, so that it will shrink when it dries and hold the stone fast. This is not a thing a raider makes on the road. It is made in a workshop."

"What is it for?"

"I should like to know." He looked up at her. "You are the one who reads the old books, Princess. Are there rope-casters in them?"

She did not need long. She had spent three winters as a girl with a maester who thought that a princess ought to know the history of the beast she meant to ride, and he had made her copy out the hunting chapters in her own hand, so that she should learn what her grandfathers' grandfathers had been afraid of. She remembered the plates. The old hunters had gone out in lines of fifty, each man with a weighted rope, and they had thrown them from hand to hand until a loop closed about a Nyxen's head, and then all fifty had hauled together. It was not to kill the beast. It was to close the throat before the roar could come.

"They are for choking," she said, and her mouth was dry. "They throw them from man to man until the loop closes about the head. If it tightens in time, the beast cannot roar."

"Ah," said Alton softly. He put the rope down.

"The hunters used to say that a Nyxen thrown by fifty men would fling a dozen of them over the trees. They needed a hundred, in the end. A hundred and a great many dead." She looked at the stone in its leather cradle. "It is two hundred years since anyone tried it. It is in a book. *Someone in the Ashlands has been reading our books.*"

"Or they have long memories, and we have short ones," said Alton. "It is the same thing, in the end."

Gareth had said nothing. He had been standing a little apart, with one hand resting on Thorn's flank. Now he walked to the edge of the hollow and looked east, along the line of the dry road, and when he spoke it was in the soft, flat voice he used when he had made up his mind and did not expect to be asked about it.

"They will use these on the day they mean to fight."

"Which will be when?"

"When they are ready. They have not tested us yet. These nine days have been to see where the Redcut is weakest, and when its Nyxens are tired, and how many men can be drawn out to chase a fire." He did not turn. "I have hunted the reaver edge of the Icewide for two winters. It is the same. They do not fight until they have found the thing that frightens you, and then they fight that."

"And what frightens us?" said Elyra.

"What always frightens a rider." Gareth patted Thorn's shoulder. "That we cannot be in two places."

She thought about it all that day, flying the long loops of the Redcut's approaches with Lark's heavy wings beating steady beneath her and Thorn a quarter-mile to the north, a grey shape against the red. She thought about it in the evenings, in the long paddock behind the farrier's where Gareth sat on an upturned bucket and worked grease into Thorn's feet while she lay in the straw beside Lark and counted the stars. She did not talk about it. She had learned, in the years since she was fifteen, that talking was what people did to put off the thing they were afraid of, and that her brother did not do it, and that she liked him the better.

On the tenth night she dreamed of ropes, and woke before dawn to the sound of horns.

* * *

They were not Kelward horns. They were a deeper note, a long, ragged, braying call that rose from the east and swelled until it filled the whole sky, and the grooms were already running in the yard when she came out of the straw with her boots in her hand. Lark was on her feet, ears flat, tail lashing. She did not cry out. She stood with her head up and her whole body drawn tight, and when Elyra put a hand against her ribs she could feel the great chest heaving beneath the tawny fur.

"Easy, girl," said Elyra. "Easy. We have to dress."

It was the work of a quarter of an hour, which was too long. She buckled the saddle straps, and the lines across her thighs, and pushed the wax into her ears as every rider did, soft yellow lumps that she kneaded between her fingers and pressed home with her thumbs. She was bareheaded, because there was no helm made that would fit under a rider's wax and her braid. She wore her father's sword, and the leather jerkin that Rodrik had given her on her nameday, and she felt not afraid, which surprised her, but light, as she had felt before the first jump of a race.

Gareth was already in the saddle when she reached the yard. He had his furs off, and his jaw set, and he did not speak. He lifted one hand in greeting and then pointed east.

The sun had not risen. But the sky above the Redcut had gone the color of tin, and she could see the whole length of the scrubland spread out below the watch-stone, grey and cold, with the long, low swell of the plain running away into the mist.

And across it, from the Redcut's mouth to the last smudge of horizon, the Ashen.

She had not thought there could be so many. She had seen the bands of ten and twenty that came in the night with their torches, and she had thought of them, without ever saying it, as the whole of what there was. These were not bands. They were an army, moving across the plain in blocks as clean and square as chess pieces: ranks of men in dull white armor lashed from bone and sinew, with their heads bare and their shields up and their long pikes slanted forward, a hedge of white thorn half a mile wide, creeping toward the Redcut at the pace of a walking man. Behind the pikes came archers, hundreds of them. Behind the archers came something that she could not make out, a slow dark mass that moved on wheels.

The horns brayed again. The thorn hedge did not hasten.

"It is the pikes," said Alton, at her stirrup. He had come up the yard on foot, in his mail, with a helm under his arm and a face as pale as the plaster on the watch-stone wall. "Do you see what they have brought? It is nothing that a bront can charge. A bront breaks horses and shield-walls. It will not break a hedge of pikes set against the ground. Twelve good ranks of them, and they would turn my brother's whole line into meat."

"Then we make them fall back."

"You can?"

She looked at the hedge of white. She looked at the sky. In the saddle between her knees, Lark was quivering, a long, fine, steady tremor that ran from her shoulders to her tail.

"I can try," said Elyra.

* * *

They took the sky together, she and Gareth, and she felt Lark's wings strike the ground behind her with a force that nearly threw her from the saddle. The yard fell away. The watch-stone spun beneath her left wing, and the red walls of the Redcut rushed past, and the cold air came into her face like a slap. She leaned forward along the beast's neck, feeling the heave of the great shoulders beneath her, and she did not look at the men on the walls, who were shouting, or at the Kelward bront-line drawn up behind the gully mouth, a long dark wedge of horn and russet hide stamping and snorting in the dawn.

Thorn rose a hundred feet to her north, steady and grey. Gareth lifted a hand.

*Now,* it said.

She turned Lark with a shift of her weight. The beast dropped her left wing and wheeled, and for a long, falling moment they hung above the line of pikes with the whole white hedge spread out beneath them, and she saw the faces tip back, pale ovals under the bone helms, and thought, absurdly, *they are boys.*

"Lark," said Elyra. "Now."

The roar began in the ribs. She felt it gather there, as she had felt it a hundred times in practice over the empty fields of the Crownfold, a long shuddering swell of pressure that rose through the great chest and filled the throat and seemed to bend the air itself. Then Lark opened her mouth, and the wax in Elyra's ears did nothing at all.

It was not a sound. It was a blow. It struck the pike-line from above like the flat of a giant's hand, and she saw a hundred men go down in the same breath, as grass goes down in a flood. Pikes snapped. Shields cartwheeled into the air. The thorn hedge buckled at its center and folded inward, and the men at the edges of the blow staggered back with their hands pressed over their ears, as men always did, as if a pair of palms could stop it. Behind them the archers had loosed their first volley, and Elyra saw the fire-arrows rise, hundreds of little orange stars, and saw them curl back in the wind of the roar and fall among their own ranks, and heard, even through the wax, the screaming.

*Again,* she thought, and Lark took a great breath and roared again, and the second blow was as hard as the first.

Beside her Thorn's voice joined in, a deeper note, a rumble that she felt in her teeth, and where the two blows met a long ragged lane opened in the center of the Ashen line, a hundred paces wide and as clean as a furrow.

Below her the horns of Eastgirt sounded, and the bront-line moved.

She had never seen it. She had heard about it all her life, in the same terms that she had heard of the Nyxens, as one of the great sights of the realm: the Kelward charge, the thunder of the bronts. But she had not understood. There were nine hundred of them, russet-brown, enormous, each higher at the shoulder than a tall man's head, each with a single long horn lowered like a lance, and as they came through the gully mouth the ground began to shake. The sound was a long, low, rolling thunder, the sound from which the beasts took their name, and it went up through the soles of her feet even from a hundred feet in the air. The Ashen in the lane saw it coming. She watched them try to close their ranks. They had not time.

The bronts struck the broken pike-line at full gallop, and went through it like a flood through a fence.

She did not look away. She would think afterward that she should have, that there were things a person should not look at, but she was a rider and a Brennan and the sister of a king, and she looked at all of it, the horns and the screaming and the white bone armor going red. Then Lark wheeled, and Thorn wheeled beside her, and they swept over the field together in a long clean sweep with the sun at last breaking over the edge of the plain. The remnants of the Ashen line were running. Whole blocks of them were running, dropping their pikes, flinging off their bone plate, streaming back across the scrubland toward the mist.

She heard herself shouting. It was not a word. It was the sound a person makes when a thing she has been waiting for turns out to be true.

* * *

It was Gareth who saw the ropes.

He had flown low across the gully mouth to rest Thorn, and Elyra had followed, because there was a smooth stretch of clay at the foot of the north wall where a Nyxen could land without turning an ankle. She came down heavily. Lark's flanks were heaving. Her tongue was out, and her great ribs worked like a bellows, and Elyra slid from the saddle and put both hands against her hide and said, over and over, "Good girl, good girl, good girl," because it was all that she could think of.

"Elyra." Gareth's voice came from the other side of Thorn. It was very quiet. "Elyra, get back on."

"She needs a moment. She has roared twice, and..."

"Get back on."

She turned. And she saw them, coming down the gully from the east where the scrubland ran into the red walls, a ragged band of perhaps sixty men in bone armor, running. They had no pikes. They had no shields. They had ropes, great coiled loops of hemp trailing from their hands with the stones swinging at their ends, and they were not running away. They were running toward her.

She did not know afterward how she got into the saddle. She remembered only the lines, her fingers fumbling at the buckles, and Lark's head swinging round, and the first rope. It came out of the air with a whistle, a long, spinning loop that she did not so much see as feel, and it struck Lark across the shoulders and slid down the long neck and caught on the angle of the jaw, and the stone swung round and thudded against her ribs.

"No," said Elyra. "No, no..."

The second rope found the first. They were casting from hand to hand, as the old book had said, and the loop closed. It closed about Lark's throat with a snap, and Elyra heard a sound she had never heard, a thin, wheezing, choked sound, and felt the whole great body beneath her convulse. Lark's head went back. Her mouth opened. And a man who could not have been twenty, a boy in a bone breastplate with a red scar across his lip, ran out from the line and hurled a clay pot, with all the strength of his arm, straight into the gaping mouth.

It broke on her tongue. A yellow cloud boiled out, and the smell hit Elyra like a fist, a thick, acid reek of sulphur and rotten eggs that burned in her nose, and Lark made a sound that was not a roar but a retching, strangled bark and began to cough.

She drew her father's sword. She did not remember doing it. She leaned out of the saddle and chopped at the rope across Lark's throat with both hands, hacking, with the useless, desperate fury of a child, and the hemp parted on the third stroke, and the loop fell away in a coil. A hand caught her boot. She kicked it free. Another rope struck her across the back and nearly dragged her from the saddle, and then the whole gully shook with a long, low, rolling thunder, and a bront burst into the line with its horn down.

It was Alton. He was in the saddle of a great russet beast with a scar across its muzzle, in mail, with a mace in his fist and his helm gone, and he went through the rope-casters like a plow through stubble. She saw him swing the mace once, and saw a man's head go sideways. She saw him wheel the bront and drive it into the second line. There was blood across his face, and he was shouting, but she could not hear what, because Thorn had come down on the other side with a long grey blur of wings, and Gareth was in the saddle, white-lipped, and the big beast opened his mouth and roared.

It was a storm-force blow, at close range. It cleared the gully for fifty paces.

When it was over there was nothing standing within sight of the north wall but a pair of Nyxens and a bront and a very great many men on the ground. Lark was on her knees. She was coughing in great shuddering heaves, with a long rope of spit swinging from her jaw, and when Elyra slid down she found that her own hands were shaking so badly that she could not undo the strap.

"I have you," said Alton. He had dismounted. He stood beside her with his bloodied mace hanging from his wrist and one hand held out, palm up, the way a groom holds a hand to a frightened horse. "I have you, Princess. You may let go of the sword."

She looked down. She was still gripping it with both hands, and the knuckles had gone white.

"They are using the old hunting," she said. Her voice came out strange. "They have read the books. They know what it is to close a Nyxen's throat. They have nets, and ropes, and... and something in a pot..."

"I know."

"Lark could have died."

"She did not." He took her wrists and gently, one finger at a time, loosened her hands. "She is alive, and she is coughing, and she will eat tomorrow. It is a good deal, for a first morning."

She did not answer. She put her forehead against Lark's wet, trembling cheek and breathed, and the smell of the yellow smoke was still in her nose, and she thought: *Nine days. They watched us for nine days. They knew what we would do, and they knew when, and they sent sixty men to die in the one place where I would put my foot down to catch my breath.*

*They sent the sixty to fix me there.*

"Alton," she said, and her voice cracked. "What are the Ashen doing now?"

"Running, Princess. They are in full flight."

"No." She lifted her head. "Not those. The others. The ones we cannot see."

He looked at her. She watched the thing come into his eyes, a slow, cold understanding, as a stone sinks through water, and she saw him turn, very slowly, toward the south wall of the Redcut, where the dry cleft of a side-gully ran up into the red cliff, so narrow and so crooked that Kelward patrols had not ridden it in a generation.

"The Dry Seam," said Alton.

* * *

By the time she saw the smoke, it was too late.

It rose from the rear of the Redcut, from behind the narrowing, where the long clay floor ran back toward the holdfast and the farms and the road to Eastgirt. It was not a thin grey thread. It was black, and thick, and spreading, and under it, a hundred feet below, the Dry Seam had opened like a wound and was pouring Ashen into the holdfast's rear.

She had not known there could be so many in so small a place. They came out of the cleft three and four abreast, in bone armor and bare heads, with fire-arrows nocked and ropes coiled, and they did not stop to form a line. They simply ran. They ran at the rear gate of the holdfast, where a squad of Kelward men and a handful of green-and-gold surcoats stood in a ragged knot with their backs to the wooden doors, and Elyra, from the air, saw Captain Harrun's face tip up toward her and heard, very faintly, a cry.

Lark's wings struck the air. She was still coughing, still shaking, but she went, as a good hound goes at a hand on its collar, and Thorn went beside her. They came over the rear gate together with the dawn full on their backs.

*Not again,* she thought. *Please, not so soon.*

"Lark. Now."

The roar was weak, a cough with a blow in it. It struck the head of the column, and the Ashen at the front went down in a heap, and the ones behind trampled them, and Gareth's roar came a heartbeat after and cleared a hundred paces of the cleft. But it did not stop them. The men at the sides of the blow were on their feet in a moment, with their ears bleeding and their eyes wide, and the ones at the back had not even felt it. The Seam was a very narrow place, and a Nyxen could not roar into the sides of a cliff.

She saw Fenn.

She would remember it afterward more clearly than anything else that day. He stood at the rear gate with a boy's round shield on his left arm and a notched sword in his right, the same boy she had spoken to in the courtyard at the outer watch, the one with the bandage on his arm who could not stop looking at Lark. He was eight and ten. He had a smear of soot across his nose. He raised his sword to the sky when he saw her, and he laughed.

He was still laughing when the first fire-arrow took him through the throat.

It was nothing. It was a small, neat thing, and over in a breath. He sat down against the gate, with the sword still in his hand and a look of enormous surprise on his face, and then the Ashen swarmed over him and the doors went in.

"No!" She did not know she had shouted. "*No!*"

They were through. The gate was down. The Ashen were pouring into the narrow place behind the Redcut, between the holdfast and the gully mouth, and Simon's bront-line, which had charged out to the plain with such glory an hour before, was suddenly a thousand paces from home with an army at its back.

Elyra saw it all at once. She saw the Duke of Eastgirt on his great brown bront, in the center of the line, with his sword up and his beard blown back, wheeling to meet the thing behind him. She saw him drive his horn-beast straight into the mouth of the Dry Seam, shouting, with a hundred bronts behind him, and she saw the Ashen pikemen, who had been waiting all this time in the cleft for exactly this, step out of the dark with their points set.

It was not a battle. It was a collision. The bronts struck the pikes at a gallop, and the sound that rose was beyond words. Animals screamed. She saw a great russet shape go down with a dozen white shafts in its chest. She saw Simon thrown. She saw him roll, and rise, and take a blow from a bone-edged blade across the thigh that opened his mail like a cloth, and go down again.

"Alton!" It was Gareth's voice, a long way off. "*Alton!*"

He was already running. He had left the bront behind and was going across the clay on foot with the mace in his fist, and she saw him reach his brother in a rush of dust and drag him up by the collar, and saw the blade come down on him, and the mace come up. She saw him fight with Simon's weight across his shoulder, step by step, back toward the gully, and she knew with a cold, sick certainty that they would not make it.

"Gareth!" She hauled Lark round. "The narrow place! If they hold it, the whole line is cut off! We have to..."

"I know."

"If we close it with a roar..."

"I know what it needs." His face, across the gap between the saddles, was gray. He did not look at her. "I know what you are going to say."

"We have to use the other roar."

"Elyra."

"They are going to die. All of them. My cousin, and the bronts, and Alton. There are two thousand men in that cut, and they will be butchered in the next quarter hour, and there is nothing else..."

"I have never done it," said Gareth.

"I know."

"I have never done it, and I do not think that I can."

"Then I will," said Elyra.

She said it very calmly. It surprised her. She turned Lark's head to the south wall of the cut, to the long, jagged mouth of the Dry Seam where the pikemen were pouring out in their hundreds, and she leaned along the great neck, and put her hands on either side of the beast's jaw, and said, as quietly as she had ever said anything in her life:

"Lark. Please."

The beast did not move.

It was a heartbeat, no more. But she felt it, through her knees and her palms and the long muscles of the neck: a small, clear, unmistakable refusal. Lark's ears had come back. Her head had turned, a hand's breadth, and one great dark eye was looking back over her shoulder at her rider, with a look so plain and so entirely an animal's that Elyra almost wept.

*I know,* she thought. *I know, my darling. I know. I would not ask it if I could not bear it more than I can bear this.*

"Please," she said again.

And Lark turned back to the cleft, and opened her mouth.

* * *

It was not a thing she would ever be able to describe.

She did not hear it. She would never hear it, not even in the dreams that came afterward. The wax in her ears did nothing, because it was not a thing the ears could hear; it was a pressure, a deep, slow, terrible stillness that fell on the whole cut like a held breath and then shattered. The Ashen in the mouth of the Dry Seam did not fall. They did not stagger. They simply stopped, all together, a hundred and more, as a bell stops, with their pikes in their hands and their faces turned up. And then the blood came out of their ears.

It came out of their noses, and their eyes, and the corners of their mouths. It ran down their faces in thin red threads. A man in the front rank put up a hand to his head and brought it away red, and looked at it, with an expression of mild puzzlement, and sat down. Another put his hands over his ears and screamed. Then he fell. Then they were all falling, in rows, folding in on themselves like cut wheat, and the bone armor that had turned a hundred blades lay open on the clay like the shells of something that had been eaten from inside.

She had known. She had been taught it, as a girl, in the same chapter as the ropes, and she had told herself that a lesson was not a thing. It did not matter. She sat in the saddle with her hands knotted in the fur of Lark's neck and watched the pike-line, which was a hundred paces wide and a dozen ranks deep, simply cease to exist, and the only thing she could think was: *They are not even screaming.*

The ones at the back were. They had seen it. They were running, back into the Seam, throwing down their weapons and clawing at each other to get away, and the Kelward line surged forward with a great hoarse cry into the space where the pikes had been.

The Ashen did not come back through the Dry Seam that morning.

* * *

They lost the Redcut anyway.

It took until noon, and it was not a rout. It was a long, grim, shouting retreat, down the length of the cut to the road to Eastgirt, with the Ashen coming on behind in numbers that no roar could stop, and the holdfast going up in black smoke at their backs. The bronts held the narrows. Harrun's remaining men, nine of them, held the gate. Gareth flew the rear, over and over, driving back each fresh wave with short sharp blows that he plainly hated, and Elyra flew the van, ahead of the column, with Lark's wings dragging and her own hands numb on the saddle strap, and did not use the other roar again.

She did not look at the cut. She did not look at what lay in it. She looked at the road, and the line of the wounded and the dying and the stumbling that stretched along it for a mile, and she counted the green-and-gold surcoats, because it was a thing to count. She counted nine.

It was nearly dusk when they came to the waystation at Hallow Ford, six leagues west of the Redcut, where a dry stream-bed cut the road and a stone barn stood against the evening. The Ashen had stopped on the road behind them. They had not given up. They were sitting down in their thousands among the ruins of the holdfast, as men sit down to a meal that has been long in coming.

Simon Kelward lay on a pallet in the barn with his leg bound to the thigh in a cloth that had been white. A maester with grey hands and a face as pale as the bandage was cutting away the mail. The duke's eyes were closed. His beard was clotted with blood, and he was breathing through his teeth in long slow hisses, and every one of them was a curse.

"Will he keep it?" said Elyra.

"He will keep it, Princess." The maester did not look up. "The blade went in at an angle. It missed the great vessel by the breadth of a finger. He will walk with a stick. He will not like it."

"I do not like it now," said the Duke of Eastgirt, without opening his eyes. "Is that you, cousin?"

"It is."

"Tell me the count."

She told him. She did it plainly, in the order in which she had counted. The Redcut was lost, and the watch-stone, and the holdfast behind it. The bronts had lost four hundred and sixty. The levy had lost, so far as anyone could tell, eleven hundred. Captain Harrun had nine men left of twenty. The Ashen had come in with some three thousand and had lost perhaps nine hundred, and had not yet sent a third of what they had behind the first line.

Simon heard it through. When she was done he lay for a time with his eyes shut and his hand clenched in the blanket.

"The boy," he said. "Fenn. He was at the gate."

"Yes."

"His mother is a laundress at Eastgirt. She will have to be told." He drew a long breath. "I will tell her myself. When I can stand."

"Cousin..."

"I asked for the king's shadow, Elyra." He opened his eyes at last, and they were bloodshot and very tired and not at all angry. "I asked for it after Mart died, and I was told it would come. I did not want a girl and a boy on two Nyxens. I wanted what the Ashen have been afraid of for twenty years. I wanted the thing that sits in the sky and does not have to be asked twice." A small, bitter smile moved his lips. "But I would not have kept the Redcut for an hour without you. And I would not have got out of it alive. You may tell my wife I said that, if I am not there to say it."

"Simon."

"Go and see to your beast, cousin. And write to the king. Tell him what you saw. Tell him the Ashen know how to kill a Nyxen, and he will not like that either."

* * *

She found Lark in the lee of the stone barn, lying on her side in the straw with her great head across a heap of saddle-blankets and her flanks rising and falling in short, uneven breaths. Gareth had been there before her. Thorn lay a little way off, with a bucket by his muzzle. The grey beast was eating, slowly, from his rider's hand, a single piece of dried pear at a time.

"She has not touched it," said Gareth, nodding at the pile of dried fruit by Lark's nose. He did not look at her. His voice was flat and tired and gentle. "I put a little honey on it. She would not take that either."

Elyra sat down beside her Nyxen and laid her palm against the hot, rough neck. The fur was damp. Under it the great vessels pulsed, quick and light, and the beast turned her head and looked at her, with the eye that had looked at her at the cleft, and made a small, low, miserable sound in her throat.

"I know," said Elyra. "I know."

She stayed there for a long time. She did not cry. She had thought she would, but she found, sitting in the straw with her hand on Lark's neck and the dark coming down over the stone barn, that there was no room in her for it. There was only a vast, silent, aching emptiness, and, somewhere very far off, the sound of a bront bellowing in the night.

"It does not get easier," said Gareth, after a while. He had not moved. "Osric's patrols used the lesser roar on the ice, on men who were trying to climb the walls of Frostholt. It was a storm, no more. They went over the edge, and some of them broke their necks, and I was sick for a week. But the other..." He shook his head. "I would not have done it."

"I know."

"I would have sat on my beast and watched them kill Alton."

"I know. That is why I asked."

He looked at her then. It was very dark in the lee of the barn, and she could see only the line of his cheek and the faint glint of the silver in his hair. His face was calm. It was her brother's face, young and lined and quiet, and she saw, quite clearly, that he was not angry with her and did not pity her and had nothing at all to say.

"You were right," said Gareth. "I should have been able to. I am glad that one of us could."

"I am not glad."

"No." He took a piece of dried pear from the heap and held it under Thorn's nose. "That is why it was you."

She sat there until her legs went numb. When she rose, it was to find that Alton Kelward had been standing at the corner of the barn for some time, in the dusk, with a cup of water in his hand and his bloodied mace hanging from his belt. He had a bandage on his forearm. He did not speak. He crossed the straw and held the cup out, and she drank it, and gave it back.

"You did not tell me to fly home," she said.

"It did not occur to me."

"It occurred to my brother. Twice. He told me in the air."

"Then your brother has more sense than I." He turned the cup in his hands. "I thought of it, Princess. When I saw the rope. I thought: she is the sister of a king, and she is going to die at the Redcut, and I will have to write to the king and tell him I let her." He looked up. His eyes were very steady. "And then I thought that you would have gone, if I asked, and that I would have thought less of you for it, and of myself a good deal less."

"That is a poor reason."

"It is the only one I had."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, and it hurt. She took the cup from him again, though it was empty, and held it, because it was something to hold.

"I should write," she said.

"I will find you a pen."

* * *

It was a very small slip of paper. She tore it herself from the end of a roll of Simon's steward's accounts, and cut it with her knife into a strip no longer than her finger, and sat in the light of a single tallow candle in the stone barn with the strip pressed flat beneath her thumb. A raven stood on the beam above her, one of Simon's, a thick-necked, bad-tempered bird that had been sent over from the Eastgirt loft that morning in a wicker cage and had not stopped complaining since. It was watching her with one black eye.

She wrote the first three lines with no difficulty.

*The Redcut is lost. Simon lives and will keep the leg. Gareth is here.*

She stopped. The pen hung over the slip, and a drop of ink gathered on the nib and fell, a small black star on the grain.

There were two ways to write the next line. She knew them both. She had known them since the cleft, in some part of her that was still sitting in the saddle with her hands in Lark's fur. She could write *Send the king.* It was what Simon had meant, and what the Ashen feared, and what the whole of the Eastverge had been asking for since the day Mart died. It would take a man a quarter of a minute to read. And Rodrik would read it in the solar at Foldhall with his hands flat on the table, and she knew exactly what his face would do, and exactly what he would say, and exactly how much of the Crownfold he would break in order to say it.

He would come. He would come in three days, and he would be too late for nothing, and he would fly into the Redcut in front of the whole Ashen army with a pregnant wife behind him and no heir in the cradle and every lord in the realm watching to see what a king did when he was asked.

She could not do that to him. She could not do it to Elyn.

She thought about it for a long time. Then she dipped the pen again, and she wrote, very small, in the cramped, careful hand of a person who has no table and a great deal to say:

*Send Hart.*

It was the same, she told herself. It was exactly the same. Rodrik would know it, and Elyn would know it, and neither of them would pretend to be deceived. But a king could be asked for his beast. He could not be asked for himself. It was a kind of mercy, and a kind of lie, and in the morning she would probably be ashamed of it.

She folded the slip and slid it into the little brass tube on the raven's leg, and the bird hopped to her wrist, and she carried it out into the night. The stars were out, hard and bright over the dry road, and in the east, beyond the dark shape of the barn, the Ashen fires had begun to burn, hundreds of them, a long red line that ran from the horizon to the Redcut and back again, hundreds upon hundreds of them.

She raised her arm. The raven spread its wings with a noise like a snapped cloth and was gone, a black shape against the stars, flying west.

Elyra stood and watched until she could not see it. Then she went back to the barn, and lay down in the straw beside her Nyxen, and put her arm across the great, trembling, tawny neck, and slept.
