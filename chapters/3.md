ELYRA
The wind at a thousand feet did not blow; it tore. 

Elyra leaned low over Lark's neck, her thighs locked against the heavy leather of the riding saddle. Below her, the road east from Foldhall had vanished entirely, replaced by the jagged, rising teeth of the arid highlands. She did not use a whip or spurs to ask for speed. A rider who needed iron to communicate with a Nyxen was a rider who would eventually fall from the sky.

Instead, she shifted her weight a fraction of an inch to the left. Lark answered immediately, dropping her left wing and banking hard into a massive updraft rising off the sun-baked gullies. The force of it hit Elyra like a physical blow, stealing the breath from her lungs. She laughed, a sound whipped away instantly by the rush of air.

She had waited sixty days to throw the saddle over this beast. Sixty days pacing the walls of Foldhall, watching her brothers argue in the hall and her king stare out the eastern windows with a tight jaw, waiting for Captain Harrun's infantry to cross a thousand miles of dirt. If she had flown the day the swords marched, she would have reached the Eastverge in three days and spent the rest of the summer sitting in a hostile courtyard alone. 

So she had waited for the grim mathematics of the march to play out. And now, the chains were broken.

Far below, a thin line of iron-grey and rust-red wound its way through a steep mountain pass. Harrun’s column. 

Elyra pressed her hand flat against Lark's coarse, tawny-brown fur, urging the beast into a shallow dive. They circled once over the men. Harrun did not halt his march, but Elyra saw the weathered captain raise a hand in salute. Faint and tinny over the roar of the wind came the sound of twenty men cheering. To swords who had walked a thousand miles on foot, the shadow of a royal Nyxen meant they were not walking into the dark alone. 

Lark let out a low, thrumming rumble that vibrated through Elyra's chest—a beast entirely at ease in its domain. The massive wings, leather-thick and covered in a fine layer of down near the joints, beat a steady rhythm as they caught a new thermal, each downward stroke possessing enough brute force to snap a pine tree in half. Elyra banked hard, leaving the slow march of the infantry far behind, the sky above the Eastverge tasting of dust and dry pine.

Below them, the Eastgirt unfolded like a crumpled parchment of brown and rust. This was not the soft, green Vine Shore or the gentle, rolling Merelands. This was the hard country. The earth was scarred by deep, twisting gullies and sudden drops, a landscape carved by ancient floods and baked hard by merciless summers. It was a place that bred hard men and unforgiving memories.

They had been flying since dawn, stopping only once to let Lark drink from a high mountain tarn. It was near that water that a shepherd had babbled to her about a massive shadow passing over the high crags three days prior. The man had sworn the beast was twice the size of Lark, its fur as dark as old iron. Oak was still alive, it seemed, flying wild in the unmapped peaks, growing larger by the year.

Elyra could feel her own beast's body heat rising, the deep furnace heat burning within Lark to keep them aloft. The air at this altitude was freezing, but Elyra was sweating beneath her riding leathers, baked by the sheer radiant heat pouring off the Nyxen's hide.

She adjusted her grip on the reins. Far ahead, perched on the edge of a deep ravine, the stone walls of the outer watch holdfast came into view. From this height, it looked like a child's toy dropped in the dirt. But as Lark angled downward, entering a long, sweeping dive, the details sharpened.

Elyra could see the wooden palisade, the slate roof of the keep, and the tiny figures of men rushing out into the courtyard.

They had seen the shadow.

Lark did not land gently. She did not know how to be gentle. The Nyxen flared her wings at the last possible second, catching the air like a massive sail. She dropped the final thirty feet like a falling boulder, her four heavy, clawed limbs striking the hard-packed earth of the courtyard with a sound like a siege engine breaching a gate.

Dust exploded outward in a blinding cloud. Men shouted. Horses in the stables went mad with terror, kicking at their stalls and screaming.

Lark folded her wings tight against her flanks and shook herself, a massive, full-body shudder that sent a secondary wave of dust over the yard. She lowered her heavy, broad head, her nostrils flaring as she took in the scent of the holdfast. She let out a short, sharp hiss, unhappy with the smell of old blood and fear that clung to the place.

Elyra unhooked her safety lines and slid down the Nyxen's shoulder, her boots hitting the dirt with a solid thud. She slapped Lark's flank once, a firm, reassuring strike.

"Easy, girl," Elyra murmured in Aurouvian, the private language of the riders. "We hunt, but not yet."

The dust began to settle. The men in the yard had drawn back, forming a wide, terrified circle around the beast. They were Kelward men, wearing the rust-red and iron-grey surcoats of Eastgirt, but their hands rested nervously on the hilts of their swords. A Nyxen was a myth to most of them, a nightmare of scale and fur and violence that belonged in the sky, not in their courtyard.

A heavy wooden door slammed open. Duke Simon Kelward strode into the yard, followed by a half-dozen of his household knights.

Simon was a man carved from the same hard rock as his land. He was broad-shouldered and thick-necked, his beard entirely grey, his face mapped with scars from a lifetime of border skirmishes. He wore chainmail beneath a boiled leather tunic, and his boots were coated in the same dry dust that covered everything in the Eastverge.

He did not look terrified. He looked angry.

"Princess," Simon barked, stopping exactly ten paces from Lark. The Nyxen tracked him with cold, intelligent eyes, a low growl starting in her chest. "I sent a courier to Foldhall demanding the king. I received a girl and a beast."

Elyra did not flinch. She stepped forward, putting herself between Simon and her Nyxen.

"You received the crown's justice, Duke Simon," Elyra said, her voice carrying clearly across the silent yard. "My brother is managing the realm so that you have a realm to defend. I am here to manage your dead."

Simon's jaw tightened. "A watchman's throat was opened in the dark. Rosegate colors were left tied to the wood. My men are calling for a march on the Vine Shore. I need the king's authority to hold them back, or the king's sword to lead them forward."

"If you march on the Vine Shore over a scrap of cloth, you will be fighting a war designed by a shadow," Elyra countered. "Rodrik knows Duke Roland. Roland is many things, but he is not a man who leaves his signature on a murder. Someone wants the Eastverge looking south while the true threat comes from the north."

Simon crossed his arms. "And you intend to prove this?"

"I intend to find the men who cut your watchman," Elyra said. She pulled her riding gloves off and tucked them into her belt. "Where is the cloth?"

A younger man stepped out from behind Simon. He was tall, leaner than the duke, with the same rust-red surcoat but a more calculated, watchful expression. He had dark hair tied back with a leather thong and eyes that did not carry the same immediate anger as his lord's. He looked at Lark, then at Elyra, and he did not step back.

"I have the cloth, Your Grace," the younger man said.

"This is my brother, Alton," Simon said roughly, though some of the anger had bled from his voice. "He found the body. He found the dye."

Elyra looked at Alton Kelward. She knew the name, of course, but Foldhall rarely saw the younger sons of the border lords. They were usually too busy bleeding in the gullies to attend feasts.

"Show me," Elyra commanded.

Alton reached into a pouch at his belt and produced a small, folded square of fabric. He stepped forward, closing the distance to Elyra. Lark shifted her weight, the movement causing the earth to tremble slightly, but Alton did not hesitate. He held the cloth out.

Elyra took it. It was stained dark with dried blood, but the underlying color was a brilliant, unmistakable blue. The blue of House Varen. The blue of the Vine Shore.

She raised it to her face and inhaled deeply.

Beneath the metallic tang of blood and the dry dust of the Eastverge, there was a faint, cloying sweetness. Rose soap.

"It is a lie," Elyra said, lowering the cloth.

"It is blue," Simon noted flatly.

"It is dye, Lord Simon, not truth," Elyra shot back. "The Vine Shore smells of salt and fresh wine. This smells of a perfumer's shop in a Crownfold market, applied heavily to mask the smell of whatever sweat actually carried it here."

Alton tilted his head, watching her closely. "A perfumer's shop is a long ride from a dry gully, Princess. Why go to the trouble?"

"To make you angry," Elyra said. She looked directly at Alton, finding a sharp, pragmatic intelligence in his gaze. "An angry man stops looking for tracks and starts looking for revenge. Did you find tracks, Lord Alton?"

"The ground around the post was trampled," Alton replied, his voice steady. "My brother's men arrived quickly. Too quickly to preserve the perimeter. But I circled wide before dawn. I found three sets of unshod prints heading north. Toward the Ashlands."

Simon let out a harsh breath. "Reavers. Emboldened by the king's absence."

"Emboldened by Oak's absence," Elyra corrected quietly.

The courtyard went entirely still. It was the truth no one in Foldhall liked to say out loud. When King Aldren VIII died, his massive, ancient Nyxen, Oak, had broken its chains and flown away to grieve in the high crags, refusing all riders. Oak had been a terror, a beast that had burned entire armies in the First Ashen War. Without Oak casting his massive shadow over the borders, the wild men of the Ashlands felt a sudden, dangerous courage.

"My brother Hart is large," Elyra continued, her voice hard. "But he cannot be in three places at once. So he sent Lark."

She turned back to the Nyxen. Lark was watching the stables, her ears swiveling to track the panicked sounds of the horses.

"Fetch me the man who survived the raid," Elyra ordered.

Simon gestured to one of his knights. A few moments later, a man was brought forward. He was young, perhaps eight and ten, with a crude bandage wrapped tightly around his upper arm. He was pale, sweating, and he could not stop looking at Lark.

"Your name," Elyra said.

"Fenn, Your Grace," the boy stammered, swaying slightly on his feet.

"You held the watch with Mart, Fenn. Tell me what you saw."

Fenn swallowed hard. "Nothing, Your Grace. That was the terror of it. There was no noise. Just the wind, and then Mart was falling, and my arm was burning. They moved like ghosts."

"Ghosts do not bleed," Elyra said. "Did you cut one of them?"

"I... I swung in the dark. My blade caught something hard. Wood, maybe. Or bone."

Elyra exchanged a glance with Alton.

"Bone armor," Alton said quietly. "Ashlands."

Elyra nodded. The reavers of the Ashlands wore armor crafted from the bones of dead beasts, lashed together with sinew. It was crude, but it turned a glancing blade as well as steel.

"You have your proof, Duke Simon," Elyra said, turning back to the older man. "It is not the Vine Shore. It is the north."

"Proof does not answer a dead man's widow," Simon growled. "I need men marching into those gullies."

"You will have them," Elyra promised. "My brother has dispatched Captain Harrun and twenty Crownfold swords. They will be here by tomorrow. They will hold this watch while you gather your levies."

Simon looked surprised, then slightly mollified. Harrun was a name respected in the Eastverge.

"And until they arrive?" Simon asked.

Elyra pulled her riding gloves back on, snapping the leather tight over her wrists.

"Until they arrive," Elyra said, a cold smile touching the corners of her mouth, "Lord Alton and I are going to take a ride. We are going to find three ghosts who leave unshod prints in the dirt."

Alton's eyebrows rose. "We, Your Grace?"

"You know the tracks," Elyra pointed out. "I have the beast. Bring a horse, Lord Alton. A fast one that does not mind the smell of such a beast."

Twenty minutes later, they rode out of the holdfast gates. Alton rode a tall, deep-chested gelding that tossed its head and rolled its eyes at the massive Nyxen loping effortlessly beside it. Lark did not fly. She stayed on the ground, using her heavy forelimbs like a bat, covering the uneven terrain with terrifying speed and grace.

Elyra rode in the saddle, her eyes scanning the horizon. The heat of the day was fully upon them now, baking the red earth and turning the air into a shimmering haze.

Alton rode twenty paces to her right, keeping his horse on a tight rein. He did not speak much, which Elyra appreciated. She hated the endless, empty chatter of the Crownfold lords. Alton was focused entirely on the ground, reading the subtle signs of disturbed dirt and broken scrub.

"They are moving fast," Alton called out after an hour of hard riding. He pointed to a faint scuff mark on a flat stone. "But they are heavy. They are carrying plunder from the lower holdfasts."

"Good," Elyra said. "Greed makes men slow."

They rode deeper into the gullies. The land here was a labyrinth of twisting canyons and dry riverbeds, the walls rising steep and jagged on either side. It was a perfect place for an ambush.

Lark knew it, too. The Nyxen's ears flattened against her skull, and she let out a low, continuous rumble that vibrated in the air like a warning bell. She was tasting the wind, her sensitive nostrils working constantly.

Suddenly, Lark stopped.

She dug her claws into the earth, throwing up a shower of dirt, and crouched low.

Elyra raised a hand, signaling Alton to halt.

"What is it?" Alton asked, his hand dropping to the hilt of his sword.

Elyra closed her eyes, feeling the tension in the Nyxen's muscles beneath her. Lark was not looking at the ground. She was looking up.

"The wind shifted," Elyra said softly. "They are not ahead of us."

She opened her eyes and looked up at the high ridge overlooking the canyon.

"They are above us."

A rock clattered down the steep canyon wall, bounding off the stones with a sharp crack.

Before the sound had completely faded, the ambush was sprung.

A dozen men rose from the ridge line, their faces smeared with grey ash, their bodies wrapped in crude bone armor and ragged furs. They did not shout. They did not scream a battle cry. They simply raised their bows and fired.

"Hold!" Elyra screamed to Lark, throwing her weight flat against the saddle.

The arrows rained down. Most of them shattered harmlessly against the rocks. Two struck Lark's shoulder, burying themselves shallowly in the thick, fire-resistant fur and the tough hide beneath.

Lark did not feel pain. She felt fury.

The Nyxen unleashed a roar that defied description. It was not a sound meant for human ears. It was a wall of shattering sound, a thunderous blast of wind and pure rage that hit the canyon walls and echoed back with multiplied force.

Alton's horse screamed and reared, nearly throwing him from the saddle. Several of the reavers on the ridge line dropped their bows and clutched their ears, stumbling backward in pain.

"Take the sky!" Elyra commanded, pulling hard on the left rein.

Lark surged forward, her massive leg muscles coiling like springs. She leaped into the air, her wings snapping open with a sound like a thunderclap. The sheer brute force of her takeoff sent a whirlwind of dust and loose stones flying into the air, blinding anyone left on the canyon floor.

They hit the updraft and shot upward, rising above the ridge line in a matter of seconds.

Elyra looked down. The reavers were scrambling over the rocks, trying to find cover, their ambush completely shattered by the reality of a Nyxen in combat. They had expected men on horses. They had expected easy kills.

"Burn them?" a dark, angry part of Elyra's mind whispered.

No. Rodrik's orders were clear. Hunt the hand that drew the blood.

"Break the line!" Elyra shouted, shifting her weight forward.

Lark tucked her wings and entered a steep, terrifying dive. She did not aim for the men. She aimed for the ridge itself.

The Nyxen slammed into the rocky outcropping just above where the largest cluster of reavers was hiding. The impact was catastrophic. Lark's heavy claws tore through the sandstone like it was wet clay. Massive boulders dislodged, tumbling down the slope in a localized avalanche of stone and dust.

Screams finally broke the silence of the reavers as the rocks rained down upon them.

Lark rebounded off the ridge, her wings snapping open again to catch the air, pulling them out of the dive with a bone-jarring jolt. Elyra gritted her teeth, feeling the strain in her shoulders as she fought to keep her seat.

She circled back, keeping Lark high above the dust cloud.

When the air finally cleared, the ambush was entirely broken. Three men lay dead or dying beneath the crushed stones. Four more were limping away frantically into the deeper gullies, abandoning their weapons.

Five men remained on the ridge, trapped behind a wall of fallen rock, cut off from escape.

Down on the canyon floor, Alton Kelward had brought his panicked horse under control. He had drawn his sword and was watching the trapped men with cold, calculating eyes.

Elyra brought Lark down on a flat plateau just above the trapped reavers. She did not dismount. She sat tall in the saddle, letting the massive beast snarl and snap its jaws at the terrified men below.

"You wear Ashlands bone," Elyra called down, her voice ringing clear in the sudden silence. "But you leave Vine Shore cloth on your kills. Why?"

The men did not answer. They looked at Lark, their eyes wide with absolute, paralyzing fear.

"Answer the Princess," Alton shouted from the canyon floor, his voice hard. "Or I will ask her to let the beast feed."

One of the men, older than the rest with a long scar down his cheek, dropped to his knees.

"Silver," the man gasped, holding up his empty hands. "We were paid in silver."

Elyra's stomach tightened. A political setup, just as Rodrik had feared.

"By whom?" she demanded.

"A man on the north road. Crownfold accent. High boots. He gave us the cloth. Told us to pin it to a dead watchman, and the Eastgirt would march south. Told us the borders would be empty for a month."

Elyra closed her eyes for a brief second. The truth was uglier than a simple raid. Someone within their own borders, someone with wealth and a Crownfold tongue, was actively trying to start a war between the Eastverge and the Vine Shore.

"Lord Alton," Elyra called down.

"Your Grace," Alton replied.

"Bind these men. We are taking them back to the holdfast. Duke Simon will want to hear this confession himself."

"And then?" Alton asked, sheathing his sword.

Elyra looked out over the vast, scarred landscape of the Eastverge. The summer wind was blowing hard now, carrying the scent of dry pine and distant rain.

"And then I send a letter to the king," Elyra said grimly. "The peace is over. We just haven't admitted it yet."


The ride back to the outer watch holdfast felt infinitely longer than the ride out. 

The heat of the afternoon had baked the Eastverge into a silent, shimmering oven. Even the insects seemed too exhausted to hum in the dry scrub. Behind Elyra and Alton, the bound reavers stumbled along at the end of a long tether tied to the pommel of Alton's saddle. They did not complain when the rope went taut, and they did not try to run. The constant, hulking presence of Lark matching their pace stride-for-stride was enough to keep them entirely docile.

Elyra kept the Nyxen on the ground. She could have taken the sky, flying ahead to deliver the news to Duke Simon herself, but there was a cold logic in riding beside the prisoners. A princess in the sky was a symbol. A princess riding in the dust, her boots caked in the same red dirt as the men of Eastgirt, was a statement. Rodrik had taught her that much. *They have to see you bleed, or sweat, or freeze with them,* he had told her once after a particularly punishing progress through the Icewide. *Otherwise, you are just a painting on a wall.*

Alton rode in silence for the first few miles. He was a difficult man to read. Where his brother Simon carried his anger like a drawn sword, Alton kept his guarded, calculating the angles. 

"You did not let the beast burn them," Alton said finally, his voice cutting through the dry crunch of hooves on stone. 

"My brother does not burn men who might still have a story to tell," Elyra replied, shifting her weight in the heavy leather saddle. "A burnt man cannot tell you who paid him the silver."

"A burnt man also cannot return to the Ashlands to tell his brothers that the skies over the Eastgirt are still lethal," Alton pointed out softly. 

Elyra glanced at him. His eyes were fixed forward on the path, his profile sharp against the harsh glare of the sun. "You think I was too soft on them."

"I think you are a Foldhall princess," Alton corrected, without looking at her. "You think of the realm. You think of the political game in the Crownfold, the friction between Rosegate and the Eastverge. Duke Simon and I... we think of the watchman who will have to stand alone at this post tomorrow night. If the Ashlands believe we are taking prisoners instead of taking heads, they will push further next time."

"If I had let Lark tear them apart in that canyon, we would not know about the Crownfold man with the high boots and the Vine Shore cloth," Elyra said, her voice tightening. "You would be marching your levies south to slaughter Rosegate men over a lie. You would give the architect of this war exactly what he wanted."

"True," Alton conceded. "But the Eastverge has always paid the price for Foldhall's diplomacy. King Rodrik understands that. I am not certain his council does."

"I am not his council," Elyra said. 

Alton finally turned his head to look at her. He studied her for a long moment, taking in the dust on her face, the sweat matting her tawny-brown hair, and the effortless way she commanded the massive, terrifying beast walking beside them. A faint, genuine smile broke through his guarded expression. 

"No, Your Grace. You are certainly not."

They reached the holdfast just as the sun began to dip behind the jagged western ridges, casting long, bruised shadows across the valley. 

Duke Simon was waiting in the courtyard when the heavy wooden gates were pulled open. He stood surrounded by his knights, his jaw set in a hard line. But when he saw the five Ashlands reavers stumbling at the end of Alton's rope, some of the tension bled out of his shoulders. 

"Reavers," Simon growled, stepping forward as Alton dismounted. 

"Ashlands," Alton confirmed, handing the rope to one of the guards. "They ambushed us in the deep gullies. Princess Elyra broke their line with the beast." He paused, looking at his older brother. "They confessed, Simon. They were paid in silver to pin the Vine Shore cloth to Mart's post. A man with a Crownfold accent hired them on the north road."

Simon's face darkened. He looked at the bound men, his hand resting heavy on the pommel of his sword. For a moment, Elyra thought he might draw it and end the matter right there in the dirt. But he looked at Elyra, then at Lark, who had dropped to her belly with a massive, exhausted sigh, and he let his hand fall away.

"Lock them in the deep cellar," Simon ordered his men. He turned his attention back to Elyra. The anger was still there, but it was no longer directed at her. It was directed north, and west, and at the shadows that were trying to play his house for fools. "You have my thanks, Princess. You proved the truth of it."

"The truth does not bring your watchman back, Duke Simon," Elyra said gently. "But it prevents more of your men from dying for a lie."

"I will hold the levies," Simon said. "For now. But if the Ashlands are moving, this border will need more than twenty Crownfold swords to hold it. Rodrik knows this."

"He knows," Elyra agreed. "I will write to him tonight."

She spent the evening in a cramped, drafty solar in the keep's upper tower, ignoring the exhaustion that ached in her bones. She called for ink and a fresh sheet of parchment. Outside the narrow slit window, she could hear the wind howling through the gullies, a lonely, desolate sound that made Foldhall feel a thousand miles away. 

She wrote the letter in her own hand, sealing it with a drop of plain red wax. She detailed the ambush, the bone armor, the confession of the silver, and the Crownfold man in the high boots. She told Rodrik that Simon was holding his spears, but that the restraint would not last the summer. The Eastverge was bleeding, and soon, it would demand an answer that could not be written on parchment. 

When she was finished, she carried the letter down to the courtyard herself. She found Alton Kelward standing near the stables, checking the hooves of his gelding by the light of a single torch. 

He looked up as she approached. "Your Grace."

"I need a fast rider," Elyra said, holding out the sealed letter. "Someone who knows the roads by night. This needs to be in my brother's hands before Harrun's column arrives tomorrow."

Alton took the letter, his fingers brushing briefly against hers. His hands were as calloused and scarred as her own. "I will ride it myself."

"You have been in the saddle since dawn," Elyra pointed out. 

"I know the dark roads better than any courier in this keep," Alton said smoothly. He tucked the letter into his tunic. "And the king will want to hear the details of the canyon skirmish from someone who saw the beast work." 

He looked at her again, that same sharp, calculating intelligence in his eyes. But this time, there was something else there, too. A quiet, undeniable respect. 

"Get some sleep, Princess," Alton said. "The Eastgirt is safe tonight."

Elyra watched him saddle a fresh horse and ride out into the dark, the hoofbeats fading quickly into the wind. She stood alone in the quiet courtyard for a long time, listening to the steady, rhythmic breathing of Lark sleeping in the stables. 

The peace was over. She had felt it in the canyon, and she felt it now, settling over the holdfast like a shroud. A new king sat on the throne, a terrifying beast had abandoned the skies, and the shadows were beginning to move. 

Elyra looked up at the stars, cold and bright above the harsh country, and prayed her brother was ready for what was coming.
