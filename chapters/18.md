ISOLDE

"Where is Dunstan?"

Hamo asked it to the window, with his back to her, in the grey before dawn, and Isolde Yarben stopped with a hairpin between her teeth and understood that the winter was over.

She took the pin out. She set it down on the washstand very gently, as one sets down a cup that is too full.

"You have asked me that four times since the harvest moon," she said.

"And you have told me four times that he is ill at Merecroft. That he took a chill on the road, and the Maester there has him in bed, and he will write when he can hold a pen." Hamo turned from the window. He had slept in his shirt again, and his thin fair hair stood up behind his ear where he had slept on it, and his face in the poor light looked younger than five and forty and a good deal older than it had. "Dunstan has written to me every ninth day for twenty years, Isolde. He wrote when his daughter was brought to bed. He wrote when the mill burned. He has not written in six months, and you have been very careful to mention it only when I do."

"Hamo."

"I am a coward. I am not a fool." He said it without heat, like a man reading from a tally. "Where is he?"

The swallows had come back to the eaves. She could hear them scrabbling above the window, the same small furious racket as in autumn, and below in the yard a pail clanked on stone and a man called to a horse. Somewhere in the long dark halls of the castle a bell struck the fifth hour of the morning. She thought of all the answers she had made over the winter, one for each time, each a little better than the last. She had grown proud of them. She stood with her hands folded in front of her grey widow's gown, and found she could not find a single one.

"He is in the oath-yard," she said. "In the north corner, under the pauper stones, with a board at his head that says Dun and nothing else. He has been there since the night of the harvest feast."

Hamo did not move.

"I could not claim him," said Isolde. "To claim him I should have had to say where he was found, and who I was with, and why. So I let the watch write him down as a footpad's work, and I paid an Oath-Warden who asked no questions, and I came back to this room and told you he was ill." She heard her own voice, level and quite dry, and thought it a poor thing to hear in such a room. "I am sorry. I thought you would run. I thought if you knew, you would take the horses in the night and go home and sign whatever they put in front of you, and they would let you live, and I would have lost nothing but my good opinion of you. I was wrong to keep it from you. I was not wrong about what you would do."

"What happened to him?"

"Pell had him killed. A boy did it, one of Lord Fenlow's, with a long knife, from behind, in an alley by the sailmaker's yard. He did not cry out." She made herself go on. "I stood there. I had a lantern. Dunstan said that he would leave Pell in the river, and he said it for me, because I was the one with the bag of silver and no sense, and the boy came out of the dark."

The room was quite silent. Hamo Merecroft stood in his shirt with his hands hanging, a small, spare, frightened man with his father's weak chin, and she waited for him to weep, or to shout, or to sit down on the bed with his head in his hands. She had seen him do all three.

He did none of them.

He crossed the room to the table where his writing case stood and opened it. He took out a folded paper, thick, sealed in green wax with the Merecroft heron pressed into it, and he looked at it for a long moment as though it were a thing he had found in a drawer.

"I wrote this on the night Prince Edwyn spoke to me in Fenlow's passage," he said. "The fifth copy. I sealed it. I did not mean to. The wax was hot and my hand did it." He turned it over. "Everything that was said at that table in the spring. In order. The names of the three lords who were sitting there and the sums that were named and the thing I said yes to. I had meant to burn it as I burned the others."

"Hamo, you do not have to."

"I should like to be a man Dunstan would not have been ashamed of." He put the paper inside his shirt, against his skin. "I thought it a small ambition. It seems that I have been a year at it." He looked at her, and his eyes were red and perfectly steady. "Where is Prince Rowan?"

She had not expected the question. She had expected, to her shame, to be arguing for an hour.

"At the wharf," said a voice from the door.

It was the guest-hall boy, a freckled lad of ten with a bucket in each hand, who had been sent up with hot water and had stopped in the doorway with his mouth open as boys do. He went red.

"I am sorry, my lady. The door was not shut." He set the buckets down. "The Prince is at the wharf. The ice went out of the Fold three nights past, and the first barges came down from the upper reaches last night, and he went down at first light with the boy and his tally-sticks to count them. The cook says he is always there for the first barges. He likes to see what is actually in them."

"Thank you," said Isolde.

"And there is a rumor, my lady, in the kitchens." The boy had plainly been holding it in the whole way up the stair. "A raven came in the night from Harrow Hold. The king's captain is dead, the one bringing the prisoners west. They were set on at a ford, a mile from Harrow’s gate. All the Ashen prisoners dead too. All five. They say it was Ashen raiders, but the cook says that is a lie, because why should Ashen raiders come two hundred miles into the Eastverge to kill five of their own."

Hamo had gone as white as the wall.

"Prince Rowan asked me to come to the counting-room yesterday," he said. "The watchman brought his invitation. I told him I was ill. I have been ill whenever there was something to say."

"He is a captain," said Isolde, slowly. "He was the one who counted the prisoners."

"Captain Harrun," said the boy. "A Crownfold man. The cook knew his wife, they say she keeps a bakehouse in the lower town, a Kelward cousin, a great loud woman. They have not told her yet."

Isolde stood very still. She thought of a small man with a leaking boot at a council in the lesser hall, and a laugh like a rusty hinge. She thought of the prisoners who had said *silver* and *high boots* and *Crownfold* on their knees in a cellar a thousand miles away. She thought: *they are tidying.*

"Pell will be tidying," said Hamo, very quietly, as if he had heard her. "Wim. Dunstan. The prisoners. The captain who would have carried them to the Prince." He swallowed. "I am the last thing on the table."

"Then we shall be untidy." She took her cloak from the hook. "We send Oswin to the castle gate for watchmen. You and I wait here with Cadd until they come. Then they can take us to the Prince."

"Pell’s men stand at the guest-hall gate, Isolde. I saw them last night. Send Oswin alone and they will follow him, or come up while he is gone. We take both men, and we use the busy street."

Isolde went to the shutter. Two men in Fenlow green stood beneath the guest-hall arch. A third watched the path to the castle.

"The quay is nearer than the keep," she said. "We go together. Cadd takes the front, Oswin the rear. If we meet the watch, we stop with them."

"Isolde." He was trying to smile. It was a poor, crooked thing and it went wrong at the corner, and she loved him so much in that moment that it frightened her. "You said you would not tell me what to do."

"I am your sister," said Isolde Yarben. "I am not your lord. Give me your arm."

* * *

The lower town woke around them as they went down through it, and she kept her hand on her brother's sleeve.

Cadd walked ahead, a thick grey man of fifty with a sword that he wore as another man wears a hat, and Oswin came behind, a long boy of nine and ten with a slow voice and a great deal of Merecroft chin. Neither of them had been told anything. They had been told only that the lord and lady were going down to the wharf and that they were to keep close, and Cadd had looked once at Hamo's face and said nothing and shifted his sword to the front of his belt.

The lane that led down from the castle gate was called Chandlers' Row, and it was already loud. Shutters banged up. A woman was throwing a pail of slops from an upper window. The smell of the tallow-boilers lay on the street like a wet blanket, and below it the smells of bread and horses and the cold green river smell that came up the hill from the Fold. Two women stood gossiping at a pump with their arms full of washing.

"...and the shepherd swears it," one of them was saying. "Up on the Fold crags, the old Nyxen. The great brown one, the old king's. Big as a hayrick, he says, and grey about the muzzle, scratching his back on the rock."

"Oh, that one." The other shifted her washing. "Aldren's? I thought it had died years since."

"Well, it has not. They live a long while."

"Longer than kings, I suppose."

"They say he has been at the Quenby pear orchards, too, in the evenings. Takes the windfalls and leaves the trees."

"Well, that is manners, at least." The second woman laughed. "I should like a lodger who left the trees."

Isolde went by them without turning her head. A shadow passed over the street as she did, long and slow, and every face in the lane tipped back at once.

It was Hart. She knew him by the size before she saw the color, and then the morning sun came through a break in the cloud and lit him from beneath, and the whole of the huge body flared the deep tawny gold of a ripe field, with darker gold stripes showing across his shoulders and flanks like furrows ruled by a careful hand. The king was a small shape on his neck, a dark smudge in a plain cloak. The great wings beat once and held, and the beast slid across the sky above the roofs toward the river with the gentle rumbling hum that the whole of Foldhall knew and no longer heard. A child in a doorway pointed and shouted. A cartman touched two fingers to his brow in the old way and went back to his mule.

"He is very pleased with himself," said Hamo, beside her, in a thin, odd voice. "He always is, in the mornings."

"Walk, Hamo."

"I was only observing."

"Observe while you walk."

They came down the last of the Row and turned toward the river, into a street of joiners' yards where the sawpits were already open and the smell of fresh pine lay thick and sweet in the damp. A sleepy boy stood in a doorway scratching himself. Carts were drawn up on either side, loaded with squared timber, and the narrow way between them was very full of men going to work. Isolde noticed the tall carved front of Lord Fenlow's town house on her left, with its figure painted over the door, and she looked straight ahead.

Cadd stopped.

"My lady," he said. "Back."

She did not see the first of them. She heard Oswin say *oh*, in a small, polite voice, as though he had been told a piece of surprising news, and then Cadd had stepped forward with his sword half out and a man came off the back of a lumber cart, brown-cloaked and quick, with a short blade in his fist, and went in under the old man's arm.

It was very fast. She would remember that more than anything. Cadd's sword came the rest of the way out of the scabbard and she saw it flash and heard it ring on something, and then the brown cloak was against his chest and the old retainer made a noise like a man lowering a heavy chest and sat down in the road with both hands pressed to his throat. Blood came between his fingers. He looked at her, puzzled.

"Hamo," said Isolde. "Run."

There were four of them. She counted, because it was the thing she did. Two had come off the carts. One stood in the mouth of a sawpit with his hand on the hilt of a knife, a man with a flat, bored face who did not look like anything at all. One came running up the street from the castle end, behind them, and she saw that he was Oswin's height and rust-haired and thick in the neck, and then she saw that he was not, and that the boy she had thought she knew was only another boy.

Oswin had his sword out. He was holding it as he had been taught, point up, elbow bent, with his weight on the back foot, and his long face was white and rapt. He cut at the nearest brown cloak and missed, and cut again and caught him on the forearm, and the man swore and struck him in the belly.

"Oswin!"

"I am all right, my lady," said Oswin. He doubled up slowly. "I am quite all right. Go."

Hamo had her by the wrist. His hand was cold and damp and he pulled with a strength she had not known he had, and she went with him, stumbling, between two stacked loads of planking into a gap no wider than a man's shoulders. Behind her a blade scraped on the timber. A voice cried out, a man's voice, a stranger's, shouting for the watch, and somewhere a bell had begun to clang.

They came out at the back of a sawpit onto a yard full of sawdust and curled shavings, with a high fence and a gate. The gate was latched. Hamo got his hand under the bar and heaved. His breath went in and out in short, shuddering gasps.

"I cannot," he said. "It is stuck. Isolde, it is stuck."

"Lift."

"I am lifting."

"Lift harder."

A man came through the gap between the planking. He was the flat-faced one, the one who had looked like nothing, and he came at an easy walk, with his knife low, and she saw him look at Hamo and then at her and choose.

Isolde did not think. She had a hairpin in her cloak, a long steel one, the pin she had set down on the washstand and then taken up again when she dressed, and she had it in her hand before she knew why. She drove it at his eye. It went in at the corner and stopped on something, and he screamed, a high, astonished sound, and his hands flew to his face and the falling knife sliced through her glove and across her palm, a thin, cold line that did not hurt at all.

Then the gate gave.

They went through it into a lane, and the lane ran downhill, and at the foot of it she saw a sudden blaze of open grey light and a long low wall of wet stone and a forest of masts. It was the head of the river stairs.

"Down," said Hamo. "There. There, Isolde. He is there."

Far below on the wharf, small as a child's figure on a map, a man in a plain dark coat was standing among stacked bales with a boy at his elbow. He had a tally-stick in his hand and he was reading something from a barge's hold with his head bent, and she knew the set of the shoulders. Beyond him a line of watchmen in green leaned on their staves outside the customs house. There were six of them. There was a sergeant, square and weather-beaten, who she thought was called Pike.

They were a hundred and forty steps away.

"Come," said Hamo.

She wanted to stop. She would think about it afterward, for a long time, in a variety of rooms. She wanted to stand there at the head of the stair, with her hand bleeding into her glove and her brother's arm in hers, and let her legs stop, and breathe. The street behind them was full of shouting and the watch bell had not stopped. Whoever had loosed those men at them was a long way behind now. They had come through a sawpit and a locked gate with a hairpin and a broken latch and they were alive, and the river was in front of them, and the Prince was on it.

But the Prince was a hundred and forty steps off, and Pike's watchmen were at the bottom of them. It would be a great deal safer at the foot of that stair than it was at the head.

"Come," she said.

They went down.

The stair was broad and old and worn in the middle into shallow troughs, with a rail of black iron along the river side and the blank wall of the joiners' yards on the other, and it ran down the hill in long straight flights broken by landings. Gulls wheeled over it. The stone was slick with the night's wet. Below on the water the barges rocked at their moorings, and the bargemen had stopped working to look up, and she heard a man shout, and a second, and saw the watchmen begin to turn their heads.

"Ten," said Hamo, beside her. He was counting under his breath. It was the old thing, the thing he did when he was frightened. "Eleven. Twelve."

"Hamo."

"It helps." He took a step, and another. "I counted on the clerks' stair, the night I did not go in. I got to a hundred and there were no more figures. I should like to see what is after it." His mouth twisted. "Thirteen. Fourteen."

"You will see it."

"Fifteen. Sixteen."

They went down together. She held his sleeve. Behind them and above, the noise of the lane rose and faded and rose, and she did not look back. The first landing went by, and the second. A bargeman at the foot of the stairs had set down his bale and was staring up at them with his jaw hanging, and beyond him the Prince had lifted his head.

"Forty," said Hamo. "Forty-one."

She saw Rowan Brennan see them. He stood quite still, with the tally-stick in his hand, and she watched him take in a woman in a grey cloak with blood on her glove and a thin man with his hair standing on end, and she saw him say something very short to the boy at his elbow. The boy turned and ran toward the watchmen. Rowan put the stick in his coat and started for the stairs.

"He is coming," said Hamo. He laughed, a cracked and wondering sound. "Isolde, look. He is coming up. He has seen us."

"Keep walking."

"Sixty. Sixty-one." The color had come back into his face. He was almost grinning. "I have a thing to tell him. I have been trying to say it for a year. I shall say it at the foot of the stair, in front of the whole wharf, and Pike's men may write it down."

"You may say it on the third landing, if you like."

"No. I shall say it at the foot. I think it will keep that long." He gave her his arm, as if she were the one who needed holding. "Seventy. Seventy-one."

Rowan was climbing to meet them, taking the steps two at a time, with his dark coat flying. A hundred feet below, the watchmen were forming up. The sergeant was shouting.

"Eighty."

She saw the tallow-boilers' smoke lying thin and white along the river. She saw a bargeman's red cap on a bale. She saw the window.

It was an upper window in the blank wall of a joiners' yard, on the landward side, a small square opening under the eaves with a shutter propped back, and she had passed it a minute ago and thought nothing of it. She had not looked. It was a yard of joiners, and there were a great many windows. But the shutter was open now and something black was resting on the sill, a short dark bar, and behind it, half in shadow, a pale shape that might have been a face.

"Hamo," said Isolde.

"Ninety." He did not hear her. He was looking down the stair at the Prince, who was thirty steps away and had stopped, with his hand lifted, saying something she could not hear. "Ninety-one. Ninety-two."

"Hamo, the window. Get down. Get down on the stair."

"Ninety-five. Isolde, he is here, he is almost here." He had stopped looking at her. His whole face had opened. He lifted his free arm and cried out over the water, loud, with the whole of his thin breath, in a voice she had never heard from him in forty years. "Your Highness! Your Highness, I have a thing to tell you!"

"Ninety-eight," said Hamo, to himself. "Ninety-nine."

She had her hand on his sleeve. She had begun to pull.

"A hundred," said her brother.

There was a small, sharp sound behind them, like a green stick snapped across a knee.

Hamo took another step. He took it quite steadily, with his lifted hand still open, and set his foot down on the hundred and first stone. Then something happened to his face. Pain would have been something she knew. This was surprise, a bland and puzzled surprise, the look of a man who has put his hand into a pocket for a key and found the pocket empty. He turned his head and looked at her. He opened his mouth.

His knees gave. She had him under the arms, and he was heavier than he looked, and she went down on the wet stone with him across her lap and her cloak soaking up the red. The bolt stood out of his chest under the left breast, a hand's breadth of black shaft with grey goose feathers, and there was a great deal more of it behind, she saw, because it had gone through him from the back. He was breathing in small, quick, wet gasps. He was still looking at her.

"It is all right," said Isolde. "Hamo. It is all right."

"I..." said Hamo Merecroft.

He did not say what. She waited. The gulls went round and round over the river. A long way off, a bell was still clanging, and a man was shouting, and she heard boots on the stone, running, many boots, and Rowan's voice somewhere very near. Her brother's eyes were open and the light was going out of them, slowly, from the edges in, as it goes out of a room when someone carries the candle away. She said his name again. She said it a third time.

Then he was not there.

"My lady." Someone had her by the shoulder. She looked up. It was Prince Rowan, white to the lips and breathing hard, with his coat open and the boy Tobb at his heels. Behind them Pike's watchmen were pouring up the stair with their staves and a bargeman had caught the foremost by the arm and was pointing at the window. "My lady, I am sorry. I am so sorry. Let me..."

"There is a man in that window." Her voice came out cold and steady, a stranger's. "He has a crossbow. He will be gone by the time they reach the yard."

"Pike!" said Rowan, without turning. "The joiners' yard, the third door on the landward side. Take four. Now."

She heard them go. She looked down at Hamo's face, and at the thin fair hair that had stood up behind his ear, and she put her bloody glove against his cheek and found that it was still warm. A hundred and forty steps. He had been forty steps from the wharf, and nearer still to the Prince, who had climbed to meet him, and he had gone down every one of them counting, with his arm up, shouting.

She took the paper out of his shirt.

It was sealed in green wax, with the heron pressed into it, and the wax had cracked across one corner and was stained dark. She held it out. Her hand was shaking now, a fine, fast, helpless tremor that she could not stop, and the cut across her palm had begun to burn.

"He had a thing to tell you, Your Highness," said Isolde Yarben. "He said so. You heard him." She heard her own voice break, finally, on the last words, and she did not care. "He was a year at it. I will tell you the rest."

Rowan Brennan took the letter. He looked at the heron. He looked at the man on the stone, and at the boy beside him, who had gone as white as paper and was staring at the bolt with his mouth a little open, and then at the window under the eaves, and at the long flat grey water of the Fold lying beyond it in the morning.

He did not say anything. His face did nothing at all. It was the face of a man at a counting-table who has been handed a sum that does not come out, and she understood, kneeling in her brother's blood with a hundred and forty steps of wet stone around her, that she would not have wanted to be Garrow Pell.

"Tell me," said the Prince.
