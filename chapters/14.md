ROWAN

The exchequer cloth had been black and white once. Eighty winters of candle wax, spilled wine, and clerks' sleeves had turned it the grey of old slate, and one corner was scorched brown where a clerk in Aldren VII's day had set down a taper and gone to his supper. Rowan would not have traded it for the price of a manor. Every mark the crown had taken in or paid out since before he was born had been counted on that cloth with bone counters, row by row, and there was a comfort in the rows that he could not have explained to Edwyn and had long since stopped trying to.

He came down to the counting-room an hour before the bakers lit the ovens, by the narrow stair behind the solar that only clerks and he himself used. Beneath the keep the cold was the steady, even cold of a cellar, which suited the work. One lantern burned over the long table. Master Jaspern, clerk of the tallies, was already there, because Master Jaspern had been there since before Aldren VIII was crowned and had never found a reason to leave.

"Good morrow, Prince Rowan," said Jaspern, without rising. He was past seventy and bent like a bow left strung, and he had stopped rising for anyone beneath the king some twenty years ago. "The Kelward accounts are on your left. The queen's petition silver is on your right. The thing you will want to read first is in the middle, and I would sooner you read it before you have eaten."

"Then I will read it after I have eaten, and be the better for it," Rowan said. But he sat, and drew the roll toward him, and did not send for bread.

Along the wall behind Jaspern, in racks that reached the vaulting, lay the tallies of the realm: hazel rods notched to show a sum and then split down their length. The crown kept the long half, which was called the stock, and the debtor kept the short half, which was called the foil, and no man could cut a new notch on one without the other proving him a liar. Rowan had loved them as a boy for that reason. A letter could be forged, and a seal could be copied, but a stick could only be matched.

The winter had been kind to no one's purse. Five hundred Crownfold swords under Ser Boremund had eaten their way east along the kingsroad for sixty days and reached Eastgirt, by the raven that came on the fifth of the month, with four men lost to a fever and none to the Ashen. They had cost the crown eleven hundred marks in bread, salt beef, horseshoes, and boot leather. Lord Fenlow's three hundred, promised with such a flourish in the great hall, had marched two hundred and twelve strong. His steward's letter blamed the rest on a flux. Rowan had written back to ask which men, by name, and had not yet had an answer. He did not expect one.

All of that he had known before he sat down. What Jaspern had set in the middle of the table was something else.

It was the quarter's roll of loans enrolled by the moneychangers of the river stairs. By a rule of Aldren VII, made after a Dorsen lord pledged the same harvest to three creditors in a single season, any loan above a hundred marks had to be written down before the crown's clerk, with the lender, the borrower, the surety, and the stated purpose. No one read the rolls. They were kept because the rule was kept. Rowan had been waiting for this one since the summer, when Master Barrow of the river stairs had told him, with great courtesy, that it would come at midwinter and not a day sooner. Jaspern had marked one line with a sliver of vellum.

Rowan read it. Then he read it again, more slowly, with his finger under the words as he had done at nine years old.

*Barrow and Wend, of the river stairs, to Garrow Pell, factor to Lord Fenlow of the western timber lands: four hundred marks in silver, Lord Fenlow's seal for surety, repayable at harvest. Purpose: timber bought in the Eastverge.*

"Four hundred," Rowan said.

"In silver, not in a bill," said Jaspern. "Pell always takes it in silver. A careful man."

"Timber in the Eastverge."

"There is none, Prince Rowan. Scrub and thorn and the odd gully-willow. They burn dried dung in the hearths at Eastgirt, and think themselves lucky to have it." The old clerk folded his spotted hands. "I thought you might like to know that a man bought four hundred marks' worth of it."

Rowan sat back. He did what he always did when a sum came out wrong, which was to go through it backward. The date on the roll was ten weeks before the raid on the outer watch. Ten weeks was enough for a man on good horses to ride east by the north road, to spend a fortnight finding the sort of men who would be paid to tie a scrap of purple cloth to a dead watchman's post, and to be somewhere else when the horn sounded at Eastgirt. Four hundred marks was a manor's price. In a country where a single mark bought a sword-hand's winter, it was a band of them for a summer and some to spare.

It was also, he told himself with the care he gave to every conclusion he liked, nothing at all. A timber factor might have been cheated. A timber factor might have bought from a Kelward man who lied. The silver might have gone to a hundred places, and a man who went to the king with half a sum was a man who would be asked for the other half.

He said a word under his breath in Aurouvian. It was one Edwyn had taught him when they were boys, and it concerned moneylenders and their mothers.

"Prince Rowan?" said Jaspern mildly.

"Nothing." Rowan lifted the vellum sliver from the roll and laid it in his own palm. "Bring me the Callenby and Elmrick lines for the same quarter. And Master Jaspern, there is no reason for a word of this to leave the room."

"There never is, Prince," said Jaspern, and went to the racks.

It was a quiet business, and it was his. No one had ever told him so. The Hand's seat had stood empty since the old king died, and the work of it had flowed to the counting-room as water finds a cellar, and he had taken it up without being asked because someone had to. The seals, the tallies, the harbor dues, the tithe-rolls, the men-at-arms' pay: it was done, and it was done right, and that was as good as being thanked. Better. A man who needed to be thanked was a man who could be managed.

He did not want the crown. He had never wanted the crown. He wanted the ledgers to be correct, and he wanted the people who used them to know whose hand had made them so. It did not seem to him a great deal to want.

* * *

Since the birth, the king had held his council in the queen's solar, and the lords had grumbled about it and come.

Rowan was the last to arrive, having stopped on the stair to be certain that his face held nothing. It was the fortieth day after the lying-in, and by evening the queen would be led out of her chamber in the sight of the court and blessed in the sept-yard, but for now she sat in her carved chair by the hearth with a blanket over her knees and a wet-nurse named Nan hovering near the cradle. The cradle held two boys. They lay head to foot, as they had from the first, each with a scrap of ribbon at the wrist, and from across the room Rowan could not have said with any confidence which was which. He did not try. He had looked at them on the morning after the birth and had felt something he had not expected, which was relief. The law no longer ran to him. Whatever else the winter had done, it had lifted that particular stone from his chest, and he had been ashamed at how light he felt.

"Sit," said Rodrik. The king stood by the window with his hands behind him and the grey light on his face. He looked as he had looked for forty days, which was tired past the point of caring and happier than Rowan had ever seen him. "We are all here, and I want this done before the feast. Orston."

The Master of Whisperers spoke from his corner in the soft voice that made men lean toward him. The Ashen were snowbound beyond the Redcut, as were Simon Kelward's men on theirs, and neither would move before the thaw. Ser Boremund's five hundred had been received at Eastgirt with much cursing and much relief. Prince Gareth had taken Thorn up into the passes to watch the Ashen roads and had sent word that he would keep them until the thaw, and wanted no one's opinion on the matter. Princess Elyra, by the last raven, was well and had written that if her brother did not fly east by the first melt she would come and fetch him herself.

"Duke Simon's leg is knitting," Orston finished. "He now walks on it, and curses at it, and I am told the two activities are not unconnected. There is one more thing. The shepherds above the Redcut speak of a great shadow on the north crags at dusk. I have set it down with the sea-serpents and the ghost of the Vine Shore, since shepherds have been seeing shadows on crags since there were shepherds."

"Let the shepherds see what they like," said Rodrik, and Rowan saw the king's eyes go, briefly, to the window and the north, and then away again. "Rowan."

He gave the treasury as he would have given it to no one else in the room: flatly, in order, with the largest numbers first. The crown could feed five hundred swords through the spring. It could not feed a thousand, and Duke Simon's last letter had hinted at a thousand. The Vine Shore tithe, due at harvest, stood at twenty-three hundred measures, of which nothing had arrived. "Duke Roland wrote from Rosegate that he would be here by the feast with grain," Rowan said. "He has been writing the same line since the first frost. If he is truly at the south gate by evening, I intend to meet him there with a counting-clerk and a rod."

"You intend to meet a duke who has ridden two hundred miles in the snow with a counting-rod," Edwyn said from his place by the cradle, where he had been sitting with one boy's fist wrapped around his finger and a look of idle contentment. "Rowan, this is why the Vine Shore loves you."

"The Vine Shore is forty days late, Edwyn."

"The Vine Shore is a hundred wagons from the south gate," said the queen, and she said it quietly, which was how she spoke when she meant to be obeyed. "Rowan. I would ask you to receive him at the feast as a guest, and count his grain in the morning. A man who is welcomed in the hall is easier to deal with in the counting-room than a man who is counted at the gate."

"If he is hoarding, Your Grace, he will tell us nothing either way."

"If he is hoarding, he will tell us nothing at the gate and be insulted besides. If he is not, we will have insulted an honest duke for the sake of a clerk's rod." She did not raise her voice. "Hospitality first. Figures after."

Rowan looked at the king. Rodrik had turned from the window and was watching his wife with an expression Rowan had seen only a few times, the expression of a man who has found the right answer in the mouth of someone he loves. "The queen is right," Rodrik said. "Hospitality first."

"As Your Grace commands," said Rowan.

He said it without heat. Elyn, he thought, was right perhaps nine times in ten, and that was the trouble with her, because it was the tenth time that would cost the realm, and she would never know it had been the tenth.

"Edwyn," said Rodrik. "You have been at the feasts all winter. The queen sent you there to listen. What have you heard?"

"A great deal, and most of it about myself." Edwyn eased his finger from the infant's grip, very gently, and the boy frowned in his sleep. "Callenby is in debt to three houses and is sulking. The second Elmrick girl is now said to have her eye on a Kelward cousin, which means she has looked at every unmarried man in Foldhall and found us wanting, and I am wounded. The Vine Shore lords who came to Foldhall at midsummer are still here and have no reason to be, which I find interesting. And Lord Fenlow, who drinks deeply when he thinks he is among friends, told me last week that the Eastverge would need a great deal of timber when it was done burning." He turned his cup in his long fingers. "He laughed when he said it. A man's own mirth is the worst witness against him, I find. He said war was the best friend a timberman ever had."

There was a short silence. Ser Corren, by the door, shifted his weight. Maester Osmund looked at his folded hands.

"Fenlow says a great many things when the wine is in him," said Rodrik.

"He does," said Edwyn. "I thought the king might like to know which ones."

Rowan kept his face as it was. It was not difficult. He had practiced it since childhood, in the days when it was the only defense against Edwyn, and it held now, but he felt the sliver of vellum in his sleeve as though it were a coal. Timber, and the Eastverge, and Fenlow's mouth. Edwyn had come at the same thing from the other end and had not the least idea what he had handed over.

"Brother," Edwyn said, and Rowan looked up to find the dark eyes resting on him with gentle interest. "Your face just did a sum."

"I have a tooth," Rowan said.

"You have a good many things, and I have never once seen you complain of a tooth." Edwyn smiled. "It is a great comfort to me, that you are so easy to read."

"Enough," said Rodrik, but without the snap that was in it when he meant it. "I ride when the passes open. Ser Boremund has the swords. Hart is fit and fat and wants only a reason. Until then I am a husband and a father, and if any man in this room has a matter that cannot wait a month, let him say so now."

No one spoke. Rowan thought of the vellum in his sleeve and of how it would sound in this room, in the hearing of the queen and the Maester and a man who was called the Master of Whisperers. *Lord Fenlow's factor bought timber in a land without trees.* Fenlow was a baron with three hundred men and half the western forests. A king who accused him on such a sum would have an enemy at his back for the rest of his life, in a council where he could not afford one. And if it was nothing, the crown would have called a great lord a traitor for buying firewood.

It would be tidier to know first. Much tidier. He could ask his questions quietly, through Jaspern and the moneychangers, while the king was in the east, and lay the whole sum before the council when it could not be disputed. It was the right course. It was also, he noticed with some distaste, the course that would show to the most advantage who had found it.

He said nothing.

* * *

The hundred wagons came in at the south gate an hour before dusk, in a thin sleet, with Varen banners hanging sodden from their poles. Rowan went down to the yard himself. He took no clerk and no rod. He told himself that this was obedience.

Roland Varen, Duke of Rosegate, was already out of his saddle when Rowan reached the gate, and already laughing at something the gate-warden had said. He was a broad, handsome man in the middle of his years, with chestnut hair going to silver at the temples and a wine-dark cloak lined with marten, and he wore his house on his doublet: a white rose on purple, its stem wound about with a grapevine worked in green thread. His laugh arrived a moment before he did. It was the warm, unguarded sort that made a cold yard seem a little less cold, and Rowan, who had no such laugh, disliked him at once and was annoyed to find that it was not the sort of dislike that lasted.

"Prince Rowan," said Roland. "I was told you would meet me with a counting-rod. I see you have come without one. Is that a kindness or a threat?"

"The queen has asked that you be received as a guest," Rowan said. "You are very welcome at Foldhall, my lord."

"Elyn said that, did she? She is a wise woman. She has always known that a man will pay a debt sooner for being asked than for being counted." Roland clasped his forearm, heartily, and held it a moment longer than Rowan liked. "And you are wondering whether I am a thief."

"I am wondering how many measures are on those wagons."

"A hundred wagons, twelve measures to the wagon, every sack sealed with my own wax. Count them if you like. I expect you will, tonight, when you think no one is looking." Roland's eyes, which were the pale brown of weak ale, were smiling, but there was something behind the smile that was not. "Twelve hundred measures, Prince Rowan. You are owed twenty-three hundred. That is eleven hundred short, and I am here to tell you so to your face, because I would rather you heard it from me than from your clerks."

Rowan had not expected that. He stood in the sleet and let the sum settle. "It is a large shortfall, my lord."

"It is. And I will tell you where it went, because I have had a winter to learn it." Roland lowered his voice, though no one was near. "The rains came wrong in the Vine Shore this year. Half of the store-barns at the river ford flooded before the harvest, and the grain in them turned. That is eleven hundred measures, and I have the ruined sacks to show you, and a man from every one of my villages who will swear to it. But I will tell you what else. There were letters that came to me at midsummer, from lords of my own shore, who had been in Foldhall and who wrote to say that the crown was bleeding silver into the Eastverge and meant to take the Vine Shore harvest to pay for it. They urged me to keep my grain home. To hold it for our own defense."

"Which lords?"

"Three. I burned two of the letters. I am sorry for it now." Roland smiled thinly. "I kept the third, because the hand was a good one, and I like to know my enemies' penmanship. You shall have it. I also have a piece of purple cloth that a Kelward raven brought me from Eastgirt, in a little packet with a note from Duke Simon asking whether I had any explanation for why my colors had been tied to a dead man's gatepost." The smile went out. "I replied that I was not such a fool. I am still a little angry that I was asked."

"The cloth was never believed to be yours, my lord," said Rowan. "The king said so the day it came. Princess Elyra proved it was a forgery."

"Did she? I had not heard. No one thought to write and tell me." Roland looked at him, and Rowan had the uncomfortable sense of being measured. "You have a great deal of the crown's work on your shoulders, Prince Rowan. It is widely remarked. Every great house in the realm knows that if they want a thing done in Foldhall, they write to you, and the king signs it afterward. They have been saying for a year that the Hand's chair has been empty and that there is a man doing the work who will not be paid for it."

"The king has not seen fit to name a Hand," Rowan said.

"No. I suppose the king has not been asked." Roland said it lightly, as a man might remark on the weather. "If I were you, I should ask him. A king is a busy man. He cannot be expected to know what a clerk wants without being told."

"It is not a thing one asks for," said Rowan.

"On the contrary, it is exactly what one asks for. I asked my father for Rosegate, when he had six other sons and no reason to give it to me. It was the best day's work I ever did." Roland clapped him on the shoulder, and before Rowan could decide whether to be angry, he had turned to shout at a teamster about a loose axle. The laugh came back. "Count my wagons after the feast, Prince Rowan. I shall be in the hall with a cup in my hand, telling lies to your brother. You will know where to find me."

Rowan stood in the yard while the wagons rolled in, one after another, white-rosed sacks stacked in their beds like the sacks of a siege-wall. He counted them anyway. A hundred. Twelve hundred measures, as promised. It was the first time in a winter of the Vine Shore's delays that anyone had told him a number and been right.

He had come out to catch a hoarder and had been told a truth instead, and handed a letter, and asked a question. It would be a long while before he stopped turning that question over.

* * *

The great hall that night held four hundred and had room for no more. Torches burned in every sconce, and braziers stood between the tables for the cold, and the smell of roast goose and spiced wine hung in the rafters like woodsmoke. The lords of the Crownfold had come in their furs, the Vine Shore lords in their velvets, and the queen's Rathen cousins in the midnight blue of Stillford. A dozen of the gentlemen wore small scraps of ribbon pinned to their cloaks. It took Rowan a moment to understand that they were blue or white, and another to understand why.

Lord Elmrick wore blue. Baron Callenby wore white. A Merelands knight at the third table wore both, one on each shoulder, and was being laughed at for it. Rowan did not laugh. Two infants lay in a cradle in the queen's solar, sleeping off the first milk of the evening, and already grown men were wearing colors for them, as though the cradle were a tourney field. It was a jest. It was the sort of jest that, in a realm with a living king and a healthy queen and two sons in the cradle, could do no harm. He noted it all the same, as he noted everything, and did not like where his mind put it.

He had been seated at the high table, at the queen's left hand, not at her invitation but at her insistence. "Sit with us, Rowan," she had said, in the tone she used when she had decided to be kind. "I will not have the steward of the realm hiding at the lower table with the gate-wardens." He had sat. He had thanked her. And he had felt, as he thanked her, a small, cold certainty that he was being given a seat in place of a title, as a man might be given a cup of wine in place of a wage. It was unkind of him to think so. He thought it anyway.

The queen was brought out with the ceremony that the old custom demanded. She came down the stair on the king's arm in her midnight blue with the silver circlet on her dark hair, pale from forty days indoors, and the hall rose as one and stayed standing until the Maester had said the words of blessing. Then the boys were carried in.

There were two nurses, and each carried one. They came down the length of the hall between the tables, past four hundred faces, with Ser Corren walking before them and two more Kingsguard behind. The boys were swaddled in Norvalen white fox and the green of the Crownfold, and each wore his ribbon: blue at the wrist of the one, white at the wrist of the other. A murmur followed them. It was not a cheer. It was the sound four hundred people made while they studied two children and failed to find a difference.

Rodrik took them from the nurses himself. He stood at the head of the hall with a son on each arm, broad and tired and grinning like a man who has won a war at the cost of a night's sleep, and the boys regarded the roof beams with identical expressions of deep suspicion.

"My lords," said the king. "These are my sons, and Elyn's. Edric and Harwin, by the blue and the white, and I am told by every man in Foldhall that I must say which is the elder." He waited for the laughter. It came, uneasily, and he let it die. "I cannot. I was not there. I was a thousand miles away in the snow, and I came six days too late for it. If any man in this hall wishes to tell me, I will hear him. But I will warn you that the queen was present, and she cannot say, and the Maester was present, and he cannot say. They are my sons. They were born on the same night of the same mother. I am quite content to call them both the first."

"Well said, Your Grace," said Roland Varen, from the third table. He had raised his cup, and he was looking at the infant with the white ribbon, and something crossed his open face and was gone. "A white ribbon, I see. The color of Rosegate's rose. We are honored."

"It is an accident of the sewing basket, my lord," said Elyn, smiling. "I am told there was no other reason."

"There is always another reason," said Roland, and drank.

The gifts came after, as they do. A cup of green Vine Shore glass from Roland, so fine that it rang when flicked. A pair of silver spoons from the Norvalen, sent on Osric's behalf, carried by Rhaenaya. A cradle of carved Western timber from Lord Fenlow, long and wide enough to hold two, with a pair of Nyxen wings worked into the headboard, so beautifully that the hall gasped as it was carried in. Fenlow himself bowed very low beside it, in his green velvet, with that smile that never reached his pale eyes. "A poor thing, Your Grace," he said, "from a humble house. But timber is what House Fenlow has."

"It is a magnificent gift, my lord," said the king.

Rowan looked at the cradle. He looked at the wings, and the careful grain of the wood, and the man who had sent it. It was a very good cradle. He thought of a factor named Pell, and of four hundred marks in silver, and of a land with no trees.

Edwyn sang after the meat.

He did not ask for silence. He never did. He stood, took up the lute that a page had fetched him, and plucked a single chord that cut through the noise of the hall like a dropped blade, and four hundred conversations stopped at once. Rowan, who disliked songs because they could not be tallied, found as he always found that he could not look away.

It was a good song. It was also, Rowan realized about halfway through the second verse, an extremely rude one. Edwyn sang of a king who flew three days through a blizzard to meet two sons he had not known he had, and he sang it so well that the queen's hand went to her mouth and the king looked suddenly at the ceiling. And then, having brought the hall to the very edge of tears, he turned the verse on its hinge and rhymed *hearth* with *Fenlow's girth*.

The hall roared. Lord Fenlow, who was a slender man and had no girth to speak of, went red to the ears and then, to his credit, laughed along with the rest, though the laugh was thin. The queen was crying with laughter into her napkin. Rodrik, by the high chair, put his face in his hand, and said something very low in Aurouvian that made Rowan's mouth twitch before he could stop it.

"He said he would not have it rhyme with *hearth*," Elyn gasped, when she could. "I said he would find something worse."

"It is worse," said Rodrik, into his palm. "It is so much worse."

Edwyn bowed to the hall, and then bowed separately to Rowan, with the lute held against his chest. "For the steward of the realm," he called, "who has calculated the cost of this song and finds it an extravagance."

"I find it a loss," said Rowan, and the hall laughed again, louder, and he understood that he had just made a joke. He did not know how. Edwyn grinned at him, wide and unguarded, for one instant, and for that instant Rowan thought he understood why the whole court loved him.

It was a small warmth. He held it carefully while it lasted.

* * *

The counting-room was colder at midnight than it had been at dawn, and quieter, and the lantern he lit showed the same grey cloth and the same bone counters as always. He had left the hall early, and no one had remarked on it, because no one ever did.

He counted Roland's wagons by candle again, from the tallies that Jaspern's boy Tobb had made at the gate, and found them correct. He laid the quarter's roll open at the marked line beside the letter that Roland had pressed into his hand as they left the hall, and he read the letter, and did not like the hand. It was a round, hurried script with a long tail on every g, and he had seen it before. He did not yet know where.

He took a small book from the drawer of the table. It was bound in plain brown calf and had a lock, and in six years it had held nothing but the sums he did not wish anyone to read. He opened it to a fresh page. He wrote, in a small, upright hand: *Pell. 400 marks. Ten weeks before the raid. Timber in the Eastverge. Fenlow's seal. Callenby and Elmrick rolls, same quarter. The letter from Rosegate, the hand.* Beneath it he wrote: *Edwyn heard it from the other end. He does not know.* Then, after a moment, he wrote: *Not yet.*

He was blotting the page when the door opened.

It was the king. He stood in the doorway with a lantern of his own and the hall still on his clothes, goose fat and wine and woodsmoke, and his ash-charcoal hair pushed back from his face in a way that Rowan remembered from when they were boys and the king had been a prince who could not sleep. Behind him, in the passage, Ser Corren waited like a statue with its back to the wall.

"I thought I would find you here," said Rodrik. "I went to your rooms and the bed had not been touched."

"It is hardly midnight, Your Grace."

"It is well past, and you know it." The king came in, set his lantern on the table, and lowered himself onto the clerk's stool on the far side of the cloth. It creaked. He looked around the room slowly, at the racks of tallies and the grey counting-cloth, as though he had not stood in it for years. "I forget how much of the realm is down here. Father used to say that the only true throne in Foldhall was a stool in the counting-room, and that the man who sat on it was the only man who had ever really ruled. I never knew what he meant until this winter."

"He was being unkind about the Hands," said Rowan.

"He was being accurate." Rodrik smiled tiredly. "You were brilliant tonight, Rowan. You took a duke's grain and did not insult him, and you let Elyn overrule you and did not sulk. I saw both."

"I do not sulk, Your Grace."

"You do not. That is why I noticed." Rodrik rubbed his face with both hands. "I want to say something before I fly. I have been meaning to say it since the birth, and I have not had the room. I have been a poor king this winter, Rowan. I have been a husband and a father and I have been looking east at a border I cannot reach. The realm has run because you have run it. I know that. I want you to know that I know it."

Rowan sat very still.

It was an opening. He saw it at once, as he would have seen a flaw in a column of figures. The king had come alone, at midnight, with no witnesses, and had said the words that a man says when he is about to offer something. There was a sentence in Rowan's mouth, fully formed, and it had been there for more than a year, and he had rehearsed it in the counting-room and on the stair and in his bed. *Name me your Hand.* Four words. He had only to say them.

"Is there anything you need from me before I go?" said Rodrik. "Anything at all. You have my ear and my seal. Ask, and it is yours."

*Name me your Hand.*

He could feel the words. He could feel the shape they made in his mouth, and the small, cold silence that would follow them, and the look that would come into his brother's face: not anger, nor refusal, but the open, startled look of a man who had not thought of it. He would have to explain. He would have to say why. He would have to say *I have done the work for a year and no one has named it,* and it would sound exactly like what it was, the complaint of a man who felt he was owed a thing and had waited to be given it. He could not bear to say it. A brother who had to ask was a brother who had not been seen.

"Two hundred more wagons from the Vine Shore, Your Grace," he said. "And a written warrant for the moneychangers of the river stairs to open their books to the crown's clerk. I would like to know who has been lending to whom."

Rodrik laughed. It was the old laugh, the rough tired one that Rowan had known since childhood, and it was very close to being fond. "That is not what I meant. But it is very like you." He stood, and the stool scraped, and he laid a hand on Rowan's shoulder as he passed, heavy and warm and entirely without calculation. "You shall have the warrant. We are at war now, and the king's hand will serve alone. Write it tonight and I will sign it on the morrow. And Rowan."

"Your Grace?"

"When I come back from the east," said the king, "there is a matter I would see settled. It has been on my mind since the coronation. I will say no more until I have something to say."

"When you come back, Your Grace," said Rowan, "the Vine Shore will still owe us eleven hundred measures."

Rodrik shook his head, and he was smiling. "Eleven hundred measures," he said again. "I shall take it up with them myself." He took his lantern and went out, and Ser Corren's shadow detached itself from the wall and followed, and the passage was quiet.

Rowan sat for a long time with his hands flat on the cold grey cloth.

*There is a matter I would see settled.* He turned the sentence over, and then again, and could not decide what it meant. It might have meant anything. It might have meant the Hand's seat, or the Varen grain, or the naming of an heir, or a whim. Kings said such things to be kind. They meant nothing by them, and then they flew east, and then they did not come back, and the matter stayed unsettled for ever.

He took up the small brown book and turned the key in its lock. He put the key in his sleeve. Then he drew a fresh roll of vellum toward him, and trimmed a quill, and began the warrant for the moneychangers of the river stairs, in a small and careful hand, with every mark correct.
