HARRUN

The lamp on Harrow's gate-tower was lit an hour early, and Harrun took it for a kindness.

He saw it from the top of the long slope above the ford, a small yellow point against a sky the color of wet slate, a mile off on its rise with the dark bulk of the holdfast heaped behind it. Seventeen days out of Eastgirt, nine of them in rain, and he had not seen a light that looked so much like a hearth since the morning he marched.

"Sir," said Wilm, at his stirrup. "Is that it?"

"That is it."

"Is it ours?"

"It is a Kelward lamp, and a Kelward cook will have a Kelward pot on the fire under it, so for tonight it is ours." Harrun shifted in the saddle. The old scar behind his left ear had begun to ache an hour back, as it always did in wet weather, a dull cord pulled tight from ear to jaw. "Tell Dorran to stop humming. The prisoners have heard that song nine times."

"He is not humming, sir. That is the cart."

It was the cart. It came down the slope behind them with one axle shrieking, drawn by two sorry grey horses and driven by a carter called Ketch, who had sworn at the road for seventeen days with great skill and no repetition. Five men sat in the bed with their ankles chained to an iron bar. They wore the dull white bone plate of the Ashen no longer, only the sacking they had been given at Eastgirt, and they had stopped looking at the road on the third day. The oldest of them, a lean man with a scar down his cheek that was a cousin to Harrun's own, had asked on the ninth day whether Crownfold bread was truly white. Harrun had given him a heel of it. He had not known why. The man had held it a long time before he ate.

Wilm fell back to give the order. He was a lad of nine and ten from the lower town at Foldhall, with a long nose and a tuneless voice, who had been in the king's twenty because his mother had told the sergeant that he needed filling out. He had filled out some. He had also learned to sleep sitting up in a saddle and to bury a man, and there was less of the boy in his face than there had been in summer.

Harrun counted what he had. He did it without deciding to, the way other men breathed. Himself. Wilm, Dorran, Carr, and four more in green and gold, which made eight of the twenty who had walked out of Foldhall's gate in high summer. Two Kelward riders on bronts, lent by the Dowager Duchess with her own seal on the order: Ansel, a grey, silent man of forty who rode a russet beast called Plough, and Gedd, a boy with a laugh like a goose, whose bront was named Burr. Ketch. Five in the cart. Seventeen, with the five.

He remembered the Duchess in the cellar at Eastgirt, three days after her son was buried under a cairn he had never seen. She had come down in the grey of the morning with the key on a cord at her neck, a tall, spare woman with her father's brown eyes and no tears left in them, and she had counted the five prisoners aloud, one by one, as a steward counts sacks. She counted them again at the cart. "I have lost a husband and a son to this road," she had said, and her voice was perfectly level. "You will bring me no more reasons to count, Captain." He had bowed. He had not trusted himself to say anything that was not stupid.

At the gate that morning there had been a boy with rust-colored hair and a thick neck, holding a knight's horse in the press, a Fenlow lad who had come up from the Redcut with Ser Boremund's squires. He had watched the cart load with his mouth a little open. Harrun had noted him as he noted everything, and thought only that the boy had the look of a lad who had seen the inside of a fight and had not liked what was in it. Then he had forgotten him.

"Bronts don't like the ford," said Ansel.

The Kelward rider had come up on his left without a sound. His beast rumbled low in its chest, a long, soft, rolling sound that Harrun felt in the saddle-bow, and its great russet head turned from the road to the willows and back.

"Wet feet," said Harrun.

"He has had wet feet for nine days. He does not mind wet feet." Ansel's grey face was calm and watchful. "He does not like the alders."

Harrun looked at the ford.

It lay at the bottom of the slope, twenty paces of shallow brown water running over a stone bed, with the road dipping into it on this bank and climbing out of it on the far side toward the holdfast. Alders crowded both banks, black and dripping, their lower branches hanging low enough to brush a rider's hat. The light was going. A rook went over, and then there was nothing, no second rook, no wren in the hedge, no sound but the cart and the water.

He did not like it either. He had not liked a place since the morning at the outer watch, when he had counted Mart's girls on his fingers and found there were only two.

But the lamp was a mile off, and the prisoners had not slept dry in nine days, and a column that sat down at the edge of a ford at dusk had asked to be looked at. He knew that too.

"We cross quick and keep going," he said. "Ansel, you and Gedd first, up the far bank, and sit there where you can see the road. Wilm, the cart behind them. I will bring the rear."

"Sir," said Ansel.

"What is it?"

"Nothing." The grey man turned his bront. "A beast has a right to an opinion."

* * *

They went down into the ford in the order Harrun had given, and for a time nothing happened at all.

Plough and Burr climbed the far bank in two great lunging strides, throwing water, and stopped at the top with their riders black against the sky. The cart went in with a shriek and a jolt, the grey horses straining against the cold, and Ketch swore at them in a low, steady, comfortable voice. Water rose to the hubs. The prisoners drew up their feet. Dorran came after on his bay, the older man's long face blank with weariness, and then Carr, and then the others in a file, and Harrun last, sitting his horse in the shallows with his hand on his sword and his eyes on the alders to his left.

He heard nothing. He would remember that afterward, that there had been no warning at all, no twig, no cough, no horn.

There was a flat, ugly sound, like a hundred hammers coming down on a hundred boards.

Dorran said something. It sounded like the start of a joke. The quarrel took him in the throat above the gorget and came out behind, and he went over the side of the bay without finishing it, and the water closed over his grey head, and his face came up again, open-eyed.

"Down!" Harrun was shouting before he knew. "Down, get down, the bank! The bank!"

The grey horses screamed. One went to its knees in the traces with three bolts in its neck and the other reared and came down across it, and the cart slewed sideways on the stone bed with its tail swinging toward the downstream bank. Ketch was gone. He had been on the board, and then the board was empty. The five in the cart were shouting, a high, ragged, terrified noise, with their chained ankles dragging at the bar, and the second volley went into them.

He saw it. He would see it for the rest of the night and for a good many nights after. Three of the five went back against the rail with the quarrels in them and did not move again, and a fourth folded over his own knees, and the old man with the scar sat in the middle of it with a bolt through the meat of his shoulder and his mouth wide open, looking at Harrun.

"They are shooting the cart," said Wilm, somewhere, in a high strange voice. "Sir, they are shooting the cart."

"I see it."

"Why are they shooting the cart?"

He had no answer. They did not take the horses. They did not take purses or swords. They had come out of the dark on both banks at once, thirty men, forty, men in rags and pale bone-colored plate with their faces smeared grey with ash, and they were wading into the ford with long knives in their hands and crossbows slung behind them, and every one of them was making for the cart.

"Cart first!" a voice called from the left bank, hoarse and quick. "Cart first, then the rest! Finish the cart!"

Harrun heard it. He had a moment to hear it. The Ashen speak slow and thick, and the Eastverge drawls; this voice had the flat, clipped vowels of the lower town at Foldhall, which he had heard all his life, which his own mother had spoken, and he turned his head toward the sound and thought: *that is a Crownfold man in Ashen ash.*

Then the first of them reached him.

He did not remember the first killing. His body did it, as it had done it at the Redcut, as it had been taught in a yard at Foldhall thirty years ago, and his horse screamed under him and went down, and he was in the water to his waist with the cold biting through his mail. A face came at him with a knife. He put the sword through it. The face went away. Another came, and he beat the blade aside and took the man under the ribs, and felt the weight of him on the steel, and shoved him off. Carr was somewhere to his left, shouting. Wilm was behind him, with his back to Harrun's back, as the sergeant had taught him in the first week, and Harrun could feel the lad's breath going in and out in short sobbing gasps.

"Keep your feet," he said. "Do not stand still. Do not look at the bank."

"Sir."

"Wilm. Keep your feet."

"Yes, sir."

Above them the far bank shook.

It was Plough. Ansel had turned him at the top of the slope and put him down it again, and the Kelward beast came into the ford like a landslide, russet and enormous, his single horn lowered and the thunder rolling out of his chest, and the men in the water scattered before him as quail scatter from a plow. Ansel did not shout. He sat low in the saddle with a long mace in his fist and his grey face quite empty. Plough took a man on the horn and lifted him clear of the water and flung him, and Gedd came behind on Burr, laughing the high mad laugh of a boy who had never been in a fight and had found he liked it.

For a breath it was turning. Harrun saw it. The knives fell back from the cart. The line on the left bank thinned and broke, and a man in ash-grey rags went crawling into the alders with a quarrel in his thigh, Wilm's quarrel, and Harrun marked the place as he marked everything and lost it in the next breath, because the next breath held the cart.

"The old one!" It was the scarred prisoner, screaming from the bed, with blood coming out of his sleeve. "Captain! The chain! Cut the chain, they are killing us!"

It was not his task. He had been given a first order and a second, and neither of them had named a murderer with an Ashen scar. But Harrun had given the man bread, and the man was alive, and the order had been *kept alive.* He went across the ford with his sword up, through water that dragged at his thighs, and reached the bed of the cart. The old man was holding up his ankle with both hands. The iron had cut to the bone.

"Hold still."

"He said it was a Crownfold man," the prisoner gasped. "The first one. The one on the north road. It is the same voice, Captain. That is his voice, the one on the bank. I would know it in my grave."

"Hold still!"

He raised the sword and brought it down on the ring.

The cart moved.

It was the grey horse in the traces, the one still living, the one with the three quarrels in its shoulder. It made a last convulsive heave against the weight of its dead brother, and the whole bed lurched up on its downstream wheels and came over. Harrun saw the iron bar swing. He saw the old man's face, very close, with its mouth open. Then the cart came down on both of them, and the cold closed over his head.

* * *

It was very quiet under the water.

He had expected a roar. He found a strange green hush, with the light of the last of the sky laid over it in a long wavering sheet, and a sound far above him like men shouting through a door. A weight lay across his legs. Another lay across his chest. He tried to push, and his hands met wood, and the wood was the underside of a cart-bed, an arm's length above his face with a hand's breadth of air-less water between.

He counted. It was the thing he did.

*One. Two. Three.*

The old man lay beside him with his eyes open. He did not move. A trickle of red came out of his mouth and went up in a thin ribbon toward the wood, and Harrun understood without surprise that the bar had broken him across the back. It seemed a hard end for a man who had asked about the bread.

*Eleven. Twelve.*

His chest had begun to burn. He pushed, and the weight did not shift. The chain was still on the old man's ankle, and the ring he had cut was only one, and there were four more, and the iron bar held the whole wreck down across him like a hand.

*Twenty.*

He thought of Edda. It came to him very plainly, with no effort, as a thing set down in front of him on a table. She was standing in the door of the bakehouse in the lower town with flour to her elbows and her hair coming down, shouting at a boy about an oven, a tall woman with a Kelward nose and a Kelward temper, who had once thrown a loaf at him across a room and then wept about it for an hour. She was a very bad cook of everything but bread. She had said, on the morning he left, *you will come home with your head on your shoulders or I will go and fetch it,* and she had said it into his collar so that he would not see her face.

*Thirty.*

Nessa had asked him for a bront horn. She was six. She wanted one to hang on her wall, a real one, as tall as a man. He had told her that a bront wore its horn for as long as it lived, and that she would have to wait until one died, and she had said that was a very long time to be without a horn. He had a little carved bront on a string in the bottom of his saddle-bag, whittled from a paring of horn by an old man at the Redcut who had said, with great dignity, that it was the best he could do at short notice.

*Forty. Forty-one.*

The light was going out. It went from the edges of the sheet, slowly, as a candle burns toward its middle. He thought that he ought to be more afraid, and he found that he was only tired, and a little ashamed, because he had been told to come home and had very nearly done it.

*Forty-two.*

Something struck the cart.

It struck it from the side, a vast dull blow that came down through the wood and the water and into his bones. The weight on his legs shifted. It shifted again, and rose, and in the green dimness above him he saw a long pale curve come in beneath the bar, a thing as thick as a man's thigh and as long as his arm, and heave. The cart came up out of the water an inch, two inches, a hand's breadth, with the iron groaning, and hands closed on his collar.

He came up into the air with a sound that was half a cough and half a sob. He lay on the stones in a foot of water with his face up and the sky going round, and someone was striking him on the back, hard, over and over, and brown water poured out of his mouth and nose in a great hot flood.

"Breathe," said Wilm. He was crying. "Breathe, sir. Breathe, damn you, breathe."

"I am," said Harrun. "Stop hitting me."

"You were under so long."

"How long?"

"I counted. I got to sixty."

"I got to forty-two." He rolled onto his side and was sick again, and the world steadied by a degree. "I lost count. Who lifted it?"

"Gedd's beast." Wilm gave a damp, helpless laugh. "He put the horn under the bar and heaved. Gedd said it was the first thing Burr had done all year that he had been asked to."

He lifted his head.

The ford was full of the dead. They lay in the shallows and on the stones and against the ruined cart, grey-faced and grey-rags, and a great many of them wore the rough ash on their cheeks and had good boots beneath. The water ran red and brown around them. Plough stood at the lip of the far bank with a long dark wound in his flank and his head hanging, and beside him Ansel lay on the gravel with a quarrel in his chest and his mace still in his fist and his eyes shut. He had not said a word of complaint. Burr stood over him, whining in his throat like a dog.

"Ansel," said Harrun.

"Dead, sir. In the first rush. I did not see." Wilm wiped his face with a muddy sleeve. "Carr is dead. Dorran. Peck, and Tam Rowe. Ketch, I do not know, he went into the water and I have not found him. The cart. The old man. All five of them, sir. All five."

Harrun turned his head and looked at the upturned bed and the thing beside it that had been a man with a scar. He did not feel anything yet. That would come. He knew its habits.

A horn sounded from the holdfast.

It was a deep, braying Kelward horn, taken up a moment later by a second, and then the great iron gate of Harrow, which had been shut against the dusk, ground open on its chains, and torches began to pour down the slope like a spilled sack of coals. He could hear them coming. A drum of hooves, and the long, rolling thunder of bronts, and a man's voice shouting orders that ran down the hill ahead of the men.

"They are coming," Wilm said. "Sir. They are coming. Thank the gods, sir, they are coming."

"Aye."

"Can you stand?"

"In a moment."

The attackers were gone. He listened for them, because it was his trade to listen, and he heard the last of them going, a ragged drumming of feet on the north bank and then a clatter of hooves in the lane beyond the alders, fading away to the north, far and then farther. Then there was nothing but the water, and the pop of the torches, and the great beasts breathing.

Wilm had got an arm under his shoulders and was trying to heave him up the bank. He was a lad of nine and ten who weighed less than the mail on Harrun's back, and he got him as far as the gravel at the top of the shallows, and there Harrun put out a hand and stopped him.

"Let me sit."

"Sir, the wall. We should get under the wall."

"It is three hundred paces."

"Sir."

"They have gone, Wilm. Hear it? North. They went north in a body." He leaned his head back against a flat stone and shut his eyes. His whole body was shaking with the cold, in great deep shudders that rattled his teeth, and he found that he did not much care. "Let me have a moment of this. I have not sat down since dawn."

"Your lips are blue."

"I have a good many things that are blue."

Wilm gave a gulp of laughter and sat down beside him, heavily, in the gravel. He pulled his own cloak off, and it was dark and heavy with water, but it was the only dry thing within fifty paces, and he put it round the captain's shoulders without asking. They sat side by side with their boots in the ford and watched the torches come down the hill.

"I thought you were dead," said Wilm.

"So did I."

"I mean it, sir. I stood there and held that cart off you and I thought, he is gone, I will have to tell Edda. And I do not even know what she looks like."

"She is tall, and she has a nose, and she will shout at you. Tell her I said she was not to." Harrun opened one eye. "She will take it badly. She takes everything badly. It is why I married her."

"Is she a good cook?"

"The worst in the lower town. I do not know how any of us have survived. But her bread is the best that was ever baked, and I will not hear otherwise." He smiled, with the bones of his face aching. "I have a girl, you know. Nessa. She is six. She wanted a bront's horn for her wall."

"Sir, a bront's horn is as long as..."

"I know how long it is. She was told to wait until one died." He looked at the great russet shapes by the far bank, the dead man and the wounded beast and the weeping boy. "I have a little one in my saddle-bag. Carved. An old man at the Redcut did it for me, and I will give it to her and say that I fought a hundred Ashen for it, and she will believe me."

"You did fight a hundred."

"Forty. Perhaps thirty. Do not tell her."

They sat there. The first of the torches had reached the foot of the slope. He could hear the Kelward voices calling to each other, and the creak of mail, and a man's voice, rough and carrying, shouting *the ford, the ford, hold the torches up.* A dozen horsemen were splashing into the shallows, with the light running red along their helms.

"I was afraid," said Wilm, very low. "In the water. I want you to know. I was so afraid I could not feel my hands."

"That is how you know you have the stomach for it."

"Is it?"

"It is the only way. A man who is not afraid is a man who has not understood what he is looking at." Harrun let his head fall back against the stone. The sky above Harrow's tower had gone from slate to deep violet, and the lamp burned in it, small and yellow, the one kindness on the whole of the dry road. "Your mother said I was to fill you out."

"She did."

"You have filled out."

"Thank you, sir."

"It was not a compliment. It was an observation." He laughed, and it hurt his ribs. "The king gave me two orders, you know. In the yard, the day we marched. I was to see that Mart's girls ate. That was the first. I have half done that, and the Duchess will do the rest. And the second..."

"What was the second?"

"That I should come home." He felt himself smile. He felt it all the way down, past the cold, past the ache, a warm, foolish, enormous thing that he had not felt since the lamp on the hill. "I told him I preferred that one. He laughed. I think he did not believe me."

"You are going to do it, sir."

"I am going to do it. It is a mile to the gate and sixty days of road and a loaf of Edda's bread, and I will be insufferable about it for the rest of my life."

"You will."

"I will tell it at every feast. I will tell it until you are all sick of me."

Wilm was laughing now, and crying, and wiping his nose on his wrist. The horsemen had reached the cart. One of them had thrown down his torch and was kneeling by Ansel, and another was shouting for a litter, and the noise of the ford had changed from the noise of a fight to the noise of a thing being gathered up. Harrun turned his head to look at them, and in the corner of his eye, against the black of the alders on the north bank, something moved.

It was very small. It was a man sitting up. He had come out of the scrub on his hands, a thin ash-faced shape with one leg dragging, the man with the quarrel in his thigh, and Harrun saw him and knew him, and knew what he had forgotten. He held no knife. A long leather cradle dangled from one hand, swinging in a slow loose circle.

*Wilm,* he thought. He did not say it. He had time to think it, and it was a long time, as these things go, a whole sentence in which he saw the man's arm, and the first lazy turn of the strap, and the stone in it that was dressed smooth and round. He thought that he should have taken the lad's advice. He thought that it was only a mile.

There was a small, flat sound, like a hand slapped on a table.

It did not hurt. That was the strange part. It was a light, sharp tap against the side of his head, as though Wilm had flicked him with a finger, and the sky above Harrow's tower went suddenly very bright and very close, and the lamp on the gate swelled until it was the whole of the dusk. He thought: *that is the lamp, it was lit an hour early.* He thought that he must tell Wilm. It seemed a long way to go for a joke.

He tried to say that he was coming home.
