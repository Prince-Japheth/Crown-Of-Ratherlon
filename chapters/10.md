SIMON

Gaffer would not lift his left forefoot, and Simon Kelward said so to the whole paddock in a voice that sent three grooms and a goat scattering.

"He is lame," said Bront-master Hale, without looking up from the hoof. Hale was the oldest man in the Redcut garrison, bald as an egg and bowlegged from sixty years on the ground beside beasts that stood higher than his head. "He is not lame. He is sulking. There is a stone in the cleft of the frog the size of my thumbnail, and he has known it was there since noon and decided to tell me by standing on it."

"Then take it out."

"I am taking it out, my lord. It would go quicker if you stopped telling him how much you love him."

"I did not say I love him. I said he is the worst animal in the Eastverge."

"That is how you say it." Hale got the pick under the stone and levered. Gaffer, who stood eight hands above any horse in the realm and had a single iron-capped horn as thick as a man's thigh, turned his great russet head and regarded the duke with a small, patient, murderous eye. A rumble started low in the barrel of his chest. It rolled out along the paddock rail and into the dusk, the long deep thunder that had given the beast its name, and every bront in the pen answered it.

Simon put a hand on the broad nose and let the animal blow into his palm. His glove was buff leather, scored and stiff from two weeks of wear. The other hand was bare to the wrist, and the side of it was stained grey-blue from the muster rolls, a scholar's stain on a soldier's arm. Bronts did not care for gloves. They liked the smell of a man.

"They are restless," Hale said, and dropped the stone into the dust. "All of them, since the sun went. They have been turning their heads east, my lord, like hounds at a door. I have known them do it before a storm, and I have known them do it before a raid. I have never known them do it at both."

"What is it tonight?"

"Neither. There is no wind. There is no smell of rain. They are listening." The old man straightened with a grunt and wiped the pick on his apron. "I would put an extra watch on the horse-lines, my lord. If they bolt, I do not want to be the one who explains it."

"You will have it."

Simon walked the paddock fence in the last of the light. Nine hundred bronts moved in the long pen behind it: a russet field that shifted and lowered and stamped. The horns stood above the herd in a thin forest of iron caps, catching the red of the sky. Past them lay the yard of the Redcut, and past the yard the gully walls, the colour of old blood, and above the narrows on the north wall the watch-stone, a squat block tower with a lamp already lit in its top.

He had known the place since he was a boy. His father had brought him here at six, put him on the parapet with a wooden bront-goad in his fist, and told him to count the fires on the road. *If you can count them, you can beat them,* Jonos had said. *It is when you stop counting that you are lost.* Simon had counted forty. His father had laughed and said the Ashen had never lit forty in a night in his life, and the boy had realized afterward that the old man had lit most of them himself, in a pit beyond the road, so a child on a wall would have something to see.

He did not count the fires tonight. Dunn would do that. But he looked east, because the bronts were looking.

* * *

Sergeant Dunn was waiting at the foot of the watch-stone stair with a wax tablet and an expression of long-standing disapproval.

"Six hundred and twelve," he said, before Simon could speak.

"You counted them twice."

"I counted them three times, my lord. The first time I got six hundred and nine and thought I had missed some. The second time I got six hundred and fifteen and thought I had counted a star." Dunn was a square, grey man with a limp and a knuckle missing on his left hand, and he had held the Redcut watch for twenty-two years under two dukes. He tapped the tablet. "Six hundred and twelve, near enough. Five to a fire, on the road they use. Three thousand."

"And the pikes?"

"They stood down at dusk. Ranks of forty, twelve deep, and they marched off the plain into their camp as neat as a barn dance. I have seen them do that twice before. They are not tired. They are rehearsing."

They climbed. The stair was cut into the wall itself, and the red stone was still warm from the day. At the top the guard changed, and a boy of eighteen straightened up from the parapet with a round shield on his arm and a bandage on his forearm that had been fresh the last time Simon had seen it. He had a smear of soot across his nose, and he grinned at the duke with all of his teeth.

"Fenn," said Simon. "How is the arm?"

"Better, my lord. It itches. Sergeant Dunn says that means it is mending." Fenn jerked a thumb toward the plain. "My lord, is it true what they say? That the Princess's beast could pick up a bront in its claws and drop it on the Ashen?"

"Who says so?"

"Everyone, my lord."

"Then everyone has been drinking." Simon looked at the boy's shield. The paint was chipped to the wood on the left side, and someone had mended the rim with a strip of green cloth: Harrun's colours, begged from one of the king's men in exchange for a pair of boots. "Lark has not picked up a bront. She does not need to. She opens her mouth, and a hundred men fall over."

"I would like to see that."

"You will." He clapped the boy on the shoulder. "When the horns go, you do not stand on this wall, Fenn. You go down and take the rear gate with the men from the king's household. The rear gate. It is the quietest post in the Redcut, and I want a boy there who can count to ten and shout."

"My lord, I would rather be on the wall..."

"The wall is for those who are not mended. Go and tell Harrun I sent you."

Fenn went, grumbling, down the stair with his chin up, and Dunn watched him with an expression Simon had seen on the old man only twice before, both times when he spoke of his sons.

"He asked after the Princess's beast three times this week," Dunn said. "I have told him it is not a pet."

"It is a little like a pet."

"It is a little like a mountain, my lord."

Simon walked the parapet alone then, to the place where the wall met the tower. The stone there was older than the rest, and cut deep with names: lords and watchmen, captains and boys who had died holding the Redcut, scratched in by their friends with knife-points and chisels, a long ragged litany running up the red block into the dark. He did not need the lamp. His fingers found the place without help.

JONOS KELWARD. DUKE.

The letters were square and deep. They had been cut by the stonemason of Eastgirt, at his widow's order, a month after the First Ashen War, and the mason had not been told the whole of it. Simon had been told. He had been eighteen and a hundred yards behind his father when it happened. The bolt had been as long as a man's arm, and it had come out of a dead gully on the left of the charge with a sound like a plank dropped on a floor. It had been meant for Oak. It had struck an old man in a russet saddle instead, and gone through his breastplate and the horn of his saddle, and he had ridden another thirty paces with his back straight and his mouth open, as if he had been about to say something and had decided to say it later.

Simon laid his palm flat against the stone. He had come here every night for ten years, and he had not said any of it aloud, and he did not say it now. He stood with his hand against his father's name until the cold began to climb through the glove. A man could be a duke at eighteen. It was a great deal harder to feel like one at twenty-eight, standing before the only man he had ever wanted to be.

"It is a good wall, Father," he said at last, quietly. "I have not let it fall."

"My lord," said Dunn from the stair. "The lamps."

* * *

The council met in the lowest room of the watch-stone, a square, bare chamber with a table cut from a single plank of bog-oak and a map of the Eastverge scratched into the wall in lime. They sat as they had sat for ten nights. Harrun at the foot, with his scar and his green-and-gold surcoat, drinking small beer as if it were a duty. Alton at the duke's right hand, tying and untying a leather thong on his wrist. The Princess Elyra on a stool by the door, her boots up on a crate and her tawny-brown braid coming loose, with a heel of bread in her fist and the air of a person who had slept in her clothes for a fortnight and had no intention of apologizing for it.

Prince Gareth sat on the floor.

He had said he did not need a stool. He sat with his back against the wall and his knees drawn up and his thin first beard catching the lamplight, and he was looking at the map as if it were a puzzle he had been set. He was fifteen years old. Simon had thought it a dozen times in ten days, usually as a surprise.

"Three thousand," said Simon. "With bone plate, pikes in blocks of five hundred, archers behind, and the thing that follows on wheels that none of my scouts will describe."

"They are rehearsing," said Harrun. "Dunn is right. They have walked off that plain in perfect order every night for a week. A band that means to fight does not rehearse the walk home."

"Then it is a feint."

"It is a question." Alton's voice was dry. He had been saying it for days. "They are asking what we will do when they come. They have counted our bronts. They know where the Princess sleeps and which way her beast faces when the wind turns. They have burned a cart a night and watched who rides out to see." He paused. "I think they have learned the answer, Simon. I think they have decided the Redcut is not the door."

"Then what is?"

"I have not found it." His brother's face was pale and quiet in the lamplight. "That is what frightens me."

Elyra spoke from the door. "It is the pikes."

They looked at her.

"It is the pikes at dawn," she said. "I have said it six nights running. Your bronts cannot go through a hedge of pikes set into the ground. If they come on in blocks of five hundred, you have to charge them or lose the mouth. And if you charge, you lose half your bronts." She swallowed bread. "So you do not charge. You wait. Lark and Gareth and I go up and open the blocks, and you ride through the gaps. It is the only plan I have, and it is a bad one, and I will say so again if you like."

"It is a good plan," said Simon.

"It depends on a roar."

"It depends on two roars."

"And on the pikes being where we expect them. And on the Ashen not having read the same books I read at sixteen." Her mouth twisted. "I told you about the ropes."

"You did. I have forty men with long axes in the rear lines whose only task is to cut a loop if it comes." Simon laid his gloved hand flat on the table. "I am not a clever man, cousin. I am a duke with nine hundred bronts, and I would sooner go through a hedge than around it. But I have fought on this road since I was a boy, and I will tell you something I know. They will not hurt us if we hold the narrows. They cannot get a hundred men abreast through the Redcut mouth. All I need is for the pikes to break, and for the Eastverge to do what it does."

"Which is?" said Gareth.

It was the first thing the boy had said all evening. He did not look up from the map.

"Charge," said Simon. "Very loudly, with a great deal of horn."

Harrun laughed. It was a short, rusty, surprised sound, and it eased something in the room. Alton smiled. Elyra shook her head. Even Gareth's mouth twitched.

"The Seam," said Dunn quietly, from the stair door.

Everyone turned.

"I have been meaning to say it, my lord. The Dry Seam. The goat-track up the south wall, behind the holdfast." The sergeant shifted his weight onto his good leg. "Your father kept a post there. Forty men, the first year. Then twenty. I kept six, until the spring. I took them for the levy, begging your pardon. You asked for every man I could spare."

"It is a goat-track, Dunn."

"It is, my lord."

"It is the width of a man's two shoulders, and it winds like a rope. An army could not go through it in a month." Simon looked around the table. Alton had gone very still. "Is there a reason you are telling me this tonight?"

"No, my lord." Dunn's face was flat. "Only that you said to tell you what I took away."

It was a long moment. In the corner, the lamp hissed. Simon felt Alton's eyes on him, and he felt his own jaw tighten, because he knew exactly what a good captain would do. He would send six men back to the Seam in the morning. He would send twelve. He would then have twelve fewer men on the wall, and the Ashen would see it, and he would have given them the very gap he feared.

"Send two men in the morning," he said. "Two. They are to climb as high as the rim, and tell me what they see. If they see a single camp-fire, I will move a hundred."

"Yes, my lord."

"Two men, Dunn. Not six. Not twelve."

"Yes, my lord," said Dunn, and went out without another word.

* * *

It was Cora who wrote to him, and she wrote at length, in the large, slanting, impatient hand of a woman who disliked paper and used it anyway.

*My dear husband,*

*The harvest at Eastgirt is in, and it was as bad as you said it would be, and I have sold the east pasture to the Hallows to pay for the oats. Do not shout. It was the only sensible thing to do. Your mother says I should have asked her. I did, and she said sell the pasture. I think she wished to see whether I would.*

*I am told the wool shirts you took are not the wool shirts I packed. I have found them in the press. You are, I think, wearing linen in a gully in the Eastverge in the autumn. Your mother has sent a boy with the wool shirts and instructions that he is not to return without a note from you saying you are wearing them.*

*It is very quiet here. I did not know a house could be so quiet. I am cross with you for going and cross with myself for being cross, and I find that I talk to your dog.*

*Come home when you can. Do not be noble.*

*Cora*

Simon read it by the single candle in the little chamber off the stair, where a plank bed and a wash-stand had been put for the duke's use. He read it twice. Then he sat for a time with it open on his knee and his boots off and his bare foot flexing slowly on the cold stone, and the thing in his chest that had been clenched for nine days eased by a degree.

A boot struck the door, once, with the confidence of a man who did not wish to be mistaken for a servant.

"Come in, you ass."

Alton came in with two cups and a stoneware jug. He set them down on the plank. "Your mother's boy has arrived. He is in the guardroom eating all of Dunn's cheese. He says you are to wear the shirts."

"I have heard."

"He also has a letter for me." Alton held up a small, tightly folded square. "I have not read it. I know what it says. It says, *Do not marry the Princess.*"

"Mother would not say that."

"No. She would say, *The Princess is eighteen and the king's sister and I should be very glad to see you do something sensible with your life, Alton.* Which is the same, only longer." He poured. "I am told you have been telling Harrun I am infatuated."

"I have said nothing of the kind. I said you ride beside her with the air of a man who has found a mountain and been told he may not climb it."

Alton laughed, quietly. He sat down on the end of the plank and took a long drink and looked at the candle for a time, and then at the letter from Cora on his brother's knee.

"You should go home, Simon," he said.

"I am where I am needed."

"You are a fool who has been up for nine days."

"I am the Duke of Eastgirt."

"Yes." Alton set the cup down. His voice had changed. "That is exactly the trouble. If I could take it from you, I would. I would sit on that wall in your place. I know the stones better than you do, and I have a better head for the tally. But it is your name over the gate, and it was Father's before you, and they will follow you." He turned his thumb along the rim of the cup. "I would be a worse duke than you. I would just be a better wall."

It was the most that his brother had said to him in years. Simon looked at him, at the long, lean, dark, careful face with the thong of leather on its wrist. Four and twenty. He had been fourteen when Jonos died. Simon had been the one who had stood in the yard, in a dead man's hall, and told a boy of fourteen to stop crying and be a Kelward.

"I was wrong to say that," Simon said, in a rough voice.

"Which?"

"In the yard. When Father died."

"Oh." Alton picked up the cup again. "You were eighteen. You did what you could." He drank. "I have not forgotten it, and I do not hold it against you. But I would like it noted that I was fourteen and did not stop crying until the end of the week, and I think that was the right amount."

They sat in silence for a time. The candle guttered. Somewhere far above them, on the wall, a guard coughed. Simon found that his hand had closed around the letter, and he opened it, carefully, and smoothed the creases out against his knee.

"Wear the shirts," said Alton, getting up.

"I will wear the shirts."

"And Simon."

"What?"

"Do not be noble."

"That is what she said."

"I know," said Alton. "That is why I wrote it on the back of my own."

* * *

It was past the middle of the night when he found them.

He had gone down to the paddock behind the farrier's, because he could not sleep, and because there was a Nyxen there that he had not yet looked at properly. It was a long, empty, starlit space, swept bare, with a few old trees and a trough and a pile of straw. There were no gates. He had been told they did not put gates on Nyxens, and he had not believed it, but there were none, and the two great beasts lay in the open with their wings folded and their heads at ease.

The tawny-brown one, Lark, lay on her side in the straw with her long neck stretched along the ground. The Princess Elyra was asleep against her ribs, curled up like a boy with her cloak pulled over her face and a hand tucked under her cheek. She was muttering. He could not hear the words, but there was a rhythm to it, a long thin rope of sound that went up and caught and went up again, and he stood very still at the edge of the paddock and did not breathe.

*Ropes,* he thought. *She is dreaming of ropes.*

A little distance off, against the flank of the big grey Nyxen, a smaller shape had curled itself into the hollow where the foreleg met the barrel.

Simon crossed the straw as quietly as a heavy man could. The boy was asleep. He lay on his side with his knees up and his mouth a little open, and one hand had closed on a fistful of grey fur. His beard was a thin dark shadow on his jaw. His boots were still on. He had put a saddle blanket over himself and it had slipped, and the night was cold enough that his breath showed in the starlight in small even clouds.

*He is fifteen,* thought Simon. *He is fifteen, and he flew three days across half a kingdom because his sister wrote a word on a scrap of paper, and tomorrow I am going to ask him to open his mouth and do the worst thing a person can do.*

Not his mouth. The beast's. It made no difference. He stood there with the blanket in his hands, and a feeling came over him that he had not had since the winter his father died. He had been commanding a wall of grey sergeants and boys with soot on their noses. He had been doing it for nine days and had not once stopped to look at it. He was a duke of the Eastverge, with nine hundred bronts and a wall, and half the Redcut was older than his father and the other half younger than his brother. He thought of Fenn, with his green cloth and his soot. He thought of the boy in his hands.

He laid his own cloak over the sleeper, tucked it at the shoulder, and stepped back.

An eye opened above him. It was dark and large and wet and perfectly awake, set in a long grey head the size of a cart-horse, and it regarded the duke for the space of three breaths. Then the great beast lowered his chin an inch, as one lowers a lance in salute, and shut the eye again.

"Thank you," said Simon, very quietly, to the dark.

He did not say anything more. He went back across the straw and out through the gap in the fence and up toward the wall.

* * *

The fires went out an hour before dawn.

He was on the parapet. He had come up to relieve Dunn, who had refused to be relieved, and the two of them stood side by side at the edge of the dark with their cloaks pulled round them, looking east. Six hundred and twelve little red points lay along the plain in the long curve of the dry road. They had burned there for ten nights, steady and small, like a necklace laid on black cloth.

"One," said Dunn.

Simon did not ask what he meant. A fire in the middle of the line had gone dark.

"Two."

Another. Then three together. Then a whole run of them, a score at a time, winking out along the road from the east end to the west as though a hand were passing along a row of candles. Dunn stopped counting. He leaned his weight onto the parapet and his knuckles went white. The red points died in a long flowing wave that came toward the Redcut across the plain, and then there was nothing at all: only a deep and silent blackness beyond the gully mouth, with the stars standing over it.

"My lord," said Dunn, and his voice was very low. "They have put them out."

"Yes."

"A man does not put out his fires when he is going to bed."

"No."

Behind them, in the paddock, nine hundred bronts began to rumble. It started with one, a long low note, and the second took it up, and then the whole herd, thunder in the dark, rolling out across the yard and the gully and up the red walls of the narrows. It shook the stone under Simon's boots. It was the sound that had given the beast its name, and it had never been so loud.

A horn answered it from the east.

It was a deep note, a long, ragged, braying call, and it rose out of the blackness beyond the gully and swelled until it filled the whole sky. A second joined it. A third. Simon stood with both gloved hands on the stone and his heart going very slowly in his chest, and he found that he was counting. He counted the horns. He counted until there were too many.

"Dunn."

"My lord."

"Sound ours."

"Yes, my lord."

"Wake the Princess. Wake the boy." He turned from the parapet, and his father's name was under his hand as he went, cold and square and deep in the red stone. "And tell Hale that the horn-lines are to be loosed on my word, and not a breath before. Tell him he may say he loves them."

Dunn put the horn to his lips. Below them, in the grey, the whole Redcut began to wake.
