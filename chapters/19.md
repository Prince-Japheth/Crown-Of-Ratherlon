WYNN

The pup had stolen Lord Tomas's hat, and neither of them was willing to admit defeat.

Wynn found them outside the nursery after the first feeding. The hat lay between the pup's forefeet, wet through and flattened almost beyond use. Lord Tomas Brennan stood over it with a pear in one hand and the patient expression of a man offering terms to a lesser lord. He was round about the middle, stooped in the shoulders, and dressed in a brown riding coat with one button missing. His hair was plain brown too. In the sun it showed a little warmth, but none of the iron sheen Wynn saw in the king's.

"I have offered her breakfast," Tomas said. "She has declined. This is a serious disagreement."

"She has had breakfast, my lord."

"Then I have offered her a second breakfast. I should have thought that strengthened my case."

The pup put her chin on the hat.

Wynn crouched beside her. Sand-tawny fur stood in damp tufts along her shoulders, and the faint stripes showed where she had rubbed herself against the wet grass. He worked his fingers beneath her jaw, lifted, and drew out the hat before she could seize it again. She sneezed against his wrist.

"There," he said. "You might have asked me."

"And deprive myself of the chance to succeed?" Tomas took the hat with two fingers. "I have been Warden of these roosts for eight and twenty years. I ought to be able to recover my own clothing."

He put it on. Water ran down behind his ear.

Wynn tried to keep his face straight and failed.

"You may laugh," Tomas told him. "Oren has been doing it since I came through the gate."

The Warden had ridden from Greywater the previous morning, following the upper Fold road south for a day to reach the castle before supper. Wynn had heard the horse coming into the yard and had been too busy with the nursery drain to go and meet him. He had meant to apologize. Tomas had arrived with a basket of apples for the handlers, eaten a bowl of their stew, and fallen asleep beside the loft fire before Wynn finished.

Now the apples were gone, the hat was ruined, and the pup had a pear.

"She has grown," Tomas said, watching her take it.

"She grows every time I carry her."

"Then stop carrying her. You will bankrupt us."

The nursery yard lay below the east bays, where the morning sun reached the grass but the wall sheltered it from the wind. For most of the winter Wynn had brought the pup out only when the paths were clear. Now she could run from the lean-to to the far wall without falling into snow. She had learned to spread her wings when she jumped, and sometimes she came down so lightly that the grass beneath her scarcely bent. Other times she landed with all her weight and looked offended by the result.

She finished the pear, shook out her wings, and sprang for the trough.

"Mind the edge," Wynn said.

She landed beside it instead of upon it and splashed water over her feet. Tomas stepped back just in time.

"There is sense in her," the Warden said. "She merely keeps it for herself."

Above them a hammer struck wood, three quick blows and a fourth. Then someone shouted, and the hammer stopped.

Wynn looked toward the eastern range.

Oren stood at the loft hatch with one hand against the frame. A carpenter knelt beside him. Beneath them the long grey mother in the end bay had raised her head from the trough and was watching the men overhead.

"My lord," Wynn said. "You had better come."

* * *

The carpenter had taken the old peg out, and half the beam had come with it.

He held a piece of blackened wood in his palm while Oren leaned over his shoulder. The sound outside was wet and steady: meltwater dripping from the upper rock onto the roof and running through a seam in the boards. Wynn had heard it for a fortnight. He had stuffed the seam with wool and put a bucket beneath it. The bucket had needed emptying twice a night.

He had not known what the water was doing inside the timber.

"How far?" Tomas asked.

The carpenter pushed his knife into the beam. It went in to the hilt.

"That far, my lord. And along it farther than I can tell you without taking the floor up."

Oren said a word he did not use in front of the young handlers.

"I have slept on that," Wynn said.

"You will sleep somewhere else tonight," Tomas told him.

The loft ran across three bays, with sleeping places above each and a narrow passage connecting them. In winter the men kept their tools beneath the cots and their boots beside the lantern, where they might dry. Under the floor lived animals that could tear a man apart with one turn of the head, and Wynn had never once feared sleeping above them. He had feared a careless boy with a candle. He had feared a rope left across the stair. He had not thought to fear the beam beneath his bed.

The carpenter pointed at the joint above the end bay.

"We can shore it for the morning. I would not leave anyone underneath while we do it."

The grey mother looked up again. She was unbonded, broad across the chest, with white fur beneath her chin and a habit of taking the mash bowl between her teeth if the handlers were slow. Her ears twitched toward the dripping water.

"Brisa," Wynn called down. "Feed her in the orchard. Take the barrow all the way out."

Brisa looked up from the yard. "She has nearly finished this one."

"Give her another. Lord Tomas will put it in the book."

"Lord Tomas will certainly put something in the book," the Warden said.

The old mother followed the barrow as soon as Brisa lifted the handles. She went down the slope at her own pace, stopping to nose a patch of grass. Two smaller unbonded adults rose from the sun beside the wall and went after her. There was no command in it. There was fruit in the barrow, and room to lie in the orchard.

Wynn carried the tools out of the loft. Oren tried to lift his own cot and found that his knee would not bear it.

"Leave that," Wynn said. "I will fetch it."

"I have carried my bed before."

"And now I am carrying it. You may tell me I am doing it wrong."

Oren moved aside with a grunt. Wynn took the cot down the narrow stair and set it against the wall beside the others. The pup watched from the nursery gate. When he brought his blanket out, she seized the trailing corner and pulled until he let it go.

"I know whose room this will be," Tomas observed.

The carpenter and his men began fitting the shores. Wynn stayed at the foot of the stair, holding the lantern high enough for them to see beneath the joint. Each blow of the mallet made loose grit fall from the ledge overhead.

"We need oak," the carpenter said when the first prop stood. "A whole length, sound through the middle. Those boards in the stores will not serve."

"There are beams at the lower yard," Wynn said. "I saw them come off the barges."

Oren looked toward Tomas.

The Warden had taken off his wet hat. He turned it between his hands.

"Yes," he said. "There are."

* * *

Six days had passed since Hamo Merecroft died on the river stairs, and nobody at Foldhall went down to the wharf without a reason.

Wynn had not seen the killing. He had heard the watch bell while he carried water, and later a boy came up from the kitchens with the news. By evening every handler had a different account. One said the lord had been struck on a barge. Another said Prince Rowan had caught the quarrel in his hand. Oren had told them to stop repeating things they had not seen.

What Wynn knew was that the lower yards had watchmen at their gates, and the tally for the roost timber had not come back.

"Master Ralf brought the lengths in with the first barges," he said. "They were ours before the river froze. I saw the mark."

"I know," Tomas said. "I went to Rowan this morning."

"Then he will let us have them."

"He will let us have what is ours when he has established which lengths were paid for and which were pledged twice. Fenlow's factors used the same yards. Some of the goods bear one mark, some another, and some have had the marks cut off."

Wynn looked up at the empty loft.

"The beam will not last while they count."

"No," Tomas agreed. "Which is why I have sent for Ralf and the carpenter's roll. We shall give Rowan something he can count."

The Warden crossed the yard to a bench beneath the wall and sat down with his account book on his knees. Its cover was patched in three places. Wynn had seen him carry it for years and had never known him to open it without finding some little scrap tucked between the leaves: a feed tally, a drawing of a wing joint, a note about a pup that had coughed. Today he drew out a strip of linen on which someone had sewn the roost's timber mark in black thread.

"That is ours," Wynn said.

"It is what we paid the men to cut. It is not proof of what they delivered." Tomas put it on the bench. "Fetch me the receiving book. And the split tally from the stores."

Wynn hesitated.

"What is it?" Tomas asked.

"If it is Fenlow's wood, will you send it back?"

"If it is another man's wood, I will buy it from the man who owns it. I shall not take it because our roof is bad." He looked up at Wynn. "Would you have your wages kept because a lord needed a new roof?"

"No, my lord."

"Neither would I. I am fond of a roof. I am fonder of being paid."

Wynn fetched the book.

The receiving marks lay on the first winter page, in Brisa's broad hand. She had entered fourteen oak lengths and the name of the carrier, but no day of delivery. The next page held pears, oats, six new troughs, and a complaint about a carter who had brought straw full of thistles.

"Fourteen," Wynn said. "That is the number I saw."

"Then we have the number," Tomas said. "Now we need the day and the man who can swear to it."

"Brisa took them in."

"Brisa took in the fourteen that went into our stores. Those are the shorter lengths. Ralf says the long ones were left at the wharf until the cliff road dried. We must not make two deliveries into one merely because we would like them to be."

Wynn felt his face grow hot. The Warden's voice was mild. That made it worse.

"I will ask her," he said.

"Ask her what she remembers. Give her room to say she does not."

* * *

Master Ralf came up from the timber yard before noon, with wet boots and a shaving caught in his beard.

Wynn knew him from the summer repairs. He was a Deepwood man, broad in the wrists, with a left thumb that would no longer bend. Three men came behind him carrying tools wrapped in sacking. One bore a long measuring rod. Another had a roll of drawings beneath his arm. The youngest was eating an onion as though it were an apple.

"You have a bad beam," Ralf said after the carpenter showed him the joint. "You have had a bad beam since the old king was alive. Someone has been painting over it."

"That someone may have been me," Oren said.

"Then you have a good hand for paint."

"I thank you."

"I did not mean it kindly."

Tomas laughed and asked them to sit. Wynn brought bread and cheese from the handler's room, and the youngest man put away his onion long enough to take both.

"Men of Timber and Caliber," Ralf said, when Tomas thanked them for coming. "My father called us that. You may tell the queen we have arrived."

"I shall tell her you have eaten half the roost's cheese," Tomas replied. "She keeps a close account of such things."

Ralf grinned. "We give honest measure, my lord. Straight wood, men who know their work. She could hire worse."

"She could," Oren said. "She has."

The Warden laid the receiving book and the stitched mark before them. Ralf looked at both, then shook his head.

"Those fourteen were the trough boards. I took them into the stores myself. The long beams are from Alderfall, cut under Fennick's leave and sold to the crown before midsummer. We brought them north through the Deepwood lanes to the upper Fold, then down by raft. The first thaw let us finish the passage."

"How long on the river?" Tomas asked.

"Eleven days on the moving water, with four nights held above the bend for ice. We put in on the night before Lord Merecroft was killed."

Wynn remembered that night. He had carried a lantern down to the orchard gate and watched the first barges come beneath the bridge. The river had sounded different with the ice gone, fuller and louder against the piers.

"I saw them," he said. "I did not go down."

Ralf drew something from inside his coat: a narrow piece of hazel, notched and split along its length.

"The factor gave me this when the crown paid the first half. Prince Rowan's clerk has its fellow. The timber is ours until the second half is paid, but we agreed the lengths could be used at once. There is a second writing for that."

"Where?" Tomas asked.

"In my chest at the yard. The watch would not let me take the chest out."

"Will you go back with the clerk and show him?"

"Gladly. I asked the sergeant yesterday. He said he had orders to keep everything where it stood."

Tomas took a fresh leaf from his book and began to write.

Wynn watched the pen move. He had expected the Warden to send a servant with a demand and have the wood brought up because a Brennan wanted it. Instead Tomas asked the carpenter how many beams were needed, wrote down the lengths, and asked Ralf to put his mark beneath the account. Then he called Brisa and had her name the shorter delivery in her own words.

The boy with the onion looked toward the nursery. The pup had her forefeet on the low gate and was considering his lunch.

"May I give her some?" he asked.

"You may give her a pear," Wynn said. "You may keep the onion."

"My mother says onions make you strong."

"Then eat two," Oren told him. "That beam will not lift itself."

Tomas sanded the page. "Wynn, will you take this to the counting-room? Jaspern knows I am here. Tell him we need a clerk who can match a tally, and a watchman to stand by while Ralf opens his chest."

"And if he says no?"

"Ask what is missing and bring me the answer. We will supply it."

Wynn took the page. He understood the words on it well enough to carry them without folding it into his apron. For the first time that morning he thought the beam might be replaced before dark.

* * *

The counting-room door was open, but a guard stood before it.

Wynn reached it by the yard stair and the passage behind the lesser hall, the way he had gone with the fruit tallies all winter. It took him less than a quarter hour. He had washed his hands first. His sleeves still smelled of the pup.

"Lord Tomas sent me," he told the guard.

The man looked at the page, then at his apron. "Wait there."

Wynn waited beside the wall. Two merchants sat on the bench opposite, each with a hat on his knees. Neither spoke. Through the open door he could see Master Jaspern at the table and Tobb standing beside him with a bundle of split sticks.

The boy had not been at the roosts since the killing. Wynn had wondered whether he was ill.

He looked thinner. He was sorting the sticks into three heaps, matching a long half to a short one and setting each pair apart. His fingers moved carefully, but when a bundle slipped from the table he started so hard that Jaspern put a hand over his wrist.

"Pick them up one at a time," the old clerk said. "There is no hurry in this room."

Tobb bent and gathered them.

Wynn stood still. He had come to ask for wood. He did not know what to say to a boy who had seen a man die a few paces away.

Jaspern looked toward the door and beckoned him in.

"The Warden's handler. What have you brought me?"

"A beam, Master. Or the want of one."

The clerk took the page and read it. He read it a second time with his finger along the measures, then held out his hand for the tally Wynn carried.

"Ralf has the other writing in his chest?"

"He says he has. He will open it before your man."

"Good. Sit."

Wynn sat on the edge of the stool. Tobb glanced at him, and for a moment Wynn saw the serious child who had apologized for counting pears.

"How is the pup?" the boy asked.

"She ate a lord's hat this morning."

"Which lord?"

"Our own. He is wearing it."

Tobb's mouth moved. It was almost a smile.

Jaspern went to the rack behind him and drew out a tied bundle. He found the crown's half of Ralf's tally without looking at three of the others. When he set it against the piece Wynn had brought, the grain met all the way down, and the notches made one line.

"That proves the payment," he said. "The delivery and the permission to use it are in the writing. Hal will go with the watch sergeant. He may release the two lengths named here if the marks and the agreement match. The rest stays until we have counted it."

"May I tell Lord Tomas?"

"You may tell him exactly that. Two lengths, after the writing is examined. No more."

The door behind Wynn opened. Prince Rowan came in with Ser Colm, and the room grew quiet at once.

The prince wore plain dark wool. His hair had been pulled back and tied with black cord, and there was no ornament upon him save the small clasp at his throat. Wynn stood so quickly that his stool scraped the floor.

Rowan took the Warden's account from Jaspern. He looked at the matched tally, and then at Wynn.

"Is anyone sleeping above the damaged beam?"

"No, Your Highness. We have cleared the loft and the bays beneath."

"Keep them clear. Jaspern's order will serve for the timber. If the men find the lengths unsound, bring the report back. They are not to use bad wood because it is what we paid for."

"Yes, Your Highness."

Rowan returned the page. "The roosts still need feeding and mending. The watch has been told to preserve goods, not stop the castle's work. I will have the distinction made plain at the yards."

Wynn bowed. He had feared the prince would ask him about fruit costs, and found himself wishing he had. There was a pale mark across Rowan's knuckles, newly healed, and he kept one hand flat upon the table while Jaspern spoke to him about a rider's account from Harrow.

The names meant nothing to Wynn. He heard only that the man had left three days earlier on fresh horses, and that he would not be at Foldhall yet.

He went out before he heard more.

At the passage door Tobb caught up with him.

"May I come to the roosts when the work is finished?" the boy asked. "Master Jaspern says I may have the evening if the tallies are matched."

"Come before the last feeding," Wynn said. "There will be something left in the pot."

Tobb nodded and hurried back to the table.

* * *

They brought the beams up in the afternoon, on a cart with six men walking beside it.

Ralf carried the writing in his own hand. A watchman came with him as far as the yard, counted the two lengths, and watched Brisa enter them into the receiving book. She put down the day this time, slowly and with a fierce look at anyone who might mention the earlier page.

Then the work began.

Wynn held the lower ladder while the carpenters lifted the first beam. Oren stayed below and passed up pegs. The onion boy had been given the long rope, and each time he hauled he made a small angry noise through his nose. Ralf told him to breathe through his mouth and save the noise for supper.

Above the yard, Hart turned in a broad circle against the pale sky.

He had come home from Eastgirt before the news from Harrow, after three days flying west along the kingsroad. Wynn had helped Oren clean the travel mud from his pads and had watched him finish two troughs of mash. The king had gone up to the nursery before changing his cloak. Hart had slept through most of the next morning.

Now the deeper gold stripes showed along his flanks as he banked, and the dark mark between his ears was visible when he lowered his head toward the orchard. Wynn recognized the scar on the left shoulder even at that distance.

The king was on his back. His hair had been bound for the flight, and a short tawny tuft at the temple caught the light when Hart passed over the wall.

He did not land in the yard. There were men on ladders and loose ropes everywhere. Hart went on to the open ground beyond the orchard and settled there, while Tomas waved from the bench with his wet hat.

By the time Rodrik came through the gate on foot, Elyn had arrived from the nursery with both boys and two nurses.

The twins were four months old and did not care that a beam had been replaced. One was asleep. The other had discovered his own fist and was trying to put more of it into his mouth than would fit. Wynn could tell them apart by their ribbons and nothing else.

The queen wore her dark hair in a coronet braid beneath the silver circlet. She stopped well clear of the ladders and let the nurses take the bench under the wall.

"Tomas," she said. "I hear you have been troubling the counting-room."

"Only with things that can be counted, Your Grace. I have tried to be merciful."

"Rowan appreciates that. He sent me the account."

Tomas bowed, then turned to Wynn. "Have we got somewhere safe for them to sit? The queen has brought half her household."

"There is the orchard seat, my lord. The old mother is at the far end. Brisa is with her."

They went by the path outside the nursery fence. Wynn carried a rug and spread it over the stone seat. Rodrik took the waking boy from his nurse, held him against his shoulder, and walked beneath the pear trees while the queen sat.

The king had a faint tawny strand caught in one of his temple braids. Up close Wynn could see where it had been worked into the darker hair.

"Hart has been complaining about the ladders," Rodrik said.

"We have complained about them longer, Your Grace," Oren replied.

"Then I shall tell him he is late."

The boy against the king's shoulder made a soft unhappy sound. Rodrik shifted him to the other arm.

"That was not addressed to you," he said. "You are entirely punctual."

Elyn smiled, and the nurse covered her mouth.

A woman came down the orchard path carrying a third infant, smaller than the twins, wrapped in brown wool. Wynn had seen her arrive two days before Tomas, after a two-day litter journey from Greywater. She was the Warden's sister, and the little girl was Brenna, his niece.

Tomas rose to meet them. He put a finger beneath the child's chin and was immediately obliged to rescue his account book from her mother's reaching hand.

"I said I would read it after supper," the woman told him. "I have not accused you of stealing from your own roosts."

"That is because you have not read it yet."

"I shall begin with the hats."

"There is only one," he said mournfully.

Wynn went back for the pup. She had pulled his blanket beneath the lean-to and was lying upon it with her feet in the air. He took the lead from its peg, showed it to her, and waited until she rolled over.

"There are children," he told her. "You will mind your feet."

She followed him to the orchard at a bounding trot.

He kept her on the grass outside the circle of nurses, with enough lead to sniff the fallen leaves and no more. She looked at the king, looked at Tomas, and tried to put her nose into the basket of bread beside the queen.

"She has my family's manners," Rodrik said.

"She has your appetite," Elyn replied.

Brenna began to cry. The pup turned her head toward the noise, sniffed the air, and went back to the bread. Wynn drew her away gently.

She chose nobody. She wanted the crust Tomas had dropped, the queen's basket, and the blanket she had stolen. He gave her the crust and sat beside her on the grass.

Rodrik passed the waking boy back to his nurse and sat by Elyn. For a little while he watched Ralf's men through the orchard gate.

"Who are they?" he asked.

"Deepwood men," Tomas said. "They brought the long oak down the Fold. They knew which chest held the writing, and your brother knew which tally matched it. Between them they have allowed us to sleep without the roof upon our heads."

"Then they shall have their balance paid," Rodrik said. "Have Jaspern bring it to me with the next accounts."

"He will," Elyn told him. "Rowan has already ordered the delivery entered."

The king looked at Tomas's book, then at the damaged hat upon the grass.

"And that?"

"A disagreement over breakfast, Your Grace. Entirely settled."

Rodrik laughed. The boy asleep in his nurse's arms opened his eyes, considered the noise, and closed them again.

* * *

Tobb came before the last feeding, as Wynn had asked, and found him brushing shavings out of the nursery.

The new beam was fitted. The carpenter had left the old piece on a trestle for Tomas to inspect, and Wynn had put his fingers into the place where the wood had gone soft. It broke apart beneath his thumb. He took his hand away.

"Is it done?" Tobb asked.

"Enough for tonight. The roof boards are tomorrow's work. We sleep in the empty store-room until Ralf says the floor is sound."

"May I help?"

Wynn gave him the smaller brush.

They worked side by side while the pup tried to catch the bristles. Tobb laughed once when she seized his sleeve and pulled him onto his knees. Wynn steadied him, and the boy stayed there a moment, with one hand resting on the warm sand-coloured neck.

"She is strong," he said.

"She is. Keep your feet under you."

The pup nosed beneath his palm. He scratched her between the ears.

Oren called them to supper. There was broth with barley, half a cheese rind, and bread left from the queen's basket. Tomas sat at the handler's table with his hat drying on the hearth rail. Ralf's youngest man had found the last onion and was offering it round. Nobody took it.

Wynn set a bowl before Tobb and sat opposite him.

Outside, the old mother came back from the orchard and settled in a sound bay, well clear of the shores. Briar answered her from the western range with a high clear chirrup. Lark and Thorn were still in the Eastverge, and their bays stood empty.

Tomas asked Oren about the grey mother's cough. Oren said it was gone. Ralf asked whether the roost would buy the shorter offcuts, and Brisa said she would speak to him in the morning, when he had stopped charging her for every shaving that fell from his knife.

Tobb ate his broth. Halfway through he looked toward the nursery and asked whether the pup had a name.

"Not yet," Wynn said.

"Will you give her one?"

Wynn could hear her moving beneath the lean-to. His blanket would be full of straw again. He would have to take it back before she slept on it, or let her keep it and find another.

"The person who raises her for riding will name her," he said.

"You raise her."

"For the roosts. It is different work."

Tobb thought about that. Then he pushed his empty bowl forward and asked for a little more broth.

Wynn filled it.

When supper was finished, he walked the boy as far as the yard gate. Two watchmen stood there with a lantern, waiting to take him back to the counting-house. Tobb thanked him for the meal and went between them, carrying the brush he had forgotten to put down.

"Bring it tomorrow," Wynn called.

The boy turned and nodded.

Wynn went back to the nursery. The pup lay on his blanket with her chin on the folded corner. He sat beside her and began picking the shavings out of her fur, one at a time, while above them Ralf's men took the ladders down and carried them safely out of the yard.
