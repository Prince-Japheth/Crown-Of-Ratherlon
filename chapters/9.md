ISOLDE

The harvest feast was loud, which meant it was going badly.

Isolde Yarben sat halfway down the high table, a position that afforded her an excellent view of the king’s left profile and the exact depth of the trouble her brother was in. The great hall of Foldhall was packed from the hearths to the doors. The air was thick with the smell of roasted pork, spilled wine, and wet wool, because it had rained since noon and three hundred lords and knights had dragged the damp in on their boots.

Hamo was at the lower tables, seated between Baron Callenby and Lord Fenlow. Hamo was laughing. He had been laughing since the second course. He was a man who ordinarily drank one cup of watered wine with his supper and fell asleep in his chair, but tonight his face was flushed, his gestures wide, and he was matching Lord Fenlow cup for cup. Every time Hamo laughed, Isolde felt a cold knot tighten under her ribs.

*You are a fool, Hamo,* she thought, cutting her meat with precise, even strokes. *You have always been a fool, and now you are a frightened one, which is the most dangerous kind.*

At the center of the high table, King Rodrik sat with his left arm bound in fresh linen from the hunt, listening to Duke Roland Varen. Varen was a massive, squarely built man with a beard the color of winter wheat. He was Paramount of the Vine Shore, the richest land in Ratherlon, and he was speaking in the low, steady rumble of a man who expects to be heard.

"The mud is to the axles, Your Grace," Varen was saying. "The Rosegate carts have been stalled for three days. If we do not have a week of sun, half the late grain will rot in the fields."

"The crown's stores will make up the difference, Roland," the king said easily. "That is what the tithe is for."

"The crown’s stores are full because the Vine Shore fills them," Varen said, not impolitely, but with the weight of a fact. "If we cannot harvest, the tithe will be short. I will not let my own people starve to feed the Eastverge garrisons."

"No one will starve," said the king, and took a drink of wine.

Isolde watched them. She watched Prince Rowan, seated three places down, who had not touched his pork but was turning a silver spoon over and over in his long fingers, watching Varen’s mouth. She watched Prince Edwyn flirting with a baron’s daughter. She watched Queen Elyn, heavy with the child, resting a hand on her belly and looking out over the hall with a tight, drawn smile.

Then she looked back at Hamo.

He had stopped laughing. Lord Fenlow was leaning in, saying something softly into his ear, and Hamo’s face had gone the color of old parchment. Fenlow clapped him on the shoulder, a heavy, friendly blow, and signaled for more wine.

Isolde laid her knife down. She wiped her mouth on her napkin, rose without haste, and walked out of the hall.

* * *

The corridor behind the screens was quiet and cold. She found Dunstan waiting by the pillar where she had told him to wait. The stout old steward wore a heavy riding cloak over his tunic and held a lantern in one hand.

"Is he still drinking, my lady?" Dunstan asked quietly.

"He is trying to drink himself brave. It is not working." Isolde pulled her own cloak tighter around her shoulders. "Do you have the bag?"

Dunstan reached into the shadows and brought out a heavy leather satchel. It chinked, a dull, dense sound. Isolde had spent the morning with the Merecroft lockbox. She had taken her own widow’s portion—the silver her husband had left her before the fever took him—and every mark of Hamo’s that she could reach without waking his clerk. It was four hundred silver marks. A fortune for a knight; a very good price for a life.

"My lady," Dunstan said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "This is a bad business. We should go to Prince Rowan. Or to the Duke."

"If we go to the Duke, Hamo loses his lands for conspiring against him. If we go to Prince Rowan, Hamo loses his head for treason against the crown." Isolde took the lantern from him. "Men like Fenlow do not leave doors open behind them. Hamo said yes to a question in a closed room. The only way out is to buy the memory of it."

"And if they will not sell?"

"Everyone sells," Isolde said. "You only have to find their price. Come."

They went down the servants' stair, past the kitchens where the heat washed over them in a wave of grease and onions, and out the postern door into the night.

The rain had stopped, but the wind was coming off the river, cold and smelling of silt. The lower town was a maze of dark alleys and shuttered houses. The feast had drawn the watchmen up to the castle gates, leaving the river-streets empty, save for the feral cats that watched them pass with glowing eyes.

They walked for ten minutes, their boots slipping on the slick cobbles. Isolde led the way to a narrow passage behind a sailmaker’s yard, a place of high blank walls and deep shadows.

A man was waiting for them under the overhang of a roof.

He wore a dark doublet and a flat cap. The lantern light caught the gleam of silver buckles at his knees. Garrow Pell, factor to Lord Fenlow, stepped forward and bowed, the same small, correct bow he would have given across a counting-table.

"Lady Yarben," he said. His voice was dry and pleasant. "You are very punctual. I admire punctuality in a great house. It speaks of order."

"I am a widow of a lesser house, Master Pell," Isolde said. "I have no time for games. My brother is a fool."

"Lord Merecroft is a highly respected gentleman."

"He is a man who agrees to things when he is frightened, and forgets them when he is sober." Isolde set the lantern down on a barrel. "He agreed to lend his name to your lord's remonstrance. I know what that document is. I know it aims to starve the crown's garrisons to force the king's hand on the border taxes. I also know that if Duke Varen discovers my brother has gone behind his back, he will strip Merecroft from us."

Pell’s pale grey eyes rested on her face. "You seem remarkably well-informed, my lady. I cannot imagine where you hear such things."

"I do not hear them. I deduce them. You had my brother's bargeman drowned because he carried a letter to Rosegate."

"The river is treacherous," Pell said mildly. "Ale and wet stairs are a fatal combination."

"Stop it." Isolde gestured to Dunstan. The steward stepped forward and dropped the heavy satchel onto the barrel beside the lantern. It landed with a thud that echoed in the alley. "Four hundred silver marks. It is more than the tithe of our lands for a year. Take it, and tell Lord Fenlow that Hamo Merecroft’s name is no longer of use to him."

Pell looked at the bag. He did not touch it.

"Four hundred marks," he murmured. "A handsome sum. You are a fiercely loyal sister, Lady Yarben. Lord Merecroft is fortunate."

"Do we have an agreement?"

Pell smiled. It was not a cruel smile. It was the smile of a tutor looking at a child who has done a sum incorrectly.

"I am afraid, my lady, that you misunderstand the currency we are dealing in. Lord Fenlow does not need silver. He has forests of timber and mines of iron. What he needs is the illusion of consensus. When the Vine Shore presents its demands to the king, it must appear that every lord, from the Duke down to the smallest baron, speaks with one voice. Lord Merecroft’s name is the mortar in that wall."

"He will not sign it," Isolde said, her voice rising despite her control. "I will not let him."

"He will sign it," Pell said softly. "Because if he does not, the draft of the remonstrance with his verbal pledge attached will find its way to Duke Varen’s desk. And as you so astutely noted, the Duke will ruin him. He is trapped, my lady. That is the nature of a trap."

"I will go to the king."

"Will you?" Pell tilted his head. "And say what? That your brother is a traitor who changed his mind? The king’s justice is swift, Lady Yarben. It usually ends on a block."

Dunstan moved.

The steward had been standing silently, his face pale in the lantern light. He was an old man, stout and grey, but he had been a man-at-arms in his youth before he took up the ledger. He stepped between Isolde and the factor, his hand dropping to the hilt of the short-sword at his belt. The blade cleared the scabbard with a sharp hiss of steel.

"You will not speak to my lady in that manner," Dunstan said, his voice thick with anger. "Take the silver and go, or I will leave you in the river with the bargeman."

Pell did not flinch. He did not even look at the sword. He looked at Isolde.

"This is the trouble with loyal men," Pell said with genuine regret. "They have no sense of scale."

From the deep shadow behind the barrels, a shape detached itself. 

Isolde saw it only as a blur of motion. She heard the wet, heavy sound of an impact, like a butcher’s cleaver hitting a side of beef. 

Dunstan’s words stopped in his throat. He stood perfectly still for the space of a heartbeat. The point of a long, dark blade protruded from the front of his neck, directly beneath his chin. The blood followed it instantly, a black torrent in the lantern light, washing down over his grey tunic.

The blade vanished, jerked back into the dark. Dunstan collapsed. He did not cry out. He fell heavily against the barrel, knocking the lantern onto its side, and slid into the mud of the alley. His legs kicked twice, aimlessly, and were still.

Isolde could not breathe. She could not scream. The world had shrunk to the sound of the rain dripping from the eaves and the dark pool spreading around Dunstan’s boots. She stared at the old man who had taught her to skip stones, who had served her family for twenty years, lying in the muck like slaughtered livestock. 

The man who had killed him stepped into the edge of the light. It was the thick-necked youth with the rust-colored hair—Fenlow’s nephew from the hunt. He wiped his blade on a piece of rag, his face blank and bored.

Pell sighed. He bent down and righted the lantern.

"I am so very sorry you had to see that, my lady," Pell said, his tone entirely conversational. "I had hoped to conduct this as a matter of business."

Isolde looked up at him. Her hands were shaking so violently she had to press them against her skirts. She had thought she was playing a game of ledgers and threats. She had thought she could buy her way out of a political corner. She had brought a steward to face butchers.

"You are monsters," she whispered.

"We are practical men," Pell corrected gently. He reached out and pushed the leather satchel back across the barrel toward her. "Keep your silver, Lady Yarben. Buy yourself a new gown for the harvest feast. And when you return to the hall, I suggest you sit beside your brother and ensure that he drinks his wine, laughs at Lord Fenlow’s jokes, and signs exactly what he is told to sign."

Pell turned away, adjusting his cap. The thick-necked nephew fell in behind him, tossing the bloody rag into a puddle.

"Good night, my lady," Pell called back over his shoulder. "Do mind the cobbles. They are treacherous in the dark."

Isolde Yarben stood alone in the alley, the cold wind biting through her cloak, listening to their footsteps fade away. She looked down at Dunstan’s body. Slowly, mechanically, she reached out and picked up the heavy bag of silver. It was smeared with mud and blood.

She did not cry. She turned and began the long walk back to the castle, knowing that the real war had already begun, and that her family was entirely on the wrong side of it.
