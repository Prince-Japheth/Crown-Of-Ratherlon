ALTON

Four hundred and thirty-eight bronts, Alton Kelward counted, in the grey before dawn at Hallow Ford, and he counted them twice because the first number was too small.

They stood in the dry stream-bed in long russet ranks with their breath steaming and their iron-capped horns lowered, each beast higher at the shoulder than a tall man's head, and the riders sat quiet on them in the dark with their reins in their fists. Nine hundred had gone into the Redcut in the autumn. Four hundred and sixty had died in it, two were still lame at Eastgirt, and four hundred and thirty-eight stood here, and the arithmetic of that had never once come out in his head to a figure he could bear. Behind the bronts stood the levy, six hundred and forty men with long axes and borrowed helms. Behind them stood two hundred and ten Eastgirt townsmen in leather caps who had marched out of their own gates five days ago when his mother had sent word that the Duke had asked for every man who could hold a pike. Ser Boremund's five hundred Crownfold swords, up from Eastgirt since the first melt, stood in the ford itself as the king's reserve, because the king would not put a column of strangers into the narrows. Then there were Harrun's nine, in green and gold, sitting on upturned buckets while their captain explained to a long-nosed boy called Wilm, with great patience, that a bront could not be taught to sing and that he would not be shown otherwise, and a maester with a satchel, and Sergeant Dunn, who had refused to be sent to the rear and was counting the fires again.

"Six hundred and forty to the left of the road," said Dunn. "Say two thousand in the holdfast and the yard. They have mended the gate. They have put iron things on the watch-stone."

"Scorpions."

"Six of them, my lord, I am told by a man who has better eyes than I do. Wheeled ones, dragged up the road behind the pikes." Dunn lowered his tablet. "They are the same as the ones that took your father. The Princess saw the wagons on the first morning and did not know what they were."

"I know what they are."

"Yes, my lord," said Dunn, and said nothing more.

Alton turned his bront and rode back along the line to the little group at the ford where the lamps were.

The king had come down out of the sky four days ago with a noise like a wall falling, in the last light before dusk, and the whole camp had gone silent. Alton had stood among them with his hand on Notch's neck and watched a Nyxen larger than any he had imagined settle in the dry stream-bed with its wings spread across thirty feet of gravel, and a man in a green cloak slide from its back and take off his glove to put out his hand to a duke on a stick. Rodrik Brennan was four and twenty and looked older. He sat now on an upturned crate by the lamp, with his hood back and the grey kid gloves drawn on and his dark hair, with its faint cold sheen, damp from the night, and he looked at the map scratched in the dust in front of him with the grave attention of a man reading a letter he had been dreading.

"We come out of the sun," he was saying. "That is the whole of it. The Ashen will be facing west. They will see the bronts at the ford and set their pikes, and they will not look up, because the sun rises behind the Redcut and a man cannot look into it. Elyra and Gareth and I go round to the east in the dark and come in at height, with the light behind us. The first roar breaks the pikes. They will scatter into knots, because they have learned that. Then the bronts go through the gaps and take the mouth."

"And the iron things on the watch-stone," said Elyra.

"They will shoot at us. They will not be able to see us. I do not think they will hit." The king looked up. His face was tired and very young. "I am not going to take Hart below two hundred feet. They have ropes, and they have the stinking pots, and they have read the same books as everyone else. We stay high. The roars do the work, and we do not swoop, and no one is to land until the yard is clear."

"That is a great deal of dull," said Elyra, but she was grinning.

"It is the only way I know of coming back." He turned his head. "Gareth?"

The boy was sitting cross-legged on the gravel with his back against the grey flank of Thorn. He had a cloak about his shoulders that Alton knew very well, because it was Simon's, a heavy Kelward cloak of brown wool that he had worn for ten years, and the boy had been wearing it since the night before the Redcut because no one had thought to take it back. He nodded without speaking.

"And the bronts," said Simon.

He was standing at the edge of the lamplight on a black ash stick with a silver ferrule, with his weight on his right leg and his left held a little stiffly, and the grey wool of a shirt showed at the collar of his mail. The shirt was thick and warm, and Alton had not seen it before. A woman who disliked to waste her time had sewn it, and Simon had put it on that morning in front of Alton with an air of enormous dignity and said nothing about it at all.

"The bronts go on the roar," said Simon. "Nobody goes before. The charge goes through the mouth in four ranks, and the first rank is mine."

"No," said the king.

There was a silence. In the dark behind the lamp a bront shifted and blew.

"I did not ask you, cousin," said Simon, quietly.

"I know. I am telling you." Rodrik rose from the crate. He stood a hand shorter than Simon and was not so heavy, and he spoke without any visible weight, only a plainness that Alton had begun to think was the real article. "You took a blade through the thigh in the autumn. It has knit, and you are walking on a stick. The Maester says you can ride, and lose the leg if the seam tears. I can order you to the rear, and I am going to."

"You can," said Simon. "I am asking you not to." His smile was crooked and without any heat. "A Kelward charge follows its own duke or it does not follow. I do not say it to insult you, Rodrik. I say it because it is a thing I know. When a Kelward charge goes through the Redcut mouth, it goes behind the man whose name is over the gate, and he leads it bareheaded, under the bront banner, so that every man in the ranks can see his face. My father did it. His father did it. If I do not, they will go, because they are brave and because it is you who asked. But they will go thinking the duke is afraid." He shifted his weight. "I will not have that said of the Eastverge."

"You are wounded."

"I am a duke with a wounded leg. It is a different thing from a man with a wounded leg."

The king stood a long moment looking at him. Alton, standing in the dark with his hand on Notch's horn, found that his own jaw had clenched so hard it ached.

"Simon," he said.

"No."

"The helm, at least. Wear the helm."

"They must see my face."

"They will see your face when you are alive to be seen."

"Alton." His brother turned his head, and in the lamplight his face was calm and strong and a little tired and entirely without fear. "You wrote it on the back of your own letter. I read it. *Do not be noble.* I am not being noble. I am being practical. A Kelward charge needs a Kelward at the head, and I am the only one left who has a name over a gate."

"You are not the only one."

"You will be at my right hand," said Simon, "and you will be a very good wall." He put a hand on Alton's shoulder, a heavy, warm, gloved hand, and let it rest a moment, and took it away. "I am wearing the shirts."

Rodrik sighed. It was a small, defeated sound.

"Then the first rank is yours," he said, and did not look at either of them. He opened his mouth as if there were something more to say. He looked at the lamp, and then at the dark, and then at Hart's great shape in the stream-bed beyond it, and he did not say whatever it was.

"Mount," said the king. "It will be light in five hours."

* * *

The ride in took four hours in the dark, eighteen miles along the dry road at the long, rocking, thundering walk of a bront column that had been told to be quiet, and Alton spent them counting.

He counted the milestones, the cairns that the road-folk had piled beside the watch-stones, each stone a name. He counted the heartbeats between the sounds from the ranks behind him: a cough, a creak, the soft clink of a mail-shirt. He counted the hours of darkness left. When the east went grey, he counted the bronts again, and found that he had forgotten to count himself, and he did.

Simon rode a pace ahead of him on Gaffer, who had gone out of his way for five miles to bite Notch, and the Duke sat the great russet beast with his stick strapped across the saddle and his left leg in a special stirrup that Hale had built him out of a hobble and a boot. He was bareheaded. His dark hair was combed back and his beard was neat. The brown bront banner on its tawny field lifted behind him on the wind, with the watch-eye above, and the sky behind the Redcut had begun to turn the colour of a bruise.

At the last cairn, a mile from the mouth, the column halted. In front of them the red walls of the gully stood against the lightening sky, and above the narrows on the north wall, black on the grey, the watch-stone, with something dark and angular on its roof that had not been there a month ago.

Alton looked at it for a long time.

"Sound nothing," Simon said, very quietly, over his shoulder. "No horns. Not a cough."

The east began to burn.

It started as a thread of gold along the rim of the plain, so thin that Alton thought it was a trick of the eye. Then it widened, and there was a long clean edge of fire along the world, and in front of it, small and black and exact, three shapes rose from the ground and climbed.

They were very high. They were so high that the Ashen on the plain could not have seen them for the sun, and the Ashen on the walls, who had spent the night staring west at the road, did not so much as turn their heads. Alton saw the first of them for what it was only because he knew where to look: a broad, tawny, thick-necked shape with its wings held out like a hawk's, hanging against the light with a dark speck at the base of the neck. It did not seem to be moving. It was merely there, a speck of burning gold, as if the sun had cast something out of itself.

"Now," whispered Simon, and Alton did not know whether he meant to say it.

The roar came across the plain like a hand laid flat on the world.

It did not make a sound at first. He felt it in his teeth. The bront beneath him gave a long shuddering lurch and threw up its head, and all along the ranks the beasts rumbled in terror, and then the noise reached them: a vast, slow, tearing note that began very low and rose until it filled the sky. It struck the Ashen line at the gully mouth from three hundred feet above, and it was a blow that Alton had never seen done. He saw the pike-blocks fold. He saw them go down as a field goes down in a flood: five hundred men in bone plate, in the first instant standing in their ranks with their pikes slanted to the west, and in the second a ragged white wreck strewn across the clay. Shields cartwheeled. Pikes snapped in the air. The sound of it came after, a thin high cry, from a very long way off.

A second roar came down from Lark, and a third, deeper and nearer, from Thorn on the right. Dunn cheered beside Alton. "That is three!" The riders’ words were lost in the noise above.

"Horns," said Simon. "*Horns.*"

The Kelward horns went up like a blade.

He did not remember afterwards the first moment of the charge. He remembered the wall of noise that rose behind him, four hundred bronts in the same breath, the drumming thunder that gave the beast its name and that no man who had heard it could mistake for anything else. He remembered the ground jumping under Notch's feet. He remembered the dry red clay of the road and the gully walls blurring past on both sides and a great smell of dust and iron and beast. He remembered the back of his brother's head. It was a long, dark, bareheaded shape on a russet bront, going away from him through the gap in the wreck of the pikes with the brown banner streaming over it, and the Ashen turning to look at it with their mouths open.

"For Eastgirt," Simon was shouting, in the great hoarse bellow he used on grooms. "*Eastgirt! Eastgirt!*"

The first rank went through the gap like a plow.

The Ashen had begun to run. They broke into knots of ten and twenty, as the king had said they would, and tried to turn, with their bone shields up and their short swords out, but there was no ground to turn on and nothing to turn to. The bronts struck them at the gallop. Alton saw a man go under Notch's horn and a second go down beside it, and he swung his mace with his right arm and found that he had hit something, and did not look. Beyond the mouth the gully opened, and he saw the yard of the Redcut lying in front of him, and the broken gate, and the long red walls with the black iron things above them on the watch-stone, and he felt a great, cold, shining surge of something that was very close to joy.

*We are winning,* he thought. *We are winning.*

It was then that the rim spoke.

He saw it before he understood it. A thin line of pale figures along the lip of the north wall, two hundred feet above the charge, standing up out of the scrub and the red rock with their short bows in their hands. They had been lying flat. They had waited for the bronts to enter the narrows, where the walls rose sheer on both sides and the Nyxens could not stoop without risking the gully, and they had waited until the Kelward ranks were packed so close that a man could not have turned his bront. They loosed together. The air above the charge went dark with a long rising hiss, and then the arrows began to fall.

They fell on the ranks like hail. Bronts screamed. A man beside Alton went off his saddle without a sound. He heard shafts crack against his own helm and rattle on his shoulders, and he put his head down and swung Notch left, toward the wall, to get out from under the rim, and as he did it he looked ahead for his brother.

Simon had not turned. He was thirty yards in front, alone at the head of the first rank, riding straight up the yard toward the gate with his sword in his hand and his head bare. He had looked up once, at the rim, and Alton saw his face tip back in the new light, calm and bright and quite without fear. He opened his mouth. He was going to call to the men behind him. He was going to tell them to keep on.

The arrow took him under the jaw.

It was a short, quick, ordinary shaft, no different from a thousand others, and it went in above the rim of his gorget in the place a man leaves bare when he wishes to be seen. For an instant nothing happened. Simon sat upright on Gaffer with his mouth still open and his hand still raised. Then the sword fell out of it, and he leaned very slowly to the left, as if he had heard something at his side, and slid from the saddle and struck the clay.

"*Simon!*"

He did not know that he had screamed. He had left Notch behind him with the reins hanging, and he was running, in full mail, across a clay yard full of bronts. The first rank was streaming past him on both sides at a gallop with their horns lowered and their banners flying, a long thundering russet wave that had not seen the Duke fall, because a Kelward charge does not look back. A bront went over him. Another. He felt the wind of the hooves and did not feel the fear, and he reached his brother and went down on his knees in the dust and pulled him against his chest.

There was a great deal of blood. It came in short hot pulses between his fingers, and it was bright, a brighter red than anything on the walls. Simon's eyes were open. They were looking at the sky.

"Don't," said Alton. He had both hands pressed under his brother's jaw and it did nothing. "Don't. Simon. *Don't.*"

And over his head the sky split.

It was a sound he would hear for the rest of his life. It was lower than the king's roar, and heavier, and it came from a smaller beast, and it had in it a note that no one had ever put there before: the wail of a creature that has been asked, by someone it loves, to do the thing it fears. Alton looked up. High on his left, against the red wall, the grey Nyxen had stooped from the sun. It was lower than the king had said, a good deal lower, no more than a hundred feet above the rim, with its wings flung back and its great head thrust forward. And on its back, small and white-faced and bent forward over the grey neck, with a brown cloak streaming from his shoulders, was the boy.

*Do it,* said Alton's mind, in a voice not his own. *Do it, Gareth. Do it now.*

The boy bent low over the grey neck and said something Alton could not hear. Thorn opened his mouth, and the sound struck the rim of the north wall like the flat of a smith's hammer, and the line of pale figures did not fall back. They simply stopped, all together, as a bell stops, and the blood came out of their ears and their noses and their eyes, and they went down along the lip of the cliff in a long ragged row and tumbled over, some of them, and dropped, and lay broken on the red rock below.

There was no more arrow-hiss. There was a stillness over the Redcut, and then a long, shuddering, shaking sigh from four hundred bronts at the end of the charge, and a roar from the men that rose out of the yard and went up the walls and was lost against the sun.

The Ashen were running. They ran for the gate and the plain and the long road east, all of them, in the broken knots of ten and twenty, with their pikes dropped and their bone plate flung off. Alton saw none of it. He knelt in the clay with his brother's head in his lap and listened to him breathe.

* * *

It took a long time to die. It took longer than Alton would have believed.

The maester came, out of breath, and knelt, and took one look, and laid his satchel down on the ground and did not open it. Dunn came. Hale came, carrying Gaffer's reins, with his bald head bare and his face grey. The old man stood a little way off and did not say anything. He took off his apron and folded it, with care, and put it down on the clay.

They had carried Simon to the foot of the watch-stone, into the shade of the north wall. Someone had laid a saddle blanket under his head. It was cool there, and quiet, and the noise of the yard seemed a long way off. Alton eased the glove off his brother’s hand and knelt beside him holding it, and over his head the red stone rose, scored with its long ragged litany of names.

"Alton," said Simon.

It was a thread of a voice. It came without much breath. The arrow had been drawn and the wound bound with a strip of Alton's own cloak, but the cloth was black and the blood still came, slowly, a little at a time, and the eyes that looked at him were very clear.

"I am here."

"Did we take it?"

"We took it. They are running. The king is in the yard." He was not certain whether his voice was steady. "The Redcut is ours, Simon. You took it."

"Good." The corner of his mouth moved. "I did say it would be loud."

"You did."

"The leg... it was all right. I rode all that way, and it did not hurt once." He swallowed, and winced. "Funny. I thought it would. I was braced for it."

"Be quiet."

"Alton."

"What?"

"The shirt." Simon's eyes moved down, with great effort, to the grey wool at the collar of his mail, which had been cut open by the maester and was dark and wet and stuck to his skin. "Tell Cora... tell her I wore it. Tell her it was warm. She will want to know."

"I will tell her."

"And Mother. Tell her... the Seam." His hand tightened on Alton's, a little, and loosened. "I should have sent more. Tell her I thought of it."

"Simon. It is not your..."

"I thought of it," said Simon, quite gently. "That is all."

A shadow crossed the stone. Alton did not look up. He heard the soft stamp of boots, and the creak of a cloak, and then a smaller shape knelt on the other side of his brother, a thin, white-faced boy with a short, dark, uneven beard, and a brown cloak that was much too large for him.

Gareth did not speak. He took the cloak from his shoulders, with hands that shook, and he laid it over the Duke of Eastgirt from his chin to his boots, and he tucked it at the shoulder as one tucks a blanket round a child. Simon's eyes turned toward him. They rested on the boy's face for a long moment, and something came into them that Alton had not seen there before, a kind of rueful, gentle wonder.

"You kept it," said Simon.

"I forgot to give it back." The boy's voice cracked. "I am sorry. I forgot."

"Keep... the next one." The mouth moved. "They are not cheap."

He did not say anything after that. He lay with the brown cloak drawn up to his throat and his head on Alton's knee and looked at the sky above the watch-stone, which had gone from red to pale gold, and once, quite clearly, he smiled at something. Then his hand went slack in Alton's, quietly, in the middle of a breath that was never finished, and the Duke of Eastgirt, who had been eight and ten when his father fell, was dead.

Alton did not move. He knelt there with the glove in his hands and a great, still, bright emptiness in his chest, and it was a long time before he heard the sound.

It was a soft sound. It came from a short distance away, and it came again, a low, ragged, shuddering breath that caught and broke and was drawn in again. He turned his head. On the stones at the wall's foot a long tawny-brown shape had come down, and a woman in a leather jerkin stood with her bare head bowed and both hands against the neck of a Nyxen that was trembling from nose to tail.

Elyra's face was perfectly white and quite dry. She stood with her cheek against Lark's shoulder and her braid come loose and her eyes fixed on the dead man's face, and she did not make a sound, and the great beast leaned against her and shook.

"I saw him fall," she said. Her voice came out flat and level and a long way off. "I was a hundred feet up, and I saw it. I could not get down. There was the rim and the ropes. I could not get down."

"I know," said Alton.

"He had no helm."

"I know."

"Gareth did it." She turned her head. "Gareth did it, Alton. He said he could not, and he did it. He did it for..."

"Yes."

"I should have been the one."

"No." Alton found that he had got to his feet. His legs did not seem to belong to him. "No. Nobody is meant to be the one." He took a step and stopped. "It was an arrow. A man loosed an arrow, and it hit. That is all it was. There was nothing at all you could have done."

It was true, and he knew it, and he saw the knowledge go through her like a knife and not help.

A great shadow slid across the yard. It was followed by a rush of wind that threw dust and straw against the wall, and the tawny beast came down in the middle of the Redcut with his wings spread across forty feet of clay, and a man in a green cloak was out of the saddle before the beast had stopped. He crossed the yard at a run. He had the gloves on. He stopped at the foot of the watch-stone and stood very still, and then he sank, slowly, onto one knee in the dust.

For a time nobody said anything.

"I could have ordered him," said the king.

"You could," said Alton. "He asked you not to, and you heard him. He knew you would. That is why he asked." He had not known he would say it. "A Kelward duke leads bareheaded so every man can see his face. It was the only thing he had left of Father." He heard his own voice crack. "You could not have stopped him without taking that from him, Your Grace. I tried, and I did not want to take it from him either."

The king raised his head. His face was young and very pale, and there was no trace in it of the weary, careful, patient man who had sat at the lamp at Hallow Ford. He looked at the brown cloak and the still face above it, and his lips moved. He put out one gloved hand, slowly, as if the cloth might burn, and laid it flat against the wool over Simon's breast.

"He was kind to me," said Rodrik. "When I was nine and had no beast yet and no one would speak to me, he came up from the Eastverge for my father's name-day and he taught me to cheat at dice." He let out a breath that did not quite steady. "I have the dice. I never learned to win."

Alton said nothing. He could not have.

"Cousin." The king's voice was very low. "I am so sorry. I am so very sorry."

It was the last thing in the world that Alton could bear, and he bore it. He stood with his arms at his sides and let the king take his hand, and the grey kid glove closed round his, warm and dry, with a kind of urgent clumsiness, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

* * *

By noon the Redcut was in Kelward hands, and by the evening the last of the Ashen had gone back beyond the frontier.

They had left their dead. They had left six thousand arrows in the walls and a great quantity of rope, and in the yard the six iron scorpions, which had been dragged out of their wagons and set on the watch-stone and never fired, because the crews had been standing in the wrong place when the sun came up. Sergeant Dunn was counting them. He had found a tablet in the guardroom and he had started at the gate and was working his way methodically round the yard, with his hands behind his back and a face like a closed door, and nobody had the heart to tell him that they were not going anywhere.

"My lord Duke," said Dunn, when Alton came out of the guardroom. He stopped. He drew himself up, as far as the bad leg would let him, and the knuckle-less hand came up to his brow in the old long salute.

Alton did not answer. He looked at the hand for some time.

"Do not call me that."

"It is what you are, my lord."

"I know what I am." He heard his voice, cold and level. "I am Alton, and I have been Alton for five and twenty years, and I was going to be Alton for a good deal longer. Give me the tablet."

Dunn gave it to him. He did not look at it. He held it for a moment and then handed it back. "Count them," he said. "All of them. The bronts, the levy, the dead. I want the list by morning. I want every name."

"Yes, my lord."

"And Dunn."

"My lord?"

"The Dry Seam." He kept his eyes on the long red wall. "I want a post on it tonight. Forty men."

"Yes, my lord."

"And I do not want to hear who took the last six away."

"No, my lord," said Dunn, very quietly. His face did not change. "You will not."

At sunset they carried Simon down to the narrows on a bier made of two lances and a saddle blanket, with the brown bront banner folded over him and his father's sword laid across his breast. The levy stood in ranks along the sides of the road, and the Eastgirt townsmen with their leather caps in their hands, and in the dry stream-bed beyond the gully the bronts stood in a long russet silence, four hundred of them, with their horns lowered and their heads down. It was a Kelward burial: no bell, no song, no speaker. A cairn of red stones at the foot of the north wall and a name cut into the watch-stone above it, beneath the name of the man who had been before him.

Alton cut it himself. The stonemason from Eastgirt was a week's ride away and Hale had fetched a mason's chisel and a heavy iron hammer from the forge in the yard, and he knelt on the red rock in the last of the light with the chisel in his left hand and the hammer in his right and the letters of the new name pressed into the stone, one blow at a time. He had never cut a letter before. They came out uneven. The S was cramped. The K slanted a little to the right.

He did not notice, until the last stroke, that someone was holding the lamp.

Elyra stood a pace away with the little tin lantern raised and the light shaking in her hand. She did not look at him. She had been there for an hour, quite silent, with her braid done up again and her face scrubbed, and he had not known it. The tawny shape of Lark lay in the dust at the foot of the cairn with her chin on her paws and one huge dark eye half-open.

"It is crooked," said Alton.

"It is good."

"The K is crooked."

"It is the best K I have ever seen." She lifted the lantern a little higher. In its yellow light the two lines of letters stood side by side in the red stone, the old one deep and square and the new one shallow and a little wrong:

JONOS KELWARD. DUKE.

SIMON KELWARD. DUKE.

She stood looking at them. Alton put the hammer down. He found that his hand was shaking, and he pressed it against his knee until it stopped.

"I have to write to my mother," he said.

"I will write with you."

"No." He shook his head. "She will want it to be me."

"Then I will hold the lamp."

He had thought that he would not be able to do it. But when he went up to the little chamber off the stair, with a plank bed and a washstand and a stub of candle, and sat down at the plank with a scrap of vellum under his hand, the first line came at once, plain and whole, as if it had been written already by someone else.

*Mother. The Redcut is ours.*

He stopped. The candle guttered. Outside a bront was rumbling in the dark, a long low note that rolled out over the walls and the gully and the road and died away.

He took up the pen, and under it, in his own small careful hand, he wrote the second line.

*Simon wore the shirts.*
