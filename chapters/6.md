CORREN

A shadow went over the kennel yard, long and tawny, and not one of the forty horses lifted its head.

Ser Corren did not look up either. He kept his eyes on the king, who had stopped Bracken under the gate arch with his hood back and his face tipped to the pale morning. One gloved thumb traveled along the thin chain at the king's throat, found the pipe, and rested there. Overhead the shadow wheeled once above the kennel roofs, wide and unhurried, and slid away toward the orchard wall.

"He has been round the Fold crags since the fourth hour," said Prince Edwyn, cantering up on his fussy grey with a cup in his hand. He wore plum velvet and a hat with a pheasant feather. "I watched him from my window. Three circles, one pear tree, and a crow that took offense."

"He does it every morning."

"Not every morning. On hunting mornings he goes quite wide." Edwyn tilted the cup toward the sky. "I believe he considers the Foldwood beneath his notice."

"He knows what the horn means." Rodrik's thumb did not leave the pipe. "He keeps clear of the dogs and the horses, and he is never farther off than I can call him. They are the only manners he has, and I have never once had to teach them to him."

"A very well brought up animal."

"A very clever one." The king smiled up at the empty blue. "Go and eat your pears, old friend. I will whistle when I want you."

Corren said nothing. His hip had begun to speak to him around the second turning of the road, and he let it, because the hip at least was honest. His knees added a remark about the damp. The cold found the place where Ashen iron had taken half his left ear ten winters ago and settled in, a thin bright ache like a pin held to a flame. He rode the way he had ridden since the old king died, a length behind and a hand to the left of whoever sat the bay, with his eyes on the hedges and his thumb on the pommel.

The royal kennels lay at the foot of the hill, a long yard of flagstone and straw ringed by low sheds. Forty horses were already in it, stamping, and sixty hounds were already in voice. Boys with leashes ran between the legs of lords. On a mounting block in the middle of the din stood Wick, the Master of Hounds, with a horn at his belt and a face like a dried apple.

"Your Grace!" Wick swept the horn up in salute. "The pack has been at the Aldermere since cockcrow. They have a hart of ten, a great old one, seen twice at the ford. I have not laid eyes on his like in years."

"Then we shall try to deserve him," said Rodrik.

Near the back of the crowd, a patient old mare stood with her head down, and on her sat Prince Rowan in plain dark wool. A clerk waited at his stirrup on a mule with a satchel of papers across his knees.

"Brother!" The king raised a hand.

Rowan inclined his head. "Your Grace." Then he turned the mare, with great composure, toward the kennel cart, and bent to say something to his clerk about a column of figures.

The king's hand stayed up a moment longer than a hand needs to stay up. He let it fall to the pommel. He did not look at Corren, and Corren looked at the hedge.

* * *

They came into the Foldwood by the north ride in a long, loose line, and the oaks closed over them.

Corren counted. It was an old habit and a useless one, and he had never been able to leave it alone. Forty horses. Eleven huntsmen on foot, nine beaters, a cart for the venison, a cart for the ladies' cushions that was following at a distance. Twenty-three men in the king's own party who wore a sword, of whom four were Kingsguard, and of the four, two were younger than his boots.

Lord Fenlow rode at the king's right hand on a black horse that cost more than the hunt. Fenlow wore a coat of Fenlow green and a smile that stopped at the corners of his mouth. A nephew trailed him, a thick-necked lad of six and ten with rust-colored hair, leading the king's spare mount and holding the reins in both fists as though somebody might take them. Behind the nephew rode a third man.

He was pale. He wore a plain dark doublet of good cloth and sat his gelding without grace and without effort, the way a man sits who has spent more hours on horses than he has enjoyed. Corren had seen him in Fenlow's train at the tourney and at the feast and once on the stair of the counting-house. Nobody had ever said his name in Corren's hearing.

At the first ditch, the pale man's eyes went to the ground.

They did not rest there. They traveled. Along the verge, across the ride, over the mud where forty horses had churned it, and back, in a slow, steady, orderly sweep, the way a harvester walks a field. Corren knew the look. He had seen scouts do it, and poachers, and one very good thief in the stews of Crownfold who had picked a bishop's purse from beneath the bishop's elbow while reading the cobbles for a loose stone.

The man lifted his head. He found Corren watching. He bowed in the saddle, a small, correct bow, the kind one clerk gives another across a counting-table.

Corren returned it with a nod. When the pale man looked away, Corren's eyes stayed on him for the length of ten hoofbeats.

Then the horn sounded from the front of the line, a long wavering note, and sixty hounds gave tongue all at once, and the hunt went into the trees at a gallop.

* * *

Hunts made Corren sweat in the way battles did not.

In a battle there was an enemy, and the enemy was over there. At a hunt every man within a hundred yards wore a blade, and carried a boar-sword, and rode a nervous horse in thick undergrowth, and was drunk by noon. A branch whipped his face. A hound ran under Pennywhistle and the prince's grey went sideways with a snort, and Edwyn laughed and sawed at the reins with a degree of incompetence that was, Corren noticed, entirely too well timed. It carried the grey straight across Lord Fenlow's line at the first ditch. Fenlow's black horse shied. For the better part of a mile Fenlow bore a flood of cheerful nonsense about the price of oats, bowing and nodding in his saddle with a smile fixed at the corners of his mouth, and never once drew within a length of the king.

"He's frightened," Corren said quietly, with a jerk of his chin at Fenlow's nephew.

"He is sixteen," said Rodrik. He rode with his face lifted to the flickering light and his shoulders loose. He was happy. Corren could hear it. "I was frightened at sixteen. So were you."

"I was frightened at sixty last winter."

"Of what?"

"Of a ladder."

The king roared with laughter, and a rook went up out of the trees.

They ran the hart north through the bracken for an hour. They ran him through a stream and over a fallen oak, and the line strung out until the huntsmen's horns were a faint bright thread behind them. At the Aldermere ford, the hounds brought him to bay.

It was a wide brown pool where the water spread across shingle beneath a bank of alders, and the old stag stood belly-deep in the middle of it with ten points of antler lowered toward the dogs. His flanks steamed. The water ran off him in sheets of silver. He turned his head, slowly, and looked at the riders pulling up along the bank, and there was nothing in his eye that Corren could have called fear.

"A magnificent beast," said Lord Elmrick, in a hushed voice.

The king swung down. The boy took his reins in both hands and gripped them.

"It is the king's right to give the stroke," Rodrik said. "I'll not have a hart like that pulled down by dogs."

"Your Grace." Fenlow was beside him, off the black horse, a hunting sword in his hand and his smile warm as a hearth. "My nephew has never made a kill. It would be an honor to my house if the king allowed it."

Corren was out of the saddle before the king answered. He did not know why. He stood a pace behind Rodrik's left shoulder and looked at the stag.

"Let the boy do it," said Rodrik.

The nephew went down the bank in his good boots. Water took him to the knees, then the thighs, and the whole hunt held its breath. Corren watched the stag. The great antlers had begun to swing to the left, slowly, a hand's breadth at a time, the way a man's head turns when he is weighing something in his hand.

"Your Grace," he said, very low. "He is going to charge."

"I know."

"Let me..."

"No."

The boy stopped three paces from the beast. He stood with the sword hanging from his fist and his mouth slightly open. The hart lowered his head.

Corren was moving before the antlers dropped. He was a heavy man and old, and it was an unfair race, because the king was already past him, in the water to his thighs, running with his sword out and his gloves still on. Corren saw a spray of silver. He saw Rodrik's arm go up across the boy's chest and the boy go sideways into the shallows. He heard a sound like a plank dropped on a floor, and the hart's head swung, and the king's left forearm was in the middle of it.

Then the sword went in behind the shoulder, low, exactly where he had told the boy to put it. The great beast folded at the knees and settled into the pool like a horse tired of standing. A red cloud spread slowly through the brown water.

"Well," Prince Edwyn said from the bank. "That was lively."

Somebody laughed, and the bank erupted. Corren heard none of it. He was in the water to his thighs, with his hand on the king's shoulder, turning him bodily to the light.

"You are hurt."

"It's a scratch."

"It has gone through your sleeve to the skin, Your Grace." His voice came out low and rough, and his hand on the king's shoulder was not steady. He saw Rodrik notice. "The queen is five months gone with the child this realm has been waiting for. You have no right to take antlers for a Fenlow."

"I took them for a boy." The king's smile was crooked, and white around the edges. "I think that a better reason than most."

Corren had heard the same words in a gully in the Eastverge ten winters ago, from a different king, on the night half his ear came off. He said nothing about it. He took the cloak a groom held out and put it round the king's shoulders, and left his hand there until the shaking in it stopped.

* * *

The ladies' party waited at the hunting lodge, a long low house of grey timber at the edge of the Aldermere meadow. Servants had spread a cloth beneath the oaks and set out cold capon, white bread, and wine cooling in the stream. The young Elmrick girl, Joan, sat on a cushion of swan down in a ring of other girls, and she looked at the king as he came out of the trees with his sleeve cut away and his arm bound in linen. Corren did not like the look. It had arithmetic in it.

Three shallow furrows ran from wrist to elbow on the king's forearm, red and clean. The skin on either side was as smooth as a girl's.

"Rowan's clerk is already totting up the gloves," said Edwyn, flopping onto the grass at the king's feet. He held up a stiff grey scrap, stained with blood and river mud. "Merelands kid. Three marks the pair."

"Rowan will send me a bill for the bill."

"He will have it illuminated."

The king laughed. Corren left them to it.

He walked the line of horses. It was a thing he did at every halt, with his hand on a girth here and a bridle there, and men took it for a Kingsguard's fussiness and let him. He looked at what stood in the stirrups. Baron Callenby's brown boots were creased at the ankle and spattered to the knee. Lord Elmrick's new ones had gone from stiff to ruined in a morning. Hollisyn's silver spurs were caked. Merecroft's boots were black with the stream to the thigh, and he had not noticed. Prince Edwyn's scarlet pair, cut by the best bootmaker in the city, looked as though they had been used to stir a pot.

At the edge of the meadow, in the shadow of an oak, the pale man stood with Fenlow's second horse, running his thumb along a girth.

His boots came to the thigh in oiled black leather with a silver-chased buckle at each knee. Four hours in the Foldwood after a night of rain. Corren looked at them. There was no mud on them. There was not a leaf on them. They gleamed like a new blade.

"Ser Corren."

The man had not looked up. His voice was dry and pleasant and carried exactly as far as it needed to.

"A handsome wound, the king took," he went on. "My lord keeps a salve of comfrey and honey boiled down for such cuts. A quarter hour and I could have a pot of it at the lodge, if the king would find it useful."

"The Maester has what the king needs."

"Of course. I would not presume." The thumb moved along the strap and found the buckle and tried it. "These woods are damp. A scratch festers in the damp."

"I do not recall giving you my name."

"The ear, Ser." Now the pale man looked up, and his eyes were grey and calm and entirely without offense. "We all know the ear."

Corren's hand rose to it before he could stop it. He lowered the hand. The man smiled very slightly, bowed his head, and went on testing the buckle.

On the cloth beneath the oaks, the king was getting to his feet.

"My lord Callenby," Rodrik called. "You have been eyeing the roosts for three weeks as though they were a bailiff. I have a mind to cure it."

The baron, who had been trying to disappear behind a cushion, put down his cup. "Your Grace?"

The king's gloved hand went to his collar and drew out the pipe on its chain. He set the horn to his lips and blew.

It made hardly any sound. A thin high note, quivering, clean as a struck bell, which a man at the far end of the meadow might have missed. Every horse on the grass lifted its head. Every hound stopped. And from far beyond the dark line of the wood, from the direction of the orchards, something enormous answered with a roar of such open, ridiculous joy that three of the ladies shrieked.

"Preserve us," said Baron Callenby, and sat down on the cushion.

The wings took less time than a horse's legs would have. A shadow came out of the east, low and fast, sliding across the treetops, and the meadow turned its face up as it passed: the ladies with their fans stilled, the huntsmen with their horns lowered, Lord Elmrick with a leg of capon halfway to his mouth. The great gold shape swung once above the Aldermere, circled, and came down at the edge of the trees with a landing so soft that the cloth beneath the oaks barely stirred.

He did not run to the king. He folded his wings and sat. His dark eyes went to the man with the pipe, and waited.

Rodrik pointed. "There."

Hart rose, walked three paces to the place the finger had named, and lay down with his chin on his forelimbs and his gaze on the king's face. His tail thumped once against the turf.

"He is cleverer than half my council, my lord," said the king. "He can see that you are afraid of him, and he will lie as still as a stone until you are not. I give you my word on it, and I would give you my hand if you wanted it. Fetch me that basket."

It was Callenby's own hamper, and it was full of small yellow pears. The baron stood. He took it up in both arms, as a man carries a sleeping infant across a river, and he walked across the grass with sweat standing on his sallow face. A long pace from the great head, he stopped. He set the hamper down. He took out one pear, and held it flat on his palm with the fingers drawn well back, and shut his eyes.

Hart took it with his lips. It was done with the delicacy of a lady lifting a sweetmeat from a tray, and the baron's hand was not touched at all.

"Oh," said Baron Callenby, with his eyes still shut. "Oh. It is very soft."

On a stump near the cloth, the Elmrick girl began to laugh. The sound was bright and clear and entirely unplanned, and it ran through the company like wine through water. A huntsman grinned. Lord Elmrick found his capon again. And Baron Callenby opened his eyes, looked into a dark patient eye the size of his own fist, and began to laugh and weep at once.

"Forgive me, Your Grace," he gasped. "I thought it would be much worse."

"It usually is," said the king.

On the far side of the cloth, by the stream, Lord Merecroft laughed with the others. His laugh stopped, quite suddenly, in the middle. His glance went sideways, over the grass, to the shadow of the oak where the pale man stood by Fenlow's second horse, and came back to the water with the speed of a hand snatched from a flame.

Corren, who had not been looking at the Nyxen, saw it.

* * *

They unmade the hart on the grass by the Aldermere in the old way, with great ceremony and little blood. The king carved the first slice and held it out to Fenlow, as the quarry belonged to Fenlow's nephew, and Fenlow took it with a bow and a few graceful words about the generosity of the crown.

Corren stood behind them with his arms folded. He watched Fenlow turn, a quarter turn, toward the edge of the circle where the factor waited. He watched the factor turn a quarter turn away.

When the sky had gone the color of apricots, they rode home. The cart groaned under the hart. The hounds trailed behind in a tired, happy knot. At a word from the king, spoken softly in a tongue Corren did not know, the great gold shape rose from the meadow, wheeled once above the cavalcade, and turned toward the roost.

Corren rode at the back with the king. The road went down between hedges thick with hips and haws. For a mile neither of them spoke, and the king rode with his bound arm against his chest and his head tipped back to the last of the light.

"Corren."

"Your Grace."

"My brother Rowan did not say two words to me today that were not about money."

"No, Your Grace."

"You were watching him."

"I watch everyone, Your Grace."

"Then tell me what you saw."

Corren thought about it as he might have turned a blade, looking for the edge. "At the kennels you raised your hand to him. He turned his mare to the cart. In the wood, he rode behind the hounds' cart for the first mile and behind the ladies' cushions for the second. A man who is offended rides in front, where he can be seen to be offended. The prince rode where nobody needed to look at him."

The king was silent for twenty paces.

"That is not what I asked."

"You asked if he is angry. I think it would be easier on you if he were."

Rodrik gave a short breath that was not quite a laugh. "I'll speak to him. Not tonight. After the muster, when Fenlow has gone back to his timber and the Vine Shore grain is in the barns. I'll say it plainly, in a room with a door."

"Yes, Your Grace."

"I should have done it a year ago."

"Yes, Your Grace," said Corren. "You should."

They rode on. A rook went over, black against the red. Corren drew breath to speak, and the words were in his mouth. He saw the pale man's boots, shining.

"Your Grace..."

"Yes?"

He held it for the space of three of Bracken's steps. The king turned his head and waited, with the weariness of the day all over his face, and his white-knuckled glove closed round the reins.

"The arm," said Corren. "See the Maester before supper."

"I will."

"Before supper, Your Grace. Not after."

"Before." The king smiled at him, and it was a young man's smile, tired and grateful. "You are an old woman, Corren."

"Yes, Your Grace."

Behind them the road dropped toward the river. The smoke of the castle's chimneys stood straight up in the still air. High above the roost, a gold shape circled once, twice, and settled out of sight, and from somewhere in the dusk a long, low, contented cry rolled across the Crownfold, and the hunt rode home beneath it.
