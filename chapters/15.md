EDWYN

Briar did not like the north.

She had shown it twice that winter, and she showed it a third time on the morning after the churching feast. At the line of the Fold crags, where the cliffs of the roost-yard gave way to the high white ridges, the slate-brown Nyxen went on flying exactly as he asked her to, but her ears went flat, her wingbeats shortened, and a low uneasy hum came into her throat that Edwyn felt through his knees. He turned her away from the ridges at once, because he had never needed more than that from her, and she went round with a long breath of what was plainly relief. He put the matter down to the cold, which was the answer to most things in winter.

The Crownfold lay spread beneath them in the pale light like a quilt that had been left out in the frost. The fields were white and the hedgerows black, and the smoke of a thousand hearths stood straight up from the villages in threads so still that he could have counted them. To the south the river Fold had frozen along both banks and run dark down the middle. A line of wagons crawled along the kingsroad toward the south gate, small as beetles, the last of Duke Roland's hundred, and he saw a teamster raise his whip to the sky in greeting without any notion of whose shadow he was greeting.

He had slept for two hours. He had gone to bed with the song still in his head and the roar of four hundred people laughing still in his ears, and he had lain looking at the canopy and thinking about his brother's face. It was not a thing he had meant to think about. But the face had done something at the council table, a small and quick thing, a tightening about the eyes when Edwyn said the word *timber*, and it had stayed with him through the whole of the feast the way a wrong note stays with a lutenist who is in the middle of a good song and cannot stop to find it.

Rowan's face was the easiest in the realm to read. Edwyn had been reading it since he could crawl. It said *I am annoyed* and *I am pleased and wish to be thought otherwise* and *I have found an error in the accounts and shall be unbearable until it is corrected.* It had never once said *I have a tooth.*

Edwyn turned Briar west toward the keep, and she went gladly.

The roost-yard was grey with rime when they came down. A pair of handlers ran out with fruit mash, and Briar shook herself from nose to tail and began to eat as though she had been starved for a week, which was the only form of gratitude she had ever shown to anyone. Edwyn slid from the saddle, stiff in the thighs, and walked the length of the bays with his hands tucked under his arms.

His brother was in the end bay with Hart. The king stood on an upturned bucket in his shirtsleeves, working grease into the great beast's neck with both hands. It was a task that Oren, the old handler, had done for twenty years and had long ago stopped trying to prevent. Hart was lying flat with his eyes half-closed and a pear-core drooping from the corner of his mouth, in an attitude of such complete contentment that Edwyn laughed aloud.

"He is fat," said Edwyn.

"He is well fed," said Rodrik, without turning. "There is a difference, and he would tell you so himself if he could speak. Come and hold this."

Edwyn held the pot. The grease smelled of tallow and crushed herbs. Rodrik worked his thumbs along a seam in the hide where the heat of the Nyxen's body made the fur steam in the cold, and for a time neither of them said anything, and it was almost like being a boy again.

"You were up before the bakers," said Rodrik at last.

"I was up before my own good sense, which is a longer tradition." Edwyn shifted the pot to the other hand. "You were not in your bed either. Elyn will say that I have corrupted you."

"Elyn sent me. She said I was walking the floor and frightening the nurses." The king stepped down from the bucket and wiped his palms on his breeches. His face, in the grey light, was lined and heavy with sleep not had, but there was a settledness to it that Edwyn had not seen since the coronation. "I wanted to ask you about the ribbons."

"Ah."

"Elmrick wore blue to the feast. Callenby wore white. There was a knight at the third table with one on each shoulder, who I believe was jesting, but I could not be sure, and I find that I would very much like to be sure." Rodrik looked at him steadily. "My father would have had the cloaks off them and the necks beneath. I think of that, Edwyn. Not because I mean to do it, but because I watched Elmrick pin on that scrap of blue and I thought, there is a man who is trying to tell me something without saying it."

"What do you think he is trying to say?"

"That he is ready to choose." Rodrik rubbed the back of his neck. "Two boys in a cradle, Edwyn. Born the same night. Nobody knows which came first, and I find I do not wish to know, and every lord in the Crownfold has already begun to take a side in a quarrel that does not exist. I will not hang a man for a ribbon. I would sooner see them laughed out of the things before the laughter stops."

"You want me to be funny at them."

"I want you to do what you do."

"It is a dreadful thing, to be asked to be funny," said Edwyn. "It is like being asked to be tall. One can stand up straighter, but one cannot do very much about it." He turned the grease pot in his hands and considered. A plan came to him, as they sometimes did, from the side and all at once, and he held still to let it settle. "Give me until the hall fills. I shall have an answer."

"I knew you would."

"You did not know it. You hoped, which is a different thing."

"I hoped," said Rodrik, and smiled, and clapped him on the shoulder with a hand that smelled of tallow. Hart opened one dark eye, regarded them both, and shut it again.

They stood a moment in the cold, side by side, watching the roost-yard wake. Somewhere a handler was swearing at a pup. A boy ran past with a bucket.

"I did something last night," Rodrik said abruptly. "I want to tell you, because I cannot tell Elyn, since she would laugh, and I cannot tell Rowan, since it concerns him. You must not repeat it."

"You have my word."

"I went down to the counting-room after the feast. I thought the time had come, and I had meant to do it before the winter ended in any case. I wanted to name him Hand." Rodrik breathed out through his nose. "I did not say so outright. It seemed the wrong way to go about it, to ambush a man with a title at midnight. I said that the realm had run because he had run it, and that I knew it, and I told him that if there was anything he needed before I flew, he had only to ask, and it was his. I said *anything.* I said it twice."

Edwyn had stopped holding the pot. "And?"

"He asked for two hundred wagons of Vine Shore grain and a warrant against the moneychangers."

There was a pause. It was a long one. Edwyn stood quite still, with his mouth open slightly, and watched a vein jump in his brother's temple, and then the grease pot slid from his fingers and rang on the stone and he sat down on the upturned bucket and laughed until he had to put his head between his knees.

"Edwyn."

"I am sorry." He wiped his eyes. "I am very sorry. It is only that it is so entirely, so perfectly Rowan. You offered him the second chair in the kingdom and he asked for *wagons.* He would ask for a better ledger on the day he was invited to sit at the high table of the realm." He drew breath. It hurt. "Did he understand what you were offering?"

"He understood. He is not a stupid man." Rodrik's laugh was rueful. "That is what I cannot account for. I watched his face. I saw him know it. He looked at me for the space of three breaths, and then he asked about the wagons, and I think he did it on purpose."

"Of course he did it on purpose. He would sooner be hanged than ask for it." Edwyn sobered, a little. "A man cannot ask for what he thinks he ought to have been given. It spoils it. Rowan has never asked for anything in his life, brother, he has only waited to be offered, and been hurt each time when he was not."

"Then I shall offer it plainly when I return, with the whole court watching, and he shall have no door to slip through." The king bent and retrieved the grease pot and held it out. "Do not tell him I told you."

"I shall carry it to my grave."

"Edwyn."

"To my grave," said Edwyn, and took the pot, and meant it. He had a great many faults, and he knew the most of them by their first names, but he had never in his life carried a thing to Rowan that was not his to carry.

* * *

By the time he had washed and dressed and combed the steel-shot brown of his hair into some semblance of order, the hall had filled for the morning meal. It was a loud and smoky place at that hour, thick with the smell of fried bread and ale. Eight or ten of the great lords of the Crownfold and the Vine Shore sat along the high table with their trenchers and their retainers, and the lesser gentry crowded the benches below. Rhaenaya's litter had gone south at first light with a Norvalen escort and a nursemaid cursing the cold, and he felt the lack of her at once. She was the only one of his siblings who laughed at all his jokes, and she did it with her whole body, like a hound shaking off water.

Lord Elmrick sat three places from the high seat with a scrap of blue ribbon pinned to his shoulder and the pleased, slightly furtive look of a man who has been indiscreet and liked it. Baron Callenby, at the far end, wore his white scrap as though it itched. They had not seen each other yet. Edwyn watched Elmrick lift his head, see Callenby's shoulder, and go slowly pink, and watched Callenby do the same, and understood that both men had imagined the other would be home abed.

It was quite perfect. He could not have arranged it better if he had tried.

He went to the table of the household knights, where a lean young man of the Merelands sat eating alone with the air of someone who has become unexpectedly notorious. He wore a ribbon on each shoulder, as he had the night before, and when he saw the prince coming he stood so quickly that he knocked his stool over.

"Sit, sit," said Edwyn. "Ser. I am told that you are the man who wore both."

"Your Highness, I meant no offence. It was a jest, only. I have a brother in the Callenby levy and a cousin in Elmrick's household, and when I was asked which I favored I said I could not tell them apart."

"Which is the most truthful thing anyone has said about the matter yet." Edwyn took the ribbons from the knight's shoulders with a gentle, practiced hand, as one might remove the leaves from a salad. The knight's face, which had been prepared for a lecture, went blank. "What is your name?"

"Ser Hugh Merrickson, Your Highness."

"Ser Hugh, you are a man of genius and you have been wasted on the Merelands." Edwyn pinned the blue ribbon to the left shoulder of his own doublet, and the white to the right. He stepped back and looked down at himself with deep satisfaction. "There. Now I am the same as you, and we shall see whether anyone is brave enough to laugh at both of us."

By the time he had walked the length of the hall, the sound had begun behind him. It started low, as a few of the younger knights caught sight of the ribbons and elbowed each other. It spread quickly. By the third table, a man in the Elmrick colors had put his fist to his mouth. By the fifth, Baron Callenby had risen from his seat with his lips pressed white and his eyes darting from the blue on Edwyn's left shoulder to the white on his right.

"Your Highness." Callenby bowed stiffly. He was a heavy, sallow man with the sulking mouth of one who feels he has been underpaid for a long time. "I fear I do not take your meaning."

"My dear Callenby, there is no meaning in it. I have two nephews and one heart." Edwyn raised both hands with a gentle flourish, so that the ribbons caught the torchlight. "It is a very ordinary heart, and not as large as people like to suppose, so I have been obliged to divide it down the middle. Half for the blue and half for the white. It was that or choose, and I am a coward. I have been told so often by my sister."

There was a roar of laughter from the lower benches. Lord Elmrick, who had been rising to protest, sat down heavily instead, and found that he was grinning, and tried to stop, and could not.

"And you, my lord," said Edwyn to Callenby, kindly. "You look as though you have been served a cold supper. I am told that your white is the Varen rose, which is a very good flower. But if you will forgive me, it looks well on me, and on a man of my coloring, and I wonder whether it does the same for you."

It was a gentle thrust. It was meant to be. Callenby's color rose, and then, to Edwyn's pleasure, a snort escaped him that he plainly had not expected, and he put up a hand to his face and shook his head slowly.

"Your Highness is impossible," said the baron.

"I am told so daily. It is the most useful thing about me." Edwyn bowed, and let the laughter build, and by the time he reached the high table there were four or five men up and down the hall plucking at their ribbons and glancing uneasily at one another. He did not look back. He did not need to. A faction can survive hatred, and it can survive persecution, and it has survived famine and plague, but it cannot survive being found funny. He had known that since he was twelve years old.

"That was very pretty," said a voice at his elbow.

He turned. Lady Isolde Yarben stood beside the end of the high table with a cup in one hand, regarding him with her head tilted. She was a widow, a tall woman with brown hair coiled low on her neck and a mouth that gave away nothing it did not wish to. She was born a Merecroft of the Vine Shore, and he privately thought her the most dangerous woman at court, because she laughed at the right moment and never at the wrong one.

"I try to be," he said. "It is better than being ugly and honest."

"Is it? I have found the two sometimes keep company." She sipped her wine. "You will be wanted at Lord Fenlow's tonight, I believe. My brother is going, and he says that you are."

"I am going in my brother's place. The king has declined, on the grounds that he is a father and must be allowed to be tedious."

"Ah." Her eyes held his. "My brother is dreading it."

"Is he? Lord Merecroft has the look of a man who dreads a great many things. I thought it was only the climate."

"He does not dread the supper, Prince Edwyn. He dreads the thing he said yes to, when he was last at Lord Fenlow's table." She said it lightly, in the same voice in which she had remarked on the ribbons, and drank again. "My brother is not a bold man. He is easily led into a corner and cannot find his way out of it, and he is a very poor liar. If one wished to know a thing, one might sit beside him and ask him kindly."

"One might," said Edwyn, "if one had a kind face."

"You have a very kind face," said Lady Isolde Yarben. "It is the best weapon you own." She smiled, and turned, and was gone into the crowd before he could decide whether he had been flirted with or recruited.

He found, to his surprise, that he did not mind either.

* * *

Lord Fenlow kept his town house on Timber Row, a long street of joiners' yards and sawpits that ran down from the castle to the river stairs. It was a tall house, three floors of dark carved timber, every beam and lintel and window-frame worked with leaves and vines and small beasts, so that it stood in the street like a very rich man's advertisement for his own trade. A liveried servant in Fenlow green took Edwyn's cloak at the door. A second took his gloves, and a third offered him a cup of spiced wine, which he accepted and held without drinking, as he had been taught.

The hall was warm and bright. A long table had been laid down the middle with white linen and silver, and eleven men sat at it. Edwyn knew most of them. Fenlow himself, at the head, in the green velvet that never seemed to trouble him in any weather. Callenby, still sulking faintly, on his right. Elmrick, who had recovered his good humor. Two lords of the Vine Shore, Hollisyn and Merecroft, side by side, the first a smooth and rounded man with a ring on every finger and the second a thin, nervous one who stood up when Edwyn entered and sat down again so abruptly that he overturned his own cup. A knight of Fenlow's household whose name he did not catch. And at the foot of the table, a little apart, as though he had been placed there to be not quite at the supper, a man in a plain dark doublet whom nobody introduced.

"Prince Edwyn," said Fenlow, rising. His pale blue eyes were warm and polite. "You do us a very great honor. We had hoped for the king."

"The king is bathing an infant," said Edwyn, "and cannot be spared. He bids me say that he is desolate."

There was laughter, and he sat in the place of honor on Fenlow's left, and the meal began.

It was an excellent supper. He gave it that at once and took no pleasure in it. There were eels in green sauce and a boiled capon in almond milk and a standing pie of venison as large as a cradle, and when the second remove came there were spiced pears, which Briar would have eaten with her whole face. He ate little and listened a great deal. He had a talent for it, a quality of attention that people mistook for friendliness. It had never been entirely false. It was only that what he gave he gave in order to receive, and he did not often stop to tell the two apart.

They talked of nothing for an hour. The price of salt at the river stairs, and the poor harvest at Granth, and the new hunting dogs that Elmrick had bought from a man in the south who swore they could scent a stag at a mile. Hollisyn told a long story about a Vine Shore wedding. Edwyn laughed at the right places. Merecroft did not laugh at all. He sat with his shoulders drawn in and his eyes on the table, turning the stem of his cup in his fingers, and every time Fenlow spoke he looked up like a hare at a hawk's shadow.

The dark-doublet man at the foot of the table said nothing. He ate neatly, as though each mouthful had been weighed first. He was thin and pale, a man of middle years with pale hair going thin, and his hands on the cloth were clean and still and rather beautiful. He did not look at the prince. It was an effort not to, Edwyn thought, and a practiced one.

It was when the pears came that Fenlow stood and called for a toast.

"To the king," he said, and they drank. "To the queen, and to the two sons that Foldhall has been given." They drank again. Edwyn lifted his cup, and the ribbons on his shoulders stirred, and he saw Elmrick glance at them and look away with his mouth twitching. "And to the king's brothers," said Fenlow, with a courteous bow toward Edwyn, "in whose hands the realm rests while the king is in the east. May they be as wise as they are well loved."

"I shall drink to that," said Edwyn, "though I confess I have my doubts about the wise."

They laughed. They drank. Fenlow sat down with an expression of mild benevolence and folded his soft hands on the cloth, and Edwyn knew, quite clearly, that the evening had now begun.

"We are loyal men, Prince Edwyn," said Fenlow. "I would not have you think otherwise. It is because we are loyal that we sometimes speak of things the king has not time to hear."

"My brother is always listening. It is the king's trade, though he does not do it well."

"He does it very well, and that is why the realm loves him. But a king cannot be everywhere, and when he is a thousand miles away at the other end of the realm, there are matters that cannot wait for a raven." Fenlow turned his cup slowly. "The crown's silver goes east. We do not grudge it. It is good silver, and spent on a good cause, and every man here has sons who would gladly bleed for the Eastverge. But a prudent crown would, I think, let the houses that pay for a war have some small voice in how it is paid."

"A great council," said Hollisyn, smoothly, the rings on his fingers glinting. "Such as the old kings used. A council of the great houses and the lesser, to meet at harvest and set the levy and the tithe. It would relieve the king of a great burden."

"It would," said Callenby, with feeling.

"My brother is delighted to be relieved of any burden," said Edwyn. "He complains of them daily. He once told me that he would have been a very happy man if he had been born a blacksmith."

"And you, Prince Edwyn," said Fenlow, with a gentle, courteous smile, "being so much at the king's ear, and so well loved by every house in the realm, might carry the matter to him. Not as a demand. As a suggestion. A thing a good brother brings to a good king."

There it was. He had been waiting for it since the eels. They had come to him because he was the one who laughed, and in his experience men always supposed that a man who laughed could be led. Rowan would have counted the cost aloud. Elyra would have put a knife in the table. Rodrik would have lost his temper and flown them all to the roof of the keep. But Edwyn, who was agreeable and idle and fond of his cup, would carry a tune wherever he was told to carry it.

It was the most useful thing about him. He had known that for a long time, and he had never found a way to dislike it.

"I would be honored to carry it," he said. He let his voice go warm and a little vague, as a man's voice goes after wine. "But I am a poor singer of other men's songs. I should want to know the whole of the verse. Who would sit on this council, my lords? I do not wish to carry a tune that has no singers."

"The great houses, naturally," said Hollisyn.

"Naturally. And at the head of the Vine Shore, Duke Roland, as the first lord of the shore? I should like to name him to my brother. It would please the king very much to know that Rosegate was ready to serve."

Nobody spoke. It was a very small silence and it lasted perhaps the space of two heartbeats, but Edwyn had spent half his life learning to hear the silences in a room, and this one rang. Hollisyn's smooth face went politely blank. Callenby looked at his cup. And Lord Merecroft, at the end of the table, made a small sound in his throat and spilled wine on the cloth for the second time that evening.

"The Duke is a very busy man," said Fenlow, evenly.

"I am sure he is. It is why he must be so tired." Edwyn smiled at him. "He is a guest at the castle, and the queen has been very particular that he be received in every honor. He was not on your list, my lord? I can see that he was not. A man of his rank would have wanted to be seated at your right hand."

"We would not trouble the Duke with a matter so slight," said Fenlow, and the warmth in his eyes did not alter in the least. "He has been a long time on the road. We should not wish to burden him with the details of a supper."

"No," said Edwyn. "Of course not."

Two lords of the Vine Shore, he thought, at a table from which their own duke had been left off. He did not yet know what it meant. But he knew that Rowan's face had done a sum when he said *timber,* and that a tall man with a quiet voice had told him two hours ago that the Vine Shore was late with its grain, and that something was being built in this house which a duke had not been asked to see. He filed it away carefully, with the care of a man who puts a coin in a pocket he does not intend to check until later.

"Shall we throw?" said Fenlow. "I have had a table set up. It is a poor house, but we keep hazard."

* * *

They played in the smaller room, off the hall, at a table of black oak with a green cloth stretched over it and a horn cup for the dice. There was a fire. There was a servant to pour. The two lords of the Vine Shore sat down at once, and Elmrick, and Callenby, who lost steadily and with a kind of bitter pleasure, as though it confirmed an old suspicion about his fortunes. Merecroft did not play. He stood at the window with his back to the room and his hands clasped behind him, and Edwyn, who had been keeping an eye on him for an hour, let him alone.

It was the pale man in the dark doublet who threw against him.

"My factor, Garrow Pell," said Fenlow, a little carelessly, from his place by the fire. "He keeps my accounts, and does me the very great favor of being honest. You will not mind if he takes your money, Your Highness. He has been taking mine for fifteen years."

"I should be delighted to lose it to an honest man," said Edwyn. "It would be a change."

Pell bowed slightly. He was soft-spoken, and when he spoke it was in the flat, clipped vowels of the Crownfold, with a faint dryness at the edges, like a good brown bread. He threw the dice, called a main of six, and lost it. He did not seem to mind. He paid with a neat, quick motion from a small leather purse, counting the coins onto the cloth without looking at them. They were silver, new-struck, each bearing the crown stamp.

"You carry a great deal of silver, Master Pell," said Edwyn, picking up the horn cup.

"I find it convenient, Your Highness."

"I am told you will not touch paper at all."

"I find that paper is a promise, and silver is a fact." The pale eyes met his, for the first time. They were grey, and very calm. "I am a plain man, and I like to know what a thing weighs."

"A creed to which I cannot subscribe, but which I admire." Edwyn threw. He lost, and paid, and watched. It was Pell's boots that he was watching, in truth. They were visible beneath the edge of the table, because the man sat with his legs a little extended, and they were good boots. They came to the thigh in oiled black leather, with a silver-chased buckle at each knee, and they had been cared for with a loving hand. They were far too good for a factor who sat at the foot of a table and was not introduced.

*A man on the north road. Crownfold accent. High boots.*

He had heard Elyra's letter read at the council in summer, and again afterward alone, for the pleasure of her handwriting complaining about the heat. He had thought nothing of the line at the time. Half the men of the Crownfold wore high boots, and half of those spoke as Pell did, and the only reason the line returned to him now was that he had spent the past three hours in a room full of men who were being very careful not to say a thing. A mind that has been tuned to listen for the wrong note will find it in a well-made lute.

"You travel, Master Pell?" he asked pleasantly. "A man in your office must."

"To the western timber lands, Your Highness. To the river stairs, and once or twice to the Merelands for the oak." Pell rolled the dice in his palm. "And I went to Eastgirt, last spring, before the troubles."

It was said without the slightest hesitation. Edwyn kept his hands where they were.

"Eastgirt. That is a long ride for a man who works in timber. What does one buy in the Eastverge?"

"Nothing, Your Highness. That was the difficulty." A dry glimmer touched his mouth. "My lord was persuaded there was gully-willow to be had cheap, for fence-posts, and I was sent to see about it. There is none. There is scrub and thorn and a great deal of red dirt. I rode two weeks to learn it."

"Four hundred marks of my money," said Fenlow, from the fire, and laughed. It was a good laugh, rueful and rich. "Four hundred marks to learn that the Eastverge has no trees. I shall never be allowed to forget it. Garrow tells the story against me at every feast. I am told the moneychangers on the river stairs have the figure painted over the door."

The others laughed. Hollisyn clapped his hands once. Even Callenby smiled. Edwyn laughed with them, wholeheartedly, and felt it go through him like cold water.

He knew the sum. Fenlow had said it aloud before anyone had asked, and he had said it in a rich, easy voice, in front of eleven witnesses, with the factor sitting by to confirm it. It was a very natural joke for a man to make against himself. It was also, Edwyn thought, precisely what he would have done if he had known that a figure was written down somewhere and meant to be explained before anyone asked. A man who tells a joke against himself before it is demanded has either nothing to hide or has practiced.

Edwyn knew exactly how much practice it took. He had spent his whole life at it.

"I should like to see this house on the river stairs with the figure over the door," he said. "I have a hundred jokes against my own purse and none of them are painted over a door."

"You shall have a seat at the rail," said Fenlow warmly.

He threw again. He lost again. He allowed it to be seen that he was merry and loose and rather drunk, and when the dice came round to Pell, he laughed at the man's neat gestures and said that he was the most solemn gambler in the realm, and Pell bowed and said that gaming was too important to be done frivolously. Edwyn thought he might have liked him in other circumstances. It was a thing about the pale factor that did not fit. He had the hands of a clerk and the eyes of a man who has been watching a door for a very long time.

Merecroft left early. He made some stammering excuse about his horse, and Edwyn watched him go from the corner of his eye, and when the lord had gone down the passage to the street door, Edwyn rose, yawning, and begged to be forgiven for the call of nature, and followed.

He found Merecroft in the yard, standing in the cold with his arms wrapped about himself and his breath smoking. His groom had not yet brought the horse. The thin lord turned at the sound of the step and flinched.

"Prince Edwyn."

"My lord. I am a poor companion for a long ride, but I find I have a great need of air." He came and stood at his side, and looked up at the stars, which were very bright and hard in the frozen night. "It is a fine night. A good night for being out of doors, if one has something on one's mind."

"I have nothing on my mind," said Merecroft.

"No? Then I envy you. I have a great many things, and most of them are other people's." Edwyn kept his voice easy, as one might talk to a nervous horse. "Your sister tells me that you dread suppers. She says it is a failing in the family."

Merecroft did not answer for a time. The groom had appeared at the gate with a grey mare and then, seeing the prince, retreated.

"Isolde talks too much," he said at last, in a low voice.

"She talks exactly enough. I find it a great relief." Edwyn turned his head. "My lord, I am not going to ask you what you said yes to. I do not think you would tell me, and if you did, I think you would regret it by the morning. But I will tell you this. My brother the king has a very large heart and a very short temper, and when he is deceived he does not forgive it quickly. And my sister the queen has neither, and she does not forget. If a man had said yes to something he did not like, I should advise him to say no in good time, to a person who could be trusted to protect him for it. Before he finds that there is nobody left to say it to."

The lord of Merecroft looked at him. His thin face was white in the starlight, and his eyes were large and wet, and for a moment Edwyn thought he would speak. His lips parted. A line of breath rose and vanished.

"I thank you for the advice, Your Highness," said Merecroft. "I shall think on it."

He took the mare's reins from the groom and mounted, clumsily, and rode out of the yard. Edwyn watched him go.

It was very little. But a hare that has been frightened once will sometimes come again.

* * *

The castle was dark and cold when he came back to it, past the second hour of the night, with the wine sourly in his stomach and his head clear as glass. He went up by the south stair to the nursery floor, because it was his habit these days, and because Elyn kept strange hours and he had discovered that a man who sang softly outside a door could often be admitted.

She was sitting up in the carved chair with both boys at her breast, one on each side, her dark hair loose and her face grey with weariness. Nan the wet-nurse slept in her cot in the corner, snoring faintly. The room smelled of milk and warm wool and tallow.

"You are very late," said the queen.

"I have been dining with eleven men and an eel. It is a thing a person recovers from slowly." He sat on the edge of the stool across from her. The two small heads were dark against her gown, and in the candlelight the tufts of hair on each caught the light like polished iron. He looked at them for a moment longer than he meant to. They were so alike. He had not been able to find a difference in forty days, and he had looked.

"Well?" said Elyn.

He told her. He told it plainly, without the little decorations he would have used for a hall, in the order in which it had occurred: the toast, the talk of the great council, the silence over Roland's name, Merecroft in the yard. He did not tell her about the boots or the four hundred marks, because he did not yet know what they meant, and he had learned long ago not to carry half a tune to a person who would take it for the whole. Elyn heard him through without speaking, and when he was done she sat still with a child in each arm and her face turned toward the fire.

"A council of the great houses," she said at last, "meeting at harvest to set the levy."

"With the king's brothers as their voice."

"With Edwyn as their voice, because Edwyn will carry anything." She did not say it unkindly. "They will try Rowan next, I think. They will say it to him as a matter of dues and tithes, because it is the language he speaks, and he will say it is a matter for the king. They will try me, and I will say it is a matter for the king. And the king will be in the east. And then they will say it is a pity that nobody can decide anything."

"You think it is a plan, then. Not a grievance."

"I think a man who wants a council wants the chair at the head of it." She shifted the boy on her left arm, and the child grunted in his sleep. "I will tell Rodrik tomorrow. Not tonight. He has been happy for forty days, and I would give him one more night of it."

"He will want to stay and hang them."

"He will want to stay. He will not hang anyone. That is why I must be the one to say it." She lifted her eyes to him. They were grey and tired, and quite steady. "You have done well, Edwyn. I did not think you would hear so much."

"It is only that I am very easily underestimated," he said. "I have made a career of it."

She smiled, a little. He bent and kissed her forehead, and then, because he could not help it, touched one finger to the fist of the child on her right, who gripped it in his sleep with surprising strength. It was the one with the blue ribbon. It was only a ribbon, and an accident of a sewing basket. But it was the child's hand, and it was warm.

"Go to bed," said Elyn.

He went out into the passage. It was very quiet. A single torch burned at the turn of the stair, and by its light he saw that there was another man there, standing at the window at the end of the corridor with his back to the stair and his hands clasped behind him. The long dark shape was familiar. The man had stood just so as a boy, at the same window, on the nights when he could not sleep.

"You are up late," said Edwyn.

"I am always up late." Rowan did not turn. "I was returning from the counting-room. I thought I would look in upon the nursery, and I found the door barred, and the queen's voice within. I did not wish to disturb her."

"You might have come in. She would have been glad."

"She is tired." He did turn then, and the torchlight caught his face. It was thin and pale and perfectly composed, and he looked at Edwyn as he had looked at him in council, with an attention so still that it was almost courteous. "You have been at Fenlow's."

"I have. I am very full of eel."

"Did you learn anything?"

It was the most natural of questions. Edwyn had known he would be asked it, if not tonight then in the morning, and he had prepared an answer on the stair, light and a little silly, the sort of thing a person says to turn aside a bore. He did not use it. He found he did not wish to.

"Rowan," he said. "What do you know of a man named Garrow Pell?"

He watched his brother's face.

It was the strangest thing. He had been watching that face for as long as he could remember, and he knew it as he knew his own hands. He knew its small weathers. He knew the way the corner of the mouth drew in, and the eyelids lowered a fraction when a thing was wrong, and the stillness that came over it when Rowan was angry and did not wish to say so. He waited for it all, quite calmly, as a man waits for a bell he has heard every hour of his life to toll.

Nothing came.

There was no change. The face remained as it was, smooth and pale and attentive, and the eyes met his without flicker or shadow. It was the face of a man who has been asked the time.

"He is Lord Fenlow's factor," said Rowan. "He buys timber."

"Yes," said Edwyn. "I thought that he might."

"Is there something about him that concerns you?"

"No. He threw very well." He kept his voice light. "I only wondered."

"I see." Rowan inclined his head. "Good night, brother."

"Good night."

He walked down the corridor and did not look back, and he did not stop until he had turned the corner of the stair, where he stood in the dark with his back against the cold stone and his heart going very fast. It was a small thing. It was nothing at all. A man had been asked a question and had answered it truly, and in all the years Edwyn could remember he had never once seen his brother's face fail to give him away.

That was what frightened him. It had always been the one thing in the world he never had to work at. Tonight Rowan had worked at it, and he had been very good.

He understood, standing there in the cold, that the easy face he had read all his life had never been a failing in his brother. It had been a door, left open for a boy who had once been kind to him, and tonight, for the first time, it had been shut.

Edwyn stood a long while on the stair. Then he went up to his bed, and slept badly, and in the morning he put a blue ribbon and a white one on his doublet and went down to the hall to be funny.
