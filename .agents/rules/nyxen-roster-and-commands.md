# Nyxen sound, commands and roster

## 1. What needs a command (corrects `nyxen-obedience-and-pipes.md`)

- Nyxens make any sound they like, any time, without a command: hum, rumble, whine, shriek, chirrup, sigh, a plain loud roar. Riders go about their business while their beasts complain or sing in the background, as dragons do in the Targaryen house. Do not write "no sound without command."
- For bonded Nyxens, only two things never happen without a spoken command, repeated for each separate discharge: the **storm roar** (the concussive wind-blast) and the **kill roar** (internal rupture). The dictionary's "normal roar" is the free one and does no harm beyond fright.
- Gareth's "lesser roar" in chapter 11 is the storm roar. Use "storm roar" everywhere.
- Obedience to riders is unchanged. A Nyxen flinches, then obeys.
- Only a Brennan-bloodline voice makes a Nyxen answer a roar-word. Servants and handlers may know the words; the beast ignores them.

**Roar friendly fire:** Storm and kill roars affect allies as well as enemies. Once troops mix in the target area, neither weapon roar is usable there. Riders switch to controlled physical attacks, reposition, or wait for separation. Ordinary vocal calls are unaffected. For steep dives, low fast passes, catches, releases, recovery space, and the limits of enemy-only targets, see “Aerial manoeuvres and close battle” in `nyxen-origins-and-hide-1.md`.

## 2. The command words (Aurouvian)

Aurouvian is the Brennans’ comfort language and their natural default in unguarded conversation with family and their Nyxens. They slip into it for affection, reassurance, irritation, jokes, and ordinary talk without making a deliberate language choice. The common tongue remains usual in public dealings, ceremony, and statecraft. Short, distinct Aurouvian commands are also practical over wind and battlefield noise; that utility supplements the habitual family language rather than being its sole cause. Their command vocabulary favors brevity and clarity over wind and screaming. Each word has its own shape and its own ending, with a hard or guttural consonant and a rolling R wherever the language allows one. No two should be mistaken for each other across a battlefield. Servants and handlers have picked up the movement words over two hundred years. They never use the roar-words, and a Nyxen would not answer them from anyone outside the bloodline.

| Word | Say it | Built from | Use |
|------|--------|-----------|-----|
| **Xyrgōs** | ZEER-gose | Xyr (severing) + -gōs (break) | Kill roar |
| **Qaenkhōr** | KWAYN-khor | Qaen (storm) + -khōr (hurl, said from the back of the throat) | Storm roar |
| **Baelzōs** | BAYL-zose | Bael (steel) + -zōs (tear) | Strike with claw or bite |
| **Fayrūth** | FAY-rooth | Fay (gale) + -rūth (go swiftly) | Fly hard, break away |
| **Aekhrōn** | AY-khrone | Ae (sky) + -khrōn (climb) | Take off, climb |
| **Daeznār** | DAYZ-nar | Dae (deep) + -znār (sink) | Descend, land |
| **Caevārn** | KAY-varn | Cae (stone) + -vārn (stand fast) | Hold, stay |
| **Behella** | beh-EL-lah | Beh (heart) + -ella (beloved) | Praise, calming (not an order) |

- Stress falls on the first syllable of the root and on the long vowel of the ending. R's are rolled. Macrons mark long vowels and are used in prose.
- **Xyrgōs** is the heavy one, said low from the chest. A rider who says it has decided to kill, and the scene must always show that as a decision. **Qaenkhōr** is the storm roar, with the guttural catch at the end.
- Each ending is its own small word. Add them to `aurouvian-language-and-names.md` section 2 as the "command endings": **-gōs** (break), **-khōr** (hurl), **-zōs** (tear), **-rūth** (go swiftly), **-khrōn** (climb), **-znār** (sink), **-vārn** (stand fast). A new command is a root plus whichever ending says what the rider wants. Never reuse one ending for more than one meaning.
- Anomaly riders (close royal nieces/nephews, regardless of house name): Alaric learned them from his mother Rhaenaya and Gareth. Aderyn learns them from Elyra. Tristan, Edwyn’s bastard son, learns the short commands by repetition at court but knows little to no conversational Aurouvian. **Cassia** does not know Aurouvian at all. Her wild-tamed Nyxen answers common-tongue words she chose herself ("Break," "Down"), and this is a point of difference, not a flaw.
- The Greywater Brennans speak an older Aurouvian but have no riders. Brenna is a non-rider; knowledge of the language or affection for the beasts does not confer a bond.

Chapter conformity (exact edits in the review): chapters 3 and 11 use Qaenkhōr, Xyrgōs and Aekhrōn at the commanded moments.

## 3. Nyxen look (design rules)

**Author shorthand only:** The coat classifications in this roster guide visual design; chapter prose must describe the actual colours and markings rather than use modern cat-coat terminology. See the coat-vocabulary rule in standing-directives-2.md.

- **Saddle:** fastened around the base of the neck, the rider seated directly above the shoulders with the wings behind his back. Never on the back or the spine.
- **Coats are like cats' coats** (`nyxen-origins-and-hide-1.md`): tabby (mackerel stripes, blotched swirls, ticked agouti), spotted or rosetted, mottled tortoiseshell, calico patches, bicolour with a white chest and socks, colourpoint with a darker mask, ears and tail, smoke and shaded coats, brindle. Solid tawny-gold and wheat-tan are common, like ginger in cats, and a ginger Nyxen is nearly always a faint tabby. A true solid in any other colour is rare. Leofric's solid ash-charcoal was an uncommon beauty.
- No horns, crowns, spines or fantastic colours. Neck short and thick, head broad and blunt, ears upright. Body structure follows the six user-supplied wyvern references, with fur replacing scales, a somewhat shorter neck, and a batlike head; never a feline body with separate wings. See the visual anatomy clarification in `nyxen-origins-and-hide-1.md` and `assets/references/nyxen-body-structure/`.
- A rider braids one tuft of his Nyxen's coat into his own hair (see `brennan-hair-greywater-and-travel.md`).

## 6. Oak (replaces the earlier cap)

**Naming (locked):** Aldren VII, Oak’s first rider, named him **Oak** and used that name in both the common tongue and Aurouvian. He never called him an Aurouvian equivalent. Elsehnor (Aldren VIII), his son and Oak’s second rider, retained that usage, as do later riders. Oak has no alternate name or pending translation. An Aurouvian command may therefore be **“Aekhrōn, Oak.”** (“Take off, Oak.”)

**Grief, knowledge and the later realization (locked):** Elsehnor (Aldren VIII) was the first recorded person to ride a Nyxen after its first rider’s death. His bond with Oak was unprecedented, not normal succession between riders. After losing both Aldren VII and Aldren VIII, Oak withdraws into solitude; the court assumes that accumulated grief will kill him. Before sightings reach them, characters assume Oak has gone to die. Credible incidental reports may then establish that he is still alive for characters and readers; survival itself need not remain uncertain. His later military significance remains unanticipated.

Oak’s survival must be available to recall without advertising his future importance. Use only rare, incidental smallfolk reports amid unrelated business, with no regular reminder schedule, investigation, ominous emphasis, strategic appraisal, or suggestion that somebody should claim him. A listener responds with genuine, brief surprise that Oak is alive and sympathy that he still suffers, and may wish him a peaceful death before returning to ordinary business. Death is the only relief from rider-loss grief; these well-wishes never mean recovery, enjoyment of solitude or gratitude that he survived. Do not dismiss sightings as foolishness, compare them to imaginary monsters, or insist that characters forget or disbelieve them. What remains overlooked is Oak’s possible military use, not the fact of his survival. Do not end scenes by reminding readers that Oak is alive. Aeian, the martial prince, must have the realization first; readers recognize Oak’s military significance with him, not anticipate the solution throughout the Twin War buildup. Readers supporting Aeian should feel sudden hope and excitement; readers opposing him should feel the alarm of remembering a forgotten threat. This moment recalls an overlooked fact rather than confirming a weapon acquisition readers have been expecting. Existing background reports: Ch 3, Ch 14, Ch 18. Do not increase their frequency merely to keep Oak in readers’ minds.

**Command word order (locked):** When a Nyxen command includes the beast’s name, put the command first and the name second: “Aekhrōn, Oak.”


**Address and delivery (locked):** Normally give a Nyxen order as **command, beast’s name**: “Qaenkhōr, Thorn!” or “Aekhrōn, Oak.” Keep the established name unchanged, including common-tongue names. In emphatic delivery, a hard throat catch separates the command from the addressed name; it is a pronunciation feature, not a new particle, syllable or word. Use ordinary comma punctuation, not phonetic clutter. Quiet commands retain their quiet delivery; do not force shouting or an exaggerated catch into every exchange. A bare command is appropriate when immediate danger, a rapid escape or a running fight leaves no time for the name. Do not make bare orders the routine merely because a scene involves battle. The name is direct address, not a magical prerequisite: the same recognition and obedience rules apply either way. Show the delivery where useful without describing the throat catch after every command.
